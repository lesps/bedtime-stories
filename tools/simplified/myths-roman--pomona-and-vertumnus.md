Pomona was a wood nymph, but she did not care for wild forests or rushing rivers. What she loved was gardens, and most of all, her orchard of fruit trees.

She did not carry a spear for hunting, like other nymphs. She carried a pruning knife. With it she trimmed the branches that grew too long and wild, and she grafted new twigs onto old trees, so that one tree could grow two kinds of fruit. When the weather was dry, she dug little streams so that the thirsty roots could drink. Nobody in the world was better at growing fruit.

Pomona was so busy with her trees that she had no time for anything else. She kept her orchard locked, and she would not let anyone in.

Many wanted to marry her. The fauns and the satyrs, the playful spirits of the woods, would have given anything to win her. So would old Silvanus, the god of the forest, and Pan, with his crown of pine needles. But she sent them all away.

The one who loved her most of all was a young god named Vertumnus. But Pomona would not let him in either.

Vertumnus had one special gift: he could change his shape and look like anyone at all. So he came to Pomona's orchard again and again, in one disguise after another.

Sometimes he came as a harvester, with a basket of wheat, looking exactly like a real farmer. Sometimes he came with hay tied around his waist, as if he had just been turning the hay in the fields. Sometimes he carried a stick for driving oxen, as if he had just finished plowing. Sometimes he came with a pruning hook, like a man who looks after grapevines, or with a ladder on his shoulder, as if he were going to pick apples. Once he even came as a soldier, and once as a fisherman with a fishing rod. Each time, Pomona let him in, and each time he looked at her and loved her more. But she never knew it was him.

At last, one day, he came disguised as an old woman, with grey hair and a cap, leaning on a walking stick.

The old woman walked into the orchard and admired the fruit. "My dear, you have done wonderful work," she said. She sat down on a grassy bank and looked up at the branches, heavy with apples.

Across from her stood an elm tree, and around it twined a grapevine, loaded with fat purple grapes.

"Look at that elm and that vine," said the old woman. "If the elm stood all alone, it would only have leaves, which are no use to anybody. And if the vine had no tree to climb, it would just lie flat on the ground. But together they are beautiful, and they give us grapes. Why don't you learn from them, my dear? Why not share your life with someone?"

Pomona said nothing.

"So many want to marry you," the old woman went on. "But take an old woman's advice. Send all the others away, and choose Vertumnus. I know him as well as he knows himself. He isn't one of those gods who wander all over the world. He lives right here in these mountains. He doesn't fall in love with every pretty face he sees. He loves only you. He is young and handsome, and he can take any shape you like. And best of all, he loves the same things you do. He loves gardens, and he admires your apples. But now he doesn't care about any of that. He only cares about you. Think about it, my dear. The gods don't like hard hearts."

Then Vertumnus could not bear to pretend any longer. He threw off his disguise, and there he stood before her in his own shape, a handsome young man. To Pomona, it was as if the sun had burst out from behind a cloud.

And this time, he did not need to say a word. Pomona looked at him, and she loved him too.

So Pomona and Vertumnus were together at last, in the orchard they both loved. And ever since, Pomona has been remembered as the goddess of apple orchards and of every kind of fruit.
