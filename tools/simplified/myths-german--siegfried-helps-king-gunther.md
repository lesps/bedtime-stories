After many adventures, Siegfried came to Burgundy, a kingdom on the river Rhine. Its king was Gunther, and he lived there with his brothers, his mother, Queen Ute, and his beautiful sister, Kriemhild. They made Siegfried so welcome that he stayed for a whole year. Gunther's grim old uncle, Hagen, was there too, with his dark, frowning face.

At the great midwinter feast, an old man with a snow-white beard came into the hall and sang a song. But it was a very sad song, about dying flowers and falling leaves, and growing old, and everyone cried.

"Good friend," said Siegfried, "that song does not suit a feast. Sing us something happy."

"I don't know any happy songs," said the old man. "I am a messenger from a land far away, and I have a message for the kings of the Rhine."

"Let him speak," said King Gunther.

"Far over the sea," said the messenger, "lies Isenland. In a green marble castle there lives the most beautiful woman in the world, Queen Brunhild. Once she was one of Odin's Valkyries, but Odin was angry with her and put her to sleep. At last a hero rode through the fire and woke her. But then he went away, and nobody knows where he lives. So Brunhild has sent messengers to every land to challenge every king to three contests: throwing the spear, throwing a heavy stone, and jumping. Whoever can beat her will be king of Isenland and marry her. But whoever tries and fails will lose his life. Many have tried, and all have failed. King Gunther, will you accept the challenge?"

And Gunther, as if he were in a dream, said, "Yes. In the spring I will go to Isenland."

Everyone was amazed. Siegfried whispered, "Think again, my friend. You don't know how strong she is. Even if you were four times as strong, you could not beat her."

But that only made Gunther more determined. Then Hagen said slyly, "Siegfried seems to know a lot about Isenland and its queen. Perhaps he would like to go with you."

"Yes, my best of friends," said Gunther. "Come with me and help me. If we succeed, ask me for any reward you like."

"I am not afraid for myself," said Siegfried. "If your heart is set on it, I will go with you. But don't take an army. Just build one strong little ship, and we four will go: you and I, and Hagen and his brother Dankwart."

All winter they got ready. Kriemhild and thirty of her maidens sewed beautiful clothes for the four heroes, covered with gold thread and jewels. Carpenters built the ship. And Siegfried did not forget his sword Balmung, or his magic cloak, the Tarnkappe, which made him invisible and gave him the strength of twelve men.

In the spring the ice melted, and they sailed away down the Rhine and out to sea, with Siegfried steering. At last they saw the tall towers of Brunhild's castle.

"Now listen," said Siegfried. "When we get there, everyone must say that Gunther is the king and I am only his servant. Everything depends on that." And the others promised.

From the castle windows, Brunhild's maidens watched them ride up. "The first is a king," said one. "But the second looks even nobler. Why, it must be Siegfried! He is riding Greyfell, the horse with the sunbeam mane!"

The four heroes were led into the castle, and their swords and armor were taken away, which made Hagen grumble. Brunhild sat on an ivory throne, with five hundred warriors standing around her with drawn swords.

"Welcome back, Siegfried," she said with a smile. "Who are these with you?"

"Noble queen," said Siegfried, "this is my master, the king of Burgundy. He has come to accept your challenge."

"I warn him to think carefully," said Brunhild. "Many have tried, and all have failed."

"We have thought," said Gunther. "We accept."

So Brunhild put on armor that no sword could cut. Her spear was so heavy that it took three men to carry it, and her shield was as broad as the sun. The heroes stared at her in fear. But while no one was looking, Siegfried slipped out, ran down to the ship, put on his magic cloak, and ran back, invisible.

"Don't be afraid," he whispered in Gunther's ear. Gunther could not see anyone, but he knew it was Siegfried, and he felt much braver.

"I will take your shield," whispered Siegfried. "You make the movements, and I will do the work."

First Brunhild threw her mighty spear. It hit Gunther's shield so hard that Gunther and the invisible Siegfried were both knocked to the ground. But they jumped up. Gunther seemed to pick up the heavy spear, but really it was Siegfried. He turned it around, blunt end first, and threw it back. It hit Brunhild's shield with a crash that echoed off the cliffs, and Brunhild fell to the ground.

She jumped up. "That was a fine throw, King Gunther," she said. "But there are two more contests."

Twelve men carried in a huge rough stone. Brunhild lifted it high over her head and threw it across the castle yard. Then she leaped after it, and landed right beside it. Everyone cheered. And Hagen bit his lip.

But Gunther and the invisible Siegfried picked up the stone and threw it nearly twice as far. And Siegfried picked Gunther up in his arms and jumped after it, all the way to the castle wall.

Brunhild thought Gunther had done it all by himself. "I am beaten," she said. And she told her people that Gunther was now their king.

But it was not right for the best of heroes to play such a trick. And one day, a long time afterwards, it would bring them all great trouble.

Siegfried ran back to the ship, took off his magic cloak, and came strolling into the castle yard. "When do the games begin?" he asked.

Everyone laughed. "You must have fallen asleep on your ship, Siegfried," said Brunhild. "The games are over, and your master, King Gunther, has won."

Siegfried looked delighted, and he really was. And they all went into the great hall for a feast.
