title: Tages, the Child from the Furrow
adaptedFrom: latin-sources--tages

Before Rome was great, the land to the north of it belonged to a people called the Etruscans. They built cities on the hilltops, and they were farmers and sailors and wonderful artists. And the Etruscans told a strange story about a field near the city of Tarquinii.

One day a farmer was plowing his field there. His oxen pulled the plow along, and the sharp blade cut a long, straight furrow through the earth. But on that day, the plow dug in deeper than it ever had before.

Then the farmer saw something very odd. In the middle of the field, a lump of earth began to move all by itself. Nobody was touching it. It wriggled and rose up. And then, little by little, it began to change. It stopped looking like earth and began to look like a person. And there, standing in the furrow, was a child.

The child looked like a little boy. But when he opened his mouth to speak, he spoke like a wise old man.

The farmer was so astonished that he let out a great shout. People came running from all directions to see what had happened. Soon there was a crowd, and the news spread, and before long people had come from all over the land of the Etruscans to stand in that field and listen.

The child's name was Tages. And he began to teach them.

He told them how to read the signs that the gods send. He told them what it means when lightning flashes in different parts of the sky. He told them how to look for signs of what is going to happen, and how to understand the will of the gods. He talked for a long time, and the people listened carefully. They wrote down every single word he said in books, so that nothing would ever be forgotten.

Those books became the most precious books of the Etruscans. Their priests studied them for hundreds of years, and learned more and more, and always went back to what Tages had first taught. Even the Romans used to ask the Etruscan priests for help when something strange happened and they wanted to know what the gods were telling them.

Many years later, a famous Roman named Cicero heard this story, and he laughed at it.

"A boy who was plowed up out of a field?" said Cicero. "If he was a god, why was he hiding in the dirt, waiting for a plow to dig him up? Couldn't a god teach people from somewhere a little higher up? And if he was a boy, how could he live buried in the ground? And where did he learn everything he knew?"

But a Roman poet named Ovid loved the story. When he wanted to describe something truly amazing, he said it was as amazing as the moment when the Etruscan farmer saw the lump of earth in his field begin to move, and turn into a boy, and open its brand-new mouth to tell of things to come.
