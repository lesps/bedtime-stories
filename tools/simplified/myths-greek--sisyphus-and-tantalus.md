Long ago there lived a king named Sisyphus. He was the craftiest, trickiest man in the whole world. He built a city called Corinth, and above it, on top of a steep hill, he built a fortress.

But the hilltop had one problem. There was no water up there. Every drop had to be carried all the way up the hill.

One day, Sisyphus saw something he was not supposed to see. He saw Zeus, the king of the gods, swoop down and carry away a young woman named Aegina, and fly off with her in secret.

Soon afterwards, an old river god came hurrying by. It was Asopus, Aegina's father. "My daughter is gone!" he cried. "Have you seen her? Do you know who took her?"

Sisyphus thought to himself. Here was his chance.

"I might know," he said. "And I might tell you. But first, you must give me something. Give my fortress a spring of fresh water, right up here on the hilltop."

So Asopus made a spring bubble up out of the rock, behind the temple on the hill. It was clear and cold, and it never ran dry. Then Sisyphus told him: "It was Zeus who took her."

But Zeus was very angry with Sisyphus for telling his secret. When Sisyphus died and went down to the underworld, Zeus gave him a punishment that would never end.

Sisyphus has to push an enormous stone up a steep hill. He pushes and heaves with his hands and his feet and his head, and the sweat pours down, and the dust flies up. And just when he is about to push it over the top, the stone slips back, and rolls all the way down to the bottom again. So Sisyphus has to trudge down after it and start all over again, forever.

There was another king who was punished in the underworld, and his name was Tantalus.

Tantalus was a favorite of the gods. They liked him so much that they invited him to their feasts on Mount Olympus, where they ate ambrosia and drank nectar, the food and drink of the gods, which make anyone who tastes them live forever.

But Tantalus could not handle so much good luck. He grew greedy and boastful. He stole some of the gods' nectar and ambrosia and took it home to share with his friends. And some say he told people all the gods' secrets too.

But no one can hide what they do from the gods. And so Tantalus was punished in the underworld.

He stands in a cool, clear pool of water, which comes right up to his chin. And he is terribly thirsty. But every time he bends down to drink, the water drains away, and there is nothing at his feet but dry black earth.

Over his head hang the branches of beautiful trees, full of fruit: pears and pomegranates, shiny apples, sweet figs and olives. And he is terribly hungry. But every time he reaches up to pick one, the wind blows the branches up and away, high into the clouds.

And above it all hangs a huge stone, always ready to fall, so that he can never feel safe or happy.

The Greeks never forgot these two kings. Even today, when something you want is dangled just out of your reach, we say it is "tantalizing." And a job that never, ever gets finished, no matter how hard you work, is sometimes called a "Sisyphean" task.
