In the north of Wales, in the land of Gwynedd, there lived a great magician called Gwydion. He was the nephew of the king, Math, who was the greatest magician of all. And Gwydion was also the best storyteller in the world.

Gwydion had a sister called Arianrhod, who lived in a castle by the sea. Once a tiny baby boy had been born to Arianrhod, and Gwydion had snatched him up and hidden him before anyone could see, and brought him up himself. And the boy grew amazingly fast. When he was one, he was as big as a two-year-old. When he was four, he was as big as an eight-year-old. And he loved Gwydion more than anyone in the world.

One day Gwydion took the boy to Arianrhod's castle. "Who is that boy with you?" she asked.

"This is your son," said Gwydion.

Arianrhod was angry. "Why have you brought him here to shame me? What is his name?"

"He hasn't got a name yet," said Gwydion.

"Then I will put a curse on him," said Arianrhod. "He will never have a name, unless I give him one myself."

"You are a wicked woman," said Gwydion. "But the boy will have a name, whether you like it or not." And he went away in a rage.

The next day, Gwydion went down to the seashore with the boy. He gathered seaweed and turned it into a boat by magic. He gathered dry sticks and reeds and turned them into the most beautiful leather anyone had ever seen. Then he changed his own face and the boy's face, so that nobody would know them, and sailed to Arianrhod's castle. And he began to make shoes.

"Who are those men in that boat?" asked Arianrhod.

"Shoemakers," said her servants.

"Go and find out what kind of leather they have."

When they told her how beautiful the leather was, she sent the shoemaker the measurement of her foot. But Gwydion made the shoes too big on purpose. So she sent him a new measurement, and he made them too small on purpose. "I can't make her shoes," he said, "unless I see her foot."

So Arianrhod came down to the boat herself. And just then a little wren landed on the deck. The boy took aim and shot at it, and hit it right on the leg.

Arianrhod smiled. "How steady the bright one's hand is," she said.

"Thank you!" said Gwydion. "Now he has a name, and a good one too. From now on he will be called Lleu Llaw Gyffes, the Bright One with the Steady Hand." And at once the boat turned back into seaweed, and the leather into sticks and reeds, and Gwydion and Lleu turned back into themselves.

Arianrhod was furious. "Then I will put another curse on him," she said. "He will never carry weapons or wear armour, unless I give them to him myself."

"He will have weapons," said Gwydion, "whatever you do."

Lleu grew up into a tall, handsome young man, who could ride any horse. But he was sad, because he had no weapons. So one day Gwydion said, "Come with me. We're going on an adventure."

They rode to Arianrhod's castle, disguised as two travelling poets, and Arianrhod welcomed them, and Gwydion told stories all evening. That night, while everyone was asleep, Gwydion used all his magic. And at dawn there was a great noise all over the land, of trumpets and shouting.

Arianrhod came knocking at their door. "We are in great danger!" she cried. "The sea is so full of enemy ships that we can't see the water! What shall we do?"

"Shut the castle gates," said Gwydion, "and we will defend it for you. Bring us weapons and armour."

So Arianrhod brought armour and weapons, and with her own hands she dressed the young man in armour and gave him his weapons.

"Have you finished?" asked Gwydion.

"Yes," said Arianrhod.

"Then we can take it all off again," said Gwydion. "There is no army. I made it up by magic, so that your son would get his weapons. And now he has them, and from your own hands."

Arianrhod was angrier than ever. "Then I will put a third curse on him," she said. "He will never have a wife from any people who live on this earth."

So Gwydion went to his uncle, Math, the greatest magician of all. "Well," said Math, "we will make him a wife, by our magic, out of flowers."

So they took the blossoms of the oak tree, and the yellow flowers of the broom, and the creamy flowers of the meadowsweet, and out of them, by their magic, they made a young woman, the loveliest anyone had ever seen. They called her Blodeuwedd, which means Flower-Face. And she married Lleu. Math gave them a fine palace and lands to rule, and Lleu ruled them well, and all his people loved him.

But one day, while Lleu was away visiting Math, Blodeuwedd heard a hunting horn. A lord called Gronw came riding by with his huntsmen. It was getting dark, so Blodeuwedd invited him in for the night. And as soon as she saw Gronw, she fell in love with him, and he fell in love with her.

They knew Lleu would never let her go. And so they made a wicked plan to get rid of him. "Find out how he can be killed," Gronw told her. "For he cannot be killed easily."

When Lleu came home, Blodeuwedd pretended to be worried. "I'm so afraid that you will die before me," she said. "Tell me how you could be killed, so that I can keep you safe."

Lleu loved her and trusted her, so he told her. "It isn't easy to kill me," he said. "It can only be done with a spear that has taken a whole year to make, and only on Sundays. And I can't be killed inside a house, or outside one, or on horseback, or on foot. Only if I stand by a river, under a little thatched roof, with one foot on the edge of a bath and the other foot on the back of a goat. Then whoever strikes me with that spear can kill me."

"Thank heaven," said Blodeuwedd, "that it will be easy to keep you safe from that."

But she sent word to Gronw at once. And Gronw spent a whole year making the spear.

When it was ready, Blodeuwedd said to Lleu, "I've been thinking. How could anyone ever stand with one foot on a bath and one on a goat? Will you show me?"

"I'll show you," said Lleu.

So she had a bath made by the river, with a thatched roof over it, and a goat brought. And Lleu climbed out of the bath and put one foot on the edge of it, and the other on the goat's back, to show her. And at that moment, Gronw, who was hiding on the hill nearby, threw the spear. It struck Lleu in the side.

And Lleu gave a terrible scream, and turned into an eagle, and flew away. And nobody saw him again.

Then Gronw took over Lleu's palace and his lands.

When Gwydion heard what had happened, he was heartbroken. "I will never rest," he said, "until I find out what has happened to Lleu." And he set out to search all over Wales.

One night he stayed at a farmer's house. And he heard the swineherd say that there was a sow that ran off every morning as soon as the pigsty was opened, and nobody knew where she went.

"Don't open the sty tomorrow until I'm there," said Gwydion.

So at dawn they opened the sty together, and the sow ran out, and Gwydion followed her. She ran up a stream to a valley, and stopped under a great oak tree, and began to eat. Gwydion looked up into the tree. And there, at the very top, sat an eagle. Its feathers were falling out, and it was thin and ill. And Gwydion knew that it was Lleu.

So Gwydion sang to the eagle:

"Oak that grows between two banks, dark is the sky and the hill. Shall I not know him by his wounds? This is Lleu."

And the eagle came down to the middle of the tree. So Gwydion sang again:

"Oak that grows on the high ground, drenched by nine score storms. In its branches sits Lleu the Bright One."

And the eagle came down to the lowest branch. And Gwydion sang a third time:

"Oak that grows beneath the hill, tall and noble to see. Shall I not say it? Lleu will come down to me."

And the eagle came down and sat on Gwydion's knee. Gwydion touched him with his magic wand, and he turned back into Lleu. But he was so thin and ill that he was nothing but skin and bone. Gwydion carried him home, and the best healers in Gwynedd looked after him, and by the end of the year he was well again.

Then Lleu and Gwydion and all the men of Gwynedd set off to take back Lleu's land. When Blodeuwedd heard that they were coming, she fled into the mountains. But Gwydion caught her.

"I will not kill you," said Gwydion. "I will do something worse. I will turn you into a bird. And because of the wrong you did to Lleu, you will never dare to show your face in the daylight, for all the other birds will chase you. But you will keep your name, Flower-Face."

And he turned her into an owl. And that is why, to this day, the owl only comes out at night, and why all the other birds chase it whenever they see it.

As for Gronw, Lleu said that the only thing that would satisfy him was for Gronw to stand where Lleu had stood, while Lleu threw a spear at him, just as Gronw had done to him. Gronw asked if he might hold a great flat stone in front of himself, and Lleu let him. But Lleu threw the spear so hard that it went right through the stone, and that was the end of Gronw. And the stone is still there, on the bank of the river, with a hole right through it.

So Lleu won back his land, and ruled it well. And in time he became the lord of all Gwynedd.
