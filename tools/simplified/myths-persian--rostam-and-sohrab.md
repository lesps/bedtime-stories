This is the story of how Sohrab fought with Rostam, and it is a story full of tears.

One day Rostam woke up feeling uneasy, so he went out hunting. He saddled Rakhsh and rode towards the city of Samangan, near the land of Turan. He hunted wild donkeys until he was tired, roasted one for his dinner, and lay down to sleep, while Rakhsh grazed beside him.

While he slept, seven horsemen of Turan rode by. They saw Rakhsh, and they wanted him. They threw their ropes at him, and Rakhsh fought hard, but there were too many of them, and they caught him and led him away to the city. When Rostam woke up and could not see his horse, he was very upset. "How can I fight the Turks, and how can I cross the desert alone?" he said. He followed Rakhsh's hoofprints, and they led him right up to the gates of Samangan.

The king of Samangan and his nobles came out to greet him. Rostam told them that Rakhsh had disappeared while he slept, and he swore that if his horse was not given back, there would be great trouble. The king spoke soothing words, and promised that no one would harm him, and invited him to stay at the palace while they searched. "Rakhsh can't be hidden for long," he said. So Rostam went in and feasted with the king, and that night he slept in a bed scented with musk and roses.

In the middle of the night the door of his room opened. In came a servant carrying a lamp, and behind her came a veiled lady. "Who are you," asked Rostam, "and what do you want of me in the dark night?"

"I am Tahmineh, the daughter of the king of Samangan," she said. "No prince on earth has been good enough for me, and no man has ever seen my face. But I have heard of your brave deeds, how you fear neither demon nor lion, and how you went alone into Mazandaran, and I have longed to see you. Now God has brought you to my father's house. If you will have me, I will be your wife, and I will marry no one else. Perhaps God will give us a son as strong and brave as you. And if you will listen to me, I will bring you back Rakhsh, your horse."

Rostam looked at her, and saw that she was beautiful and wise. And when he heard about Rakhsh, he made up his mind. He sent a wise man to the king to ask for Tahmineh's hand, and the king was glad. They were married, as the custom was, and everyone in the city rejoiced.

Rostam took from his arm a precious onyx stone, famous all over the world, and gave it to Tahmineh. "Keep this jewel," he said. "If God gives you a daughter, fasten it in her hair, and it will keep her from harm. But if you have a son, tie it on his arm, as his father wore it. He will be strong and tall, like the heroes of my family."

The next day the king came to tell Rostam that Rakhsh had been found. Rostam ran out to stroke his horse, and saddled him with his own hands, and thanked God. Then he knew it was time to go. He held Tahmineh in his arms, and wept, and kissed her hair, and then Rakhsh carried him away. Tahmineh was very sad. And Rostam rode home to Zabulistan, thinking about all that had happened, but he told no one about it.

Nine months later, Tahmineh had a son who looked just like his father, a baby whose mouth was full of smiles, and he was called Sohrab. When he was one month old he was as big as a one-year-old. At five he could use all the weapons of war, and at ten no one in the land could beat him in games of strength. Then he came to his mother and said, "I am taller and stronger than all the other boys. Tell me who my father is! What shall I say when people ask me?"

Tahmineh smiled, because her son was as fiery as his father. "Be glad, my son, and don't be angry," she said. "You are the son of Rostam, from the family of Sam and Zal. Since God made the world there has never been anyone like Rostam, your father." She showed him a letter from Rostam, and the gold and jewels Rostam had sent when he was born. "But keep this secret," she said. "Afrasiyab, the king of Turan, is Rostam's enemy. If he found out about you, he would try to destroy you because he hates your father. And if Rostam knew how strong you had become, he might send for you, and then I would lose you."

"Nothing can be hidden for ever," said Sohrab. "Everyone knows about Rostam, so why did you keep it from me so long? I will lead an army of Turks into Iran, and throw Kay Kavus off his throne, and give the crown to Rostam. Then together we will beat Afrasiyab, and you shall be Queen of Iran. But first I need a horse strong enough to carry me."

Tahmineh was proud of his courage, and she had all the horses brought out. Sohrab tested them, just as his father had once done, and pressed them down with his hand, but none was strong enough. At last someone told him of a young horse whose father was Rakhsh. Sohrab tested it and found it strong, and he jumped on its back. "With a horse like you," he cried, "I will make the world dark for many!"

His grandfather, the king of Samangan, opened his treasury for him, and Sohrab gathered an army. When Afrasiyab heard, he was pleased, and he sent two of his warriors, Human and Barman, to join Sohrab's army. But he told them a secret. "Sohrab is Rostam's son," he said. "But Rostam must not find out who it is that he is fighting. Then perhaps the young lion will kill Rostam, and Iran will fall into my hands, and afterwards we can deal with Sohrab. And if Rostam kills Sohrab, his grief when he learns that he has killed his son will bring him to the grave."

So Afrasiyab sent his warriors with rich gifts and a friendly letter, and Sohrab was delighted, for he thought no one could stand against him now. He led the army into Iran, spreading fear wherever they went, until they came to the White Castle, the fortress that guarded Iran.

The keeper of the castle, Hujir, rode out to fight Sohrab. But Sohrab beat him as if he were a baby, and tied him up, and sent him to his camp as a prisoner. When the people in the castle heard this, they were afraid. Now in the castle there lived a warrior maiden, Gurdafrid, the daughter of old Gustahem. She was ashamed that Hujir had been captured. So she put on armour, hid her long hair under a helmet, rode out before the walls like a warrior, and called out a challenge. None of the Turks dared to answer, for they did not know that she was a woman, and they were afraid of her. But Sohrab stepped forward.

Gurdafrid shot arrows at him as thick as hail, and Sohrab had to hide behind his shield. Then he charged at her. She dropped her bow and thrust at him with her lance so hard that she nearly knocked him off his horse. Sohrab was furious. He grabbed her horse's reins and pulled her to the ground. But she drew her sword, broke his lance in two, jumped back on her horse, and rode back towards the castle. Sohrab chased after her, caught her, and pulled off her helmet, to see the face of the warrior who had stood up to him. And out tumbled her long dark hair! Sohrab saw that it was a woman who had fought him so well. "If the daughters of Iran are like you," he said, "no one can beat this land." And he caught her in his rope.

But Gurdafrid was clever. "If you take me back as your prisoner," she said, "your army will laugh to find that you were held off by a woman. Let us make peace instead. The castle will be yours. Follow me, and take it."

Sohrab believed her, and followed her to the castle gate. Her father opened it, and Gurdafrid slipped inside, and shut the door in Sohrab's face! Then she came out on the walls and laughed at him. "Go home," she called. "If you can't beat a woman, what will you do when Rostam comes?" Sohrab rode off in a rage. That night everyone in the castle escaped through a secret tunnel, but first old Gustahem sent a letter to Kay Kavus, the king of Iran, to warn him that a young warrior had come from Turan, a child in years but a lion in strength.

Kay Kavus was afraid. "Who can stand against this Turk?" he said. "They say he is as strong as Rostam."

"Only Rostam can help us," said all his warriors. So the king sent Gew to fetch Rostam, and told him not to waste a moment.

When Rostam read the king's letter, he asked Gew about Sohrab. "I have a son in Samangan," he said, "but he is still a little child, playing children's games. This can't be him." And Rostam was in no hurry. He feasted with Gew for three days, and only on the fourth did they set out.

Kay Kavus was furious at the delay. "Who is Rostam, to disobey me?" he shouted. "Take him and hang him!" Then Rostam was furious too. "I am a free man," he said, "and I serve no one but God. Without Rostam, Kay Kavus is nothing!" And he stormed out, and rode away on Rakhsh. The nobles were horrified, for without Rostam they could never stand against the Turk. Wise old Gudarz made the king see how wrong he had been, and then rode after Rostam. "If you leave now," he said, "people will say that Rostam ran away from a beardless boy." So Rostam came back, and this time Kay Kavus came down from his throne to meet him and begged his pardon, and they were friends again.

Then the great army of Iran marched out, and pitched its tents on the plain near the White Castle. That night Rostam dressed up as a Turk, and crept secretly into the castle to see what sort of man Sohrab was. He saw Sohrab feasting with his warriors, tall as a cypress tree, with arms as strong as a hero's.

Now beside Sohrab sat his uncle Zindeh, Tahmineh's brother. She had sent him with Sohrab to point out his father to him, so that the two would never fight each other, for he was the only man in the army who knew Rostam by sight. Zindeh thought he saw someone spying, and went to look. "Who are you?" he said. "Come out into the light." But before he could say any more, Rostam struck him down, and he never spoke again.

Sohrab was very sad when they found Zindeh, but he did not let the feast stop. And Rostam went back to his camp, and told the king that no one like Sohrab had ever come out of Turan.

In the morning Sohrab took his prisoner, Hujir, up a hill from which they could see the whole Iranian camp. "Tell me the truth," he said, "and I will set you free. Whose tents are those?" Hujir named them one by one. Then Sohrab pointed to a green tent, with Kaveh's banner in front of it and a huge horse standing beside it, and a great warrior sitting inside. "Whose is that?" he asked. He thought he could see the signs his mother had told him about.

But Hujir thought, "If I tell him that is Rostam, he will go and attack him." So he said, "That is some friend of the king's from far-off China. I don't know his name." Three times Sohrab asked, and three times Hujir said he did not know. "Rostam is at home in Zabulistan," he said, "for it is the feast of roses." At last Sohrab threatened him: "Show me Rostam's tent, or lose your head!" But Hujir thought, "What is my life, compared to Iran?" and still he would not tell. Then Sohrab struck him down.

Sohrab rode at the Iranian camp, broke through the fence with his spear, and called out in a voice of thunder, challenging the king himself to fight him. No one dared to answer. "Only Rostam can face him!" they cried. They buckled Rostam into his armour, threw his leopard skin around him, saddled Rakhsh, and pushed him out. "Hurry!" they called. "This is no ordinary fight!"

When Rostam saw the young man, so brave and strong, he said, "Let us go away from the armies, and fight between them." And when they stood alone, Rostam's heart filled with pity. "Young man," he said, "the air is warm and soft, but the earth is cold. I don't want to take your life. No one has ever beaten me, not men, nor demons, nor dragons. Leave the Turks. Iran needs heroes like you."

As Rostam spoke, Sohrab's heart went out to him. "Tell me your name, hero," he said. "Please tell me the truth. I think you must be Rostam, the son of Zal."

"You are wrong," said Rostam. "I am not Rostam. Rostam is a great champion, and I am only a servant, with no crown or throne." He said this to frighten Sohrab, so that he would think there was someone even stronger in the Iranian camp. But Sohrab's high hopes were dashed, and the bright day grew dark for him.

Then they fought. They fought with spears until the spears broke, and with swords until the swords were notched like saws, and with clubs until the clubs broke. They wrestled until their armour was torn and their horses were worn out, but neither could win. Rostam had never fought anyone like this; even the White Div had been nothing to it. Then Sohrab struck Rostam with his club so hard that Rostam staggered. And as the day was ending, they agreed to stop until the morning.

That night Sohrab said to Human, "This old man I am fighting is as tall as I am, and I think I can see the signs my mother told me about. My heart goes out to him. Could he be Rostam, my father?" But Human remembered Afrasiyab's orders. "I have often seen Rostam in battle," he lied, "and this man is not like him at all." Sohrab said no more, but he was not satisfied.

In the morning Sohrab came out smiling. "Why should we fight?" he said. "Let us throw down our weapons, and sit together as friends. I feel that this fight is wrong. Tell me your name. I think you are Rostam, the lord of Zabulistan, the son of Zal."

But Rostam said, "We have not come here to talk, but to fight. I am old and you are young, but God will decide between us."

"Then let it be as it must be," said Sohrab. They fought from morning until sunset, and at last Sohrab threw Rostam to the ground, knelt on him, and drew his sword. Then Rostam knew that only a trick could save him. "Young man," he said, "you don't know the rules of fighting. The law of honour says that the first time you throw a brave man down, you must let him go. Only if you beat him a second time may you kill him."

Sohrab believed him, and let him go. But when he told Human, Human said, "You fool, that is not the rule at all!" Sohrab was ashamed. "Never mind," he said. "He won't stand against me a third time."

Rostam went to a stream, and washed, and prayed to God to give him strength. God gave him so much strength that the rock he stood on gave way under his feet. Then Rostam prayed for some of it to be taken back, and God listened.

When they met for the third time, Sohrab came out fresh as a giant, but Rostam was full of worry. He prayed for his strength to be given back to him. Then he grabbed Sohrab by the belt and threw him to the ground so hard that Sohrab was hurt past healing, and he drew his sword.

Sohrab knew it was the end. "This is my own fault," he said. "But I didn't come for glory. I came to find my father. My mother told me how I would know him, and now I die longing for him, without ever seeing his face. But I tell you this: even if you turned into a fish in the deepest sea, or a star in the farthest sky, my father would find you, when he hears what has happened. For my father is Rostam, and he will be told how his son Sohrab died looking for him."

When Rostam heard this, his sword fell from his hand. A groan broke from his heart, the world went dark, and he fainted beside his son. When he opened his eyes, he cried out, "Do you have some sign of Rostam with you, so I know this is true? For I am Rostam, the unhappy!"

Sohrab's misery was endless. "If you are really my father," he cried, "then you have hurt your own son, because you were so stubborn. I begged you to tell me your name. Open my armour, and look at the jewel on my arm. It is the onyx my father gave as a sign, so that he would know me."

Rostam opened his armour and saw the onyx. Then he tore his clothes, and covered his head with ashes, and wept and roared with grief. But Sohrab said, "It's no use. Don't cry. It must have been meant to happen."

When the sun set and Rostam did not come back, the Iranians went to look for him, and when they found Rakhsh alone, they thought Rostam must be dead. Sohrab saw them coming in the distance. "Father," he said, "do one thing for me, out of love. Don't let the king attack the men of Turan. They only came because I wanted them to; this was all my doing. I came like thunder, and I go like the wind. But perhaps we shall meet again above."

Rostam promised. He went to the Iranians and told them what he had done, and they wept with him. And he sent a message to Human: "Take your army and go home across the river. I will fight no more. But I will never speak to you again, for you hid from my son the signs of his father, and led him into this pit."

Then Rostam went back to Sohrab. He remembered that Kay Kavus had a healing balm, and he sent Gudarz to beg the king for it. But the king's heart was hard. He remembered only Rostam's proud words, and he was afraid that if father and son joined together, they would be stronger than he was. So he refused. "The king's heart is like stone," said Gudarz. "Go yourself, and perhaps you can soften it." Rostam set out to go to the king, but before he got there, a messenger came after him, and told him that Sohrab had died.

Then Rostam wept as the earth had never heard anyone weep. "I am old, and I have killed my son," he cried. "I am strong, and I have cut down this mighty boy." He made a great fire, and burned his tent, his saddle, his leopard skin and all his armour, and stood and watched his pride turn to dust.

He had Sohrab wrapped in cloth of gold, and carried him home to Zabulistan, with the whole army in mourning. When Zal saw the army coming home so sadly, he did not know what had happened, for he could see Rostam riding at the front. Then Rostam led him to the coffin, and showed him the young man, so like his grandfather Sam, and told him everything. Rudabeh came out and wept with them. They built Sohrab a tomb shaped like a horse's hoof, and laid him in it, and for a long time Rostam's house was full of sorrow, and he could not hold up his head.

The news reached Turan, and there too everyone wept for the young hero who had died so young. When Tahmineh heard, she was wild with grief. She had Sohrab's clothes and his horse brought to her, and she stroked the horse and wept over its hooves, and held his clothes as if her son were still inside them. She gave his gold and jewels to the poor. And a year later she died of grief, and her spirit went after Sohrab, her son.
