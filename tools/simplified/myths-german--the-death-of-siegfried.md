Siegfried married Kriemhild, King Gunther's sister, and took her home to his kingdom, the Land of the Nibelungs. They were very happy there. And King Gunther married Queen Brunhild and brought her home to Burgundy.

Some time later, Gunther invited Siegfried and Kriemhild to a great feast. But when Queen Brunhild saw how everyone honored Siegfried, she began to wonder. Wasn't Siegfried supposed to be only Gunther's servant? And she remembered the bright hero who had once woken her from her long sleep, and how Gunther had won the contests in Isenland but could never do anything so strong again. She grew jealous, and then very angry. The two queens quarreled, and at last Brunhild guessed that she had been tricked.

Grim old Hagen saw his chance. He hated Siegfried, because he was jealous of him. "Siegfried is a traitor," he told Gunther. "He wants to take your kingdom." And weak King Gunther believed him.

Now, there was a secret about Siegfried that only Kriemhild knew. Long ago, when he killed the dragon Fafnir, he had been covered in the dragon's blood, and it had made his skin so hard that no weapon could hurt him. But a leaf from a linden tree had fallen onto his back, between his shoulders, and that one little spot had not been touched by the blood. That was the only place where Siegfried could be hurt.

Hagen went to Kriemhild and pretended to be worried about Siegfried. "I will ride close behind him in battle and protect him," he said. "Is there anything I should know?"

Kriemhild trusted him, because he was her uncle. So she told him the secret. "Mark the spot on his coat," said Hagen, "so I know where to guard him." And Kriemhild sewed a little silk linden leaf onto Siegfried's coat, right over the place.

The next day Gunther said, "It's Siegfried's last day with us. Let's go hunting in the Odenwald."

Siegfried's favorite hunting clothes had gone missing, so he put on the embroidered coat instead, and he did not notice the little silk leaf. But Kriemhild was afraid now. She had begun to think she had made a terrible mistake.

"Please don't go hunting," she begged. "Last night I dreamed that Odin's ravens said, 'What is worse than a false friend? What is more dangerous than a secret enemy?' And then a dead leaf blew in through the window and fell on my bed."

But Siegfried only laughed at her dream, and said goodbye until the evening.

In the forest, Siegfried hunted with just one huntsman and his dog. He was faster and stronger than anyone. "My lord," said the huntsman at last, laughing, "if you keep on like this, there will be no animals left in the whole forest!"

When the others joined them, they saw that Siegfried had caught ten times as much as all of them together, and Hagen's face grew dark with jealousy.

On the way to lunch, a big bear came out of the bushes. "Here's a guest for our dinner!" cried Siegfried. He chased it, caught it in his arms, tied it up with a rope, and led it back to camp. When he untied it, the bear ran right through the cooks' fires, knocking over all the pots and pans, and everybody laughed.

Then they sat on the grass to eat. But there was nothing to drink, no wine and no water.

"I sent the wine to the wrong place, by mistake," said Hagen. "But there is a cool spring near here, under a linden tree. They say Siegfried is the fastest runner in the world. Let's have a race to the spring and see who wins."

"Agreed!" said Siegfried. "And to make it fair, I will carry my spear and shield and sword and bow, and you two can run with nothing."

So they ran. Gunther and Hagen ran like panthers, but Siegfried flew like an arrow, and he reached the spring before they were halfway there. He laid down his weapons and leaned his shield against the tree. But he did not drink. He was too polite to drink before the king. He waited until Gunther had come and had his drink.

Then Siegfried knelt down at the edge of the spring to drink. And while he was bending over the water, Hagen quietly hid Siegfried's sword and bow. Then he picked up Siegfried's own spear and threw it, straight at the little silk leaf on his back.

It was the most cowardly thing anyone had ever done.

Siegfried sprang up. He looked for his sword, but it was gone. So he grabbed his heavy shield, and he chased Hagen, and he caught him and knocked him to the ground. But then Siegfried's strength left him, and he fell down among the flowers.

"You cowards," he said. "I have always tried to help you, and this is how you pay me back. You will be sorry for this forever."

Gunther began to cry and wring his hands.

"It is too late for tears," said Siegfried. "You should have thought of that before. If there is any goodness left in you, take care of Kriemhild, your sister."

And then Siegfried, the greatest of heroes, died.

The trees shivered and dropped their leaves. The birds stopped singing and flew away. And a great silence fell over the forest, as if the whole world was sad.

All the men who were there, except the four who had planned it, bowed their heads and wept. They laid Siegfried gently on his shield, with his weapons beside him. And when night came, they carried him home to the castle.
