---
title: Midir and Etain
basedOn: the Wooing of Etain (Tochmarc Étaíne), an Irish tale preserved in manuscripts from the 1100s onwards
workId: irish-etain
order: 7
---

Long ago, when the magical Tuatha Dé Danann lived in Ireland, there was a proud lord called Midir, who lived inside a fairy hill. He fell in love with Etain, the most beautiful woman in all Ireland, and she became his wife, and he brought her home to his hill.

But Midir already had a wife, called Fuamnach, and she was terribly jealous. She knew magic, and she had a druid to help her. One day she struck Etain with a wand of rowan wood, and turned her into a pool of water.

Then Fuamnach went away. And the warmth of the fire and the air dried up the pool, and out of it came a little worm. And the worm turned into a fly. But this was no ordinary fly. It was a great, shining purple fly, as big as a man's head, the most beautiful creature in the world. The humming of its wings was sweeter than the music of harps and pipes. Its eyes shone like jewels. Its sweet scent took away anyone's hunger and thirst. And the drops of water that fell from its wings could cure any sickness.

The fly was Etain. And Midir knew it. She flew everywhere with him. Her humming sang him to sleep at night, and if anyone came near who did not love him, she woke him. And Midir loved no one else as long as he could see her.

When Fuamnach found out, she was furious. She called up a magic wind, which blew Etain out of Midir's hill and far away. For seven years the wind blew her about over Ireland, and she could not rest on any hill or tree, but only on the rocks and the waves of the sea.

At last she landed, tired and weak, on the cloak of Angus, Midir's foster son, at his house beside the River Boyne. Angus knew at once who she was. He made a little sun-house for her, with clear windows all around it, and filled it with sweet-smelling flowers, so that she could live on their scent. Every night she slept in it, and Angus carried it with him wherever he went. And little by little, Etain grew strong and bright again.

But Fuamnach found out where she was. And she came and blew her away again, with another magic wind, for seven more years.

At the end of the seven years, the wind dropped Etain down through the roof of a house in Ulster, where a feast was being held. She fell into a cup of wine that the lady of the house was holding. The lady drank it, and swallowed the fly without knowing. And not long afterwards, she had a baby daughter, and called her Etain. It was Etain born again, more than a thousand years after she had first been born. But she did not remember anything of her life before.

Etain grew up to be the most beautiful woman in Ireland again, and she married the High King of Ireland, whose name was Eochaid.

Then, one lovely summer day, King Eochaid looked out from the wall of Tara and saw a stranger standing there: a young warrior in a purple cloak, with golden-yellow hair and eyes that shone. He was carrying a silver chessboard, with chessmen of gold.

"Who are you?" asked the king.

"My name is Midir," said the stranger. "I have come to play chess with you. They say you are the best player in Ireland."

"What shall we play for?" asked the king.

"Whatever the winner chooses," said Midir.

They played, and the king won. "I choose fifty fine horses," said the king. And the next morning, there were fifty fine horses outside Tara.

They played again, and again the king won. This time, he set Midir hard tasks: to clear all the stones from the fields of Meath, to clear the rushes from another land, to plant a forest, and to build a road across the great bog of Lamrach, where nobody had ever been able to walk.

That night, the king's steward crept out to watch. He saw a great host of fairy folk building the road. They piled up a whole forest of tree trunks for the bottom of it, and gravel and stones on top, and Midir stood over them, urging them on. And in the morning, the road across the bog was finished.

Then Midir came back to the king. "Let's play one more game," he said. "And this time, whoever wins can choose any prize."

"Very well," said the king. And this time, Midir won.

"What do you choose?" asked the king.

"To put my arms around Etain," said Midir, "and to kiss her."

The king was silent for a long time. Then he said, "Come back in one month, and you shall have it."

For Midir had already spoken to Etain, in secret. He had sung to her about his own land, the wonderful land inside the fairy hills: "Will you come with me, lady, to the wonderful land where there is music? There, people's hair is the colour of primroses, and nobody grows old, and there is no sorrow and no sin." But Etain did not remember Midir. "I will only go with you," she said, "if the king himself lets me go."

Now the king was determined that Midir should never take her. On the day the month was over, he gathered all the best warriors of Ireland inside Tara, and locked the doors, and set guards all around. The king and Etain sat in the middle of the great hall, with the warriors all around them.

And suddenly Midir was standing among them, more handsome than ever. Nobody had seen him come in.

"I have come for what you promised me," he said.

"I have not thought about it," said the king.

"You promised," said Midir. "And Etain has said she will come with me if you let her go."

"Then you may put your arms around her," said the king, "here in the middle of the hall."

So Midir took his weapons in his left hand, and put his right arm around Etain. Then Midir rose up into the air with her, up and up, and out through the hole in the roof.

The king and all the warriors rushed outside. And there, high in the sky over Tara, they saw two white swans, flying round and round. Then the two swans flew away together, towards the fairy hills in the south. And Etain went home with Midir at last.
