title: The Hare in the Moon
adaptedFrom: cowell-jataka--the-hare-in-the-moon

namesNote: The Jātaka tells of the Buddha's earlier lives, before he became the Buddha. Sakka is the king of the gods, and a brahmin is a priest.
---

Long ago, when Brahmadatta was king of Benares, the Buddha-to-be was born as a young hare. He lived in a wood, with a mountain on one side, a river on another, and a village on the third. The hare had three friends: a monkey, a jackal and an otter. All day long each of them looked for food in his own part of the wood, and in the evening they all met together. And the wise hare taught his friends to be good: to give to those in need, to keep the rules of goodness, and to keep the holy days.

One evening the hare looked up at the sky, and saw from the moon that the next day would be a holy day, a day for fasting. "Tomorrow is a holy day," he told his friends. "Let us all keep the rules of goodness. And if anyone comes to you begging, give him some of your own food." His friends agreed, and went home.

Early the next morning, the otter went down to the river Ganges to look for food. Now a fisherman had caught seven red fish, strung them together, and buried them in the sand on the bank, and then gone off downstream to catch more. The otter smelled the fish and dug them up. "Does anyone own these fish?" he called out, three times. No one answered, so he took the fish home, to eat later. Then he lay down, thinking how good he was.

The jackal went out to look for food too. In a field-watcher's hut he found two roasting spits, a lizard and a pot of curds. "Who do these belong to?" he called, three times. No one answered, so he hung the pot around his neck by its rope, took the spits and the lizard in his teeth, and carried them home, to eat later. Then he lay down, thinking how good he was.

The monkey climbed into the trees and picked a bunch of mangoes, and took them home, to eat later. Then he lay down, thinking how good he was.

But the hare thought, "I eat only grass, and I cannot give grass to a beggar. I have no oil or rice or anything like that. If a beggar comes to me, I will have to give him my own body to eat."

Far away in heaven, the white marble throne of Sakka, the king of the gods, grew warm at such great goodness. Sakka looked down to see why, and he decided to test the hare.

First Sakka dressed himself as a brahmin, and went and stood by the otter's home. "Why are you standing there?" asked the otter.

"Wise sir," said the brahmin, "I am fasting today. But if I could get something to eat, I could do my holy work."

"I will give you food," said the otter. And he said:

> Seven red fish I brought safe from the river.
> Eat your fill, brahmin, and stay in this wood.

"Let it wait until tomorrow," said the brahmin. And he went to the jackal, who offered him food too:

> A lizard and a pot of curds, the watchman's supper,
> and two spits to roast them on, I stole, though I should not have done.
> What I have, I give to you. Eat, brahmin, I pray,
> and stay a while with us in this wood.

"Let it wait until tomorrow," said the brahmin. And he went to the monkey, who offered him food too:

> An icy stream, a ripe mango, and the cool green shade
> are yours to enjoy, if you will stay in the forest.

"Let it wait until tomorrow," said the brahmin. And last of all he went to the wise hare. When the hare heard that he wanted food, he was delighted. "Brahmin," he said, "you have done well to come to me. Today I will give a gift I have never given before. But you must not break the rules of goodness by killing any living thing. Go and gather wood, and light a fire, and tell me when it is ready. I will jump into the fire, and when my body is roasted, you shall eat it, and do your holy work." And he said:

> No sesame, no beans, no rice have I to give,
> but I give you my own body, roasted in the fire.

Then Sakka, by his wonderful power, made a heap of glowing coals appear, and came and told the hare that it was ready.

The hare got up from his bed of grass and came to the fire. He shook himself three times, so that any little insects in his fur could jump out and not be burned. Then, giving his whole body as a gift, he leaped up, full of joy, and dropped onto the glowing coals, like a royal swan landing on a bed of lotus flowers.

But the fire did not burn him. It did not even warm the tips of his fur. It was as if he had jumped into frost and snow. "Brahmin," said the hare, "your fire is icy cold. It cannot even warm the hairs on my body. What does this mean?"

"Wise hare," said Sakka, "I am not a brahmin. I am Sakka, and I came to test your goodness."

"Sakka," said the hare, "if you, and everyone in the whole world, came to test me, you would never find me unwilling to give." And he gave a great shout of joy, like a lion's roar.

"Wise hare," said Sakka, "let your goodness be known for ever and ever." And he squeezed a mountain, and with the juice that came out of it, he drew the shape of a hare on the moon. Then he laid the hare gently on a bed of soft young grass in the wood, and went back to heaven.

And the four wise friends lived happily together, keeping the rules of goodness and the holy days, all their lives.

Long afterwards, the Buddha told this story. "In those days," he said, "I myself was the wise hare."
