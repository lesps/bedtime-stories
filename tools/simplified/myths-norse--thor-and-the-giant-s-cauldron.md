One day the gods came home from hunting, hungry and thirsty, and they wanted a feast. So they shook their magic twigs and read the signs, to find out where to have it. And the signs said: at the hall of Aegir.

Aegir was a giant who lived by the sea, and he brewed the best beer in the world. He was sitting at home, as happy as a child, when Thor came in and stared him straight in the eye. "You will make a feast for the gods," said Thor, "and you will make it often."

Aegir did not like being told what to do. So he thought of a way to make trouble. "Very well," he said. "But first you must bring me a kettle big enough to brew beer for all of you."

The gods did not have a kettle that big. Nobody did. None of them knew where to find one, until Tyr, who was a good friend to Thor, spoke up.

"Far away in the east, past the icy rivers, at the very edge of the sky, lives a giant named Hymir," said Tyr. "He is my father, and he is very fierce. And he has a cauldron that is a mile deep!" (In this old poem Tyr is a giant's son, though other stories say his father was Odin.)

"Do you think we can get it?" asked Thor.

"Yes, my friend," said Tyr, "if we are clever about it."

So they drove away from Asgard that very day, as fast as they could go, until they came to Hymir's house. Thor put his goats in the stable, and the two of them went into the giant's hall.

The first person they met was Tyr's grandmother. She had nine hundred heads, and Tyr did not like her one bit. But then Tyr's mother came out to meet them. She was all shining with gold, and she brought her son a cup of beer.

"Welcome, both of you," she said. "But you had better hide under the kettles. My husband is often grumpy with guests."

Late that evening, Hymir came home from hunting. As he stomped into the hall, the icebergs rang, and his beard was frozen stiff, like a bush covered in frost.

"Good evening, Hymir!" said his wife. "Be cheerful! Your son has come home from his long journey, and with him is our famous enemy, Thor, the friend of people. Look, there they are at the end of the hall, hiding behind that post."

Hymir glared at the post. His glare was so fierce that the post shattered into pieces, and the beam above it snapped in two. Down came eight kettles with a crash, and every one of them broke, except one big, hard-hammered cauldron.

Tyr and Thor came out. Hymir looked at Thor, and he did not like what he saw. But he had three oxen cooked for supper, and Thor ate two of them all by himself.

"Tomorrow night," grumbled Hymir, "the three of us will have to live on whatever fish we catch."

"I'll row out to sea and fish with you," said Thor, "if you give me some bait."

"Go and find your own bait among my cattle," said Hymir, "if you're brave enough."

So Thor went into the woods and found a huge black ox, and he took its head, horns and all, for his bait.

"You'd have done better to sit still," grumbled Hymir.

They rowed out to sea. Thor wanted to row farther, but Hymir did not want to go any farther at all. Hymir fished, and he pulled up two whales on his hook at once. But at the back of the boat, Thor was quietly getting his own fishing line ready. And on the hook he put the ox's head.

Down at the bottom of the sea lay the Midgard Serpent, the great snake that circles the whole world. It saw the bait, and it opened its mouth wide and gulped it down.

Thor pulled and pulled, and he hauled the serpent up, glistening with poison, right to the side of the boat. Then he struck it on the top of its ugly head with his hammer.

The icebergs boomed. The caves howled. The whole old earth shook. And then the serpent sank back into the sea.

Hymir was not happy at all. He did not say a word all the way home. When they reached the shore, he said, "Will you do half the work? Will you carry the whales up to the house, or tie up the boat?"

Thor grabbed the front of the boat and picked it up, with the water still sloshing inside it, and the oars and the bailing scoop too, and he carried it all up through the woods to Hymir's house.

But Hymir still wanted to argue. "You may be able to row," he said, "but nobody is truly strong unless he can break my cup."

Thor took the cup and threw it at a stone post. The post broke in two! But the cup came back to Hymir without a scratch.

Then Tyr's mother leaned over and whispered a secret to Thor, one that only she knew. "Hit Hymir's head with it. His head is harder than any cup."

So Thor got up on one knee, and he swung the cup at Hymir's head with all his godly strength. Hymir's head was not hurt at all. But the cup broke into pieces.

"Much that was good has gone from me," said Hymir sadly, "now that my cup is broken." Then he said, "Now let's see if you can carry the cauldron out of my house."

Tyr tried twice to lift it. But each time the cauldron stayed right where it was.

Then Thor grabbed it by the rim. He pulled so hard that his feet went right through the floor. He lifted the cauldron up and put it on his head, like a giant hat, and its rings jingled down around his heels.

They had gone a long way when Thor looked back. And there came Hymir out of the rocky caves in the east, with a whole crowd of many-headed giants, chasing after them!

Thor put the cauldron down. He threw his hammer, Mjollnir, and that was the end of the giants who had come chasing them.

On the way home, one of Thor's goats went lame, and the poem says that was Loki's fault. But that is another story.

At last Thor came to the gods' meeting, proud and strong, carrying Hymir's cauldron. And ever since then, every year when the harvest is in, all the gods go to drink beer at Aegir's hall, brewed in the giant's great cauldron.
