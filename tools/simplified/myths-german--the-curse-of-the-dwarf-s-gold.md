Siegfried rode his shining horse Greyfell back to the forest, to his old master Regin. Regin welcomed him gladly and took him into the room full of treasure.

"At last," said Regin, and his eyes were full of longing. "I have waited so many years for this day. Now a great wrong will be put right, and a great treasure will be mine."

"What wrong?" asked Siegfried. "And what treasure?"

Regin picked up his harp and played a soft, sad tune. Then he told this story.

"Long, long ago, when the world was young, the gods used to come down from Asgard to walk among people. Once, three of them came together: Odin, the wise; Hoenir, who made people cheerful; and Loki, who only ever made trouble. They came dressed as ordinary huntsmen, and they had no more strength than ordinary men.

"One afternoon they came to a waterfall, and there on the bank they saw an otter about to eat a salmon. Loki threw a stone and killed the otter, and he boasted about it, and he took the otter and the fish with him.

"That night they came to a farmhouse and asked for shelter. The farmer, Hreidmar, was my father. Loki threw down the otter and said, 'We've brought our own supper.' But my father cried out in horror. 'That was no otter. That was my son Oddar, who liked to play in the shape of an otter. You have killed him!'

"He called for help, and my brother Fafnir and I ran in and tied the three huntsmen up. 'You must pay for what you have done,' said my father. 'Fill this otter skin with gold and jewels, until not a single hair can be seen.'

"So Loki was untied, and he put on his magic shoes, which could carry him over land and sea, and he flew away to find the gold. He went to the place where the river Rhine begins, a tiny stream running out from under a mountain of ice. There lived the dwarf Andvari, who had the greatest treasure in the world. But all Loki could see there was a salmon swimming in the stream.

"Loki was clever. He knew that salmon was really Andvari. So he borrowed a magic net from Ran, the queen of the sea, a net that nothing could escape from. He threw it into the stream, and the salmon was caught. And when Loki pulled it out, it was not a fish any more, but the dwarf Andvari.

"'Tell me where your treasure is!' said Loki.

"Andvari helped him roll away a great stone, and underneath was a room shining brighter than the sun, full of gold and jewels. Loki scooped it all into the net. But then he saw a ring on the dwarf's finger. It was shaped like a snake with its tail in its mouth, with tiny diamonds for scales and red rubies for eyes.

"'Give me that ring too!' said Loki.

"'Please let me keep this one thing,' begged the dwarf. But Loki tore it off his finger.

"Then Andvari was very angry, and he put a curse on the gold. 'May this treasure bring nothing but trouble to everyone who owns it! And may that ring bring sorrow to whoever wears it!'

"Loki did not care. He flew back to our farmhouse. My father spread out the otter skin, and it grew and grew until it covered a whole field! The gods covered every bit of it with gold and jewels. But there was still one hair showing, by the otter's nose. And there was no gold left.

"Now Odin had kept the snake ring for himself, because he liked it so much. But now he had to take it out and lay it on that last hair. So the ransom was paid, and we let the gods go.

"As soon as they had gone, Fafnir and I asked our father to share the treasure with us. But he said no. He would keep it all. So we went out to the fields to look after the sheep, and our father sat down to guard his gold.

"He picked up the snake ring and stared into its red ruby eyes. And as he stared, he thought only of his gold. There was no room left in his heart for love or kindness. And slowly he began to change. His blood turned cold. His body grew long and scaly. And he became a great snake, coiled around his treasure, just like the ring.

"When Fafnir came home that evening, he found a huge snake lying around the gold, and no father. He thought the snake had eaten our father, so he drew his sword and killed it. And so the dwarf's curse began to come true.

"Then Fafnir took all the treasure and carried it far away to a bare, empty heath. He put on a golden coat of armor that was in the treasure, and a terrible helmet called the Helmet of Dread. And he gazed and gazed at the snake ring, until he too turned into a monster, a great dragon. And there he lies on the Glittering Heath to this day, wrapped around the gold, with his eyes always open, so that no one can take it.

"When I came home and found the treasure gone and the dead snake lying there, I was afraid, and I ran away. I came to the land of your family, the Volsungs. I taught people how to plant seeds and harvest grain, and how to make iron, and how to build houses and ships. I taught the girls to spin and weave. I taught people to read the runes and to make poems and songs. But nobody remembered that I had taught them. They said Frey taught them, or Freyja, or Odin. And all that time I longed for the gold, but I was too weak to fight a dragon.

"So I asked the Norns, the wise women who know what will happen. And they said, 'When a prince of the Volsung family comes who is a better smith than you, and Odin gives him a shining horse, then your waiting will be over.'"

Regin stopped. For a long time neither of them spoke.

"I know what you want," said Siegfried at last. "You think I am that prince. You want me to fight the dragon Fafnir and win the dwarf's treasure for you."

"Yes," said Regin.

"But the treasure has a curse on it," said Siegfried.

"Let the curse be on me," said Regin. "I am so wise, I am sure I can escape it. Will you help me? Will you rid the world of a terrible monster?"

"Tomorrow," said Siegfried, "be ready to come with me to the Glittering Heath. The treasure shall be yours, and the curse too."

"And the curse too," said Regin.
