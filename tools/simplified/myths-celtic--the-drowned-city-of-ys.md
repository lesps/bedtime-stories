---
title: The Drowned City of Ys
basedOn: the Breton legend of the city of Ys, first told in full by Albert Le Grand in his Lives of the Saints of Brittany (1680) and retold many times since
workId: breton-ys
order: 7
---

Long ago, in Brittany, in the far west of France, there was a king called Gradlon. He was a good king, who said his prayers and ruled his people well. And he had a daughter called Dahut, whom he loved more than anything in the world.

Dahut loved the sea. So, to please her, King Gradlon built her a city right beside the sea, on land that was lower than the water. The city was called Ys, and it was the most beautiful city in the whole world, with towers and palaces and churches, and their bells rang out across the bay.

To keep the sea out, a great wall was built all round the city, with a gate in it. When the tide was low, the gate was opened, so that ships could sail in and out. And when the tide came in, the gate was shut, to keep the sea out. There was only one key to the gate, and the king kept it himself, on a chain around his neck. He never took it off, not even when he slept.

The people of Ys grew rich, and they grew proud. And the princess Dahut was the proudest and wildest of them all. She cared for nothing but feasting and dancing and having her own way. A holy man, Saint Gwénolé, came to warn the king. "This city has forgotten God," he said. "If the people do not change their ways, it will be punished."

One day a handsome stranger came to Ys, dressed all in red. He flattered Dahut, and told her how beautiful she was, and she fell in love with him. And one night, he whispered to her, "If you really love me, bring me the key to the sea gate."

So that night, while her father was asleep, Dahut crept into his room, and gently slipped the chain from around his neck, and took the key. Some say that the stranger was really the Devil himself, come to destroy the city. And when he had the key, he went to the great gate and opened it. And the tide was coming in.

The sea poured in through the gate. It rushed through the streets, higher and higher, and swept over the houses and the palaces.

Saint Gwénolé ran to wake the king. "Get up, King Gradlon!" he cried. "The sea is coming! Get up and ride away, or you will be lost!"

The king jumped up, and ran out to his horse. He pulled Dahut up behind him, and they galloped away, with the waves roaring at their heels. But the sea came after them, faster and faster, and the waves were catching up.

Then a voice rang out over the waves. "King Gradlon! If you want to live, let go of what you are carrying behind you!"

The king held on to his daughter. But Dahut's hands slipped, and she fell from the horse, down into the waves. And at once the sea stopped rising, and fell back, and the king's horse carried him safely to dry land.

King Gradlon looked back. Where the beautiful city of Ys had stood, there was nothing but the grey sea. Ys was gone, under the water, and it has stayed there ever since, in the Bay of Douarnenez.

The king went sadly away to the town of Quimper, and there he lived for the rest of his life, and he made it his new capital.

And Dahut? She did not drown. She became a mermaid, a morgen, as the Bretons call them. Fishermen say they have seen her sitting on the rocks, combing her long golden hair and singing sad songs.

And what about Paris, the great city on the river Seine? Some Bretons say that its name comes from "Par-Is", which means "like Ys". And they say that one day, when Paris sinks, Ys will rise up again out of the sea.

Until then, it lies under the waves. But on quiet days, at low tide, the fishermen of Douarnenez Bay say that you can hear the bells of the churches of Ys, still ringing under the sea.
