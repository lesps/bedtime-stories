So Joseph came to Egypt, and Potiphar, the captain of Pharaoh's guard, bought him from the traders.

The Lord was with Joseph, and everything he did went well. Potiphar saw that the Lord was with him, and that the Lord made everything Joseph did succeed. So Potiphar put Joseph in charge of his whole house, and of everything he owned. And from then on, the Lord blessed Potiphar's house because of Joseph.

Now Joseph was strong and handsome, and Potiphar's wife wanted Joseph for herself. But Joseph said no. "My master trusts me with everything he has," he said. "How could I do such a wicked thing, and sin against God?" She asked him again and again, day after day, but he would not listen to her.

One day, when none of the other servants were in the house, she grabbed hold of his cloak. Joseph pulled away and ran outside, leaving his cloak in her hand. Then she told lies about him. She called the servants and said that Joseph had attacked her, and when her husband came home, she showed him the cloak and told him the same lies.

Potiphar was furious. He took Joseph and put him in the prison, where the king's prisoners were kept.

But the Lord was with Joseph even in the prison, and was kind to him. The keeper of the prison liked Joseph, and put him in charge of all the other prisoners. Whatever Joseph did, the Lord made it go well.

Some time later, Pharaoh grew angry with two of his officers, his chief butler, who served his wine, and his chief baker. He put them in the same prison as Joseph, and Joseph looked after them.

One night they both had dreams. In the morning Joseph found them looking sad. "Why do you look so sad today?" he asked.

"We have had dreams," they said, "and there is no one to tell us what they mean."

"Don't meanings belong to God?" said Joseph. "Tell me your dreams."

So the chief butler told his dream. "In my dream there was a vine in front of me, with three branches. As soon as it budded, it blossomed, and its clusters became ripe grapes. Pharaoh's cup was in my hand, and I took the grapes and squeezed them into Pharaoh's cup, and gave the cup to Pharaoh."

"This is what it means," said Joseph. "The three branches are three days. In three days Pharaoh will lift up your head, and give you back your job, and you will hand Pharaoh his cup as you used to. But when all is well with you, remember me, and be kind to me. Tell Pharaoh about me, and get me out of this place. For I was stolen away from the land of the Hebrews, and here too I have done nothing to deserve this dungeon."

When the chief baker heard that the meaning was good, he told his dream too. "In my dream there were three baskets of white bread on my head. In the top basket there were all kinds of baked things for Pharaoh, and the birds were eating them out of the basket on my head."

"This is what it means," said Joseph. "The three baskets are three days. In three days Pharaoh will lift up your head too, but not in a good way. Pharaoh will put you to death."

Three days later it was Pharaoh's birthday, and he gave a feast for all his servants. He gave the chief butler back his job, and the butler handed Pharaoh his cup. But the chief baker was put to death, just as Joseph had said.

But the chief butler did not remember Joseph. He forgot all about him.

Two whole years later, Pharaoh had a dream. He was standing by the river, and out of the river came seven cows, fine and fat, and they grazed among the reeds. Then seven more cows came up out of the river after them, ugly and thin, and the thin cows ate up the fat cows! Then Pharaoh woke up.

He fell asleep again, and had a second dream. Seven ears of grain grew on one stalk, full and good. Then seven more ears sprang up after them, thin and dried up by the east wind, and the thin ears swallowed up the full ones! Then Pharaoh woke up, and knew it had been a dream.

In the morning, Pharaoh was troubled. He sent for all the magicians and wise men of Egypt, and told them his dreams. But no one could tell him what they meant.

Then the chief butler spoke up. "Today I remember my mistake," he said. "When Pharaoh was angry with me and the chief baker, and put us in prison, we both had dreams. There was a young Hebrew there with us, a servant of the captain of the guard. We told him our dreams, and he told us what they meant. And everything happened exactly as he said."

So Pharaoh sent for Joseph, and they brought him quickly out of the dungeon. He shaved, and changed his clothes, and came before Pharaoh.

"I have had a dream," said Pharaoh, "and no one can tell me what it means. But I have heard that when you hear a dream, you can explain it."

"It is not in me," said Joseph. "God will give Pharaoh an answer of peace."

So Pharaoh told Joseph his dreams.

"Pharaoh's two dreams are really one," said Joseph. "God has shown Pharaoh what He is about to do. The seven good cows are seven years, and the seven good ears of grain are seven years. The seven thin, ugly cows, and the seven empty ears dried by the east wind, are seven years of famine.

"Seven years of great plenty are coming to all the land of Egypt. But after them will come seven years of famine, when nothing will grow. The famine will be so terrible that all the plenty will be forgotten. And the dream came to Pharaoh twice because God has decided it, and He will do it soon.

"So now let Pharaoh find a wise and sensible man, and put him in charge of the land of Egypt. Let him gather a fifth of all the food in the seven good years, and store up grain in the cities. Then there will be food for the land in the seven years of famine, and the land will not die of hunger."

Pharaoh and all his servants liked this plan. "Can we find anyone like this man," said Pharaoh, "a man with the spirit of God in him?"

And Pharaoh said to Joseph, "Since God has shown you all this, there is no one as wise and sensible as you. You shall be in charge of my house, and all my people shall do as you say. Only on my throne will I be greater than you. See, I put you in charge of the whole land of Egypt."

Then Pharaoh took his ring from his own finger and put it on Joseph's finger. He dressed him in clothes of fine linen, and put a gold chain around his neck. He had Joseph ride in his second chariot, and people called out before him, "Abrech!" Pharaoh gave Joseph an Egyptian name, Zaphenath-paneah, and he gave him a wife, Asenath. Joseph was thirty years old when he stood before Pharaoh, king of Egypt.

In the seven years of plenty, the land grew grain in heaps. Joseph gathered up the food, and stored it in the cities. He stored so much grain that it was like the sand of the sea, so much that they stopped counting it.

Before the years of famine came, Joseph and Asenath had two sons. Joseph named the first Manasseh, "for God has made me forget all my troubles, and all my father's house." And he named the second Ephraim, "for God has made me fruitful in the land where I suffered."

Then the seven years of plenty came to an end, and the seven years of famine began, just as Joseph had said. There was famine in every land, but in Egypt there was bread. When the Egyptians were hungry, they cried to Pharaoh for bread, and Pharaoh told them, "Go to Joseph, and do what he says." So Joseph opened the storehouses and sold grain to the Egyptians. And people came from every country to Egypt, to buy grain from Joseph, because the famine was terrible all over the earth.
