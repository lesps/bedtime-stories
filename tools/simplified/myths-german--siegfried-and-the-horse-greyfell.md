Siegfried was happy in Mimer's smithy. Mimer taught him everything he knew: how to carve magic runes, how to speak the languages of faraway lands, and how to play sweet music on the harp. Siegfried grew wise, and he grew very strong.

But the other twelve apprentices grew more and more jealous of him. One day, while Mimer was away, Veliant, the head apprentice, hit him. Siegfried raised his heavy hammer high. Veliant shook with fear. But Siegfried brought the hammer down on Veliant's anvil instead, and the great iron block broke into a thousand pieces.

"What if I hit you like that?" said Siegfried.

Veliant fell on his knees and begged for mercy.

"You are safe," said Siegfried, walking away. "I would never hurt someone like you."

But the apprentices met in secret and made a plan to get rid of him. The next morning Veliant came to Siegfried with a false smile on his face.

"Siegfried," he said, "we have almost no charcoal left for our fires. Someone must drive to the forest and fetch some from Regin, the great charcoal-burner. Would you like to go? It's a pleasant trip on a summer day."

Siegfried could tell that Veliant was planning something. But he was not afraid. So he hitched the donkey to the cart and set off.

At first the road ran through sunny meadows where people were bringing in the harvest. But in the afternoon it went into the forest, where it was dark and damp and silent. Grey wolves slunk across the road, and owls blinked down from the trees. Night came, and the forest was full of shrieks and howls. Bats flapped around Siegfried's head, and he thought he saw goblins peeping out from behind the trees. But Siegfried looked up at the stars shining through the branches, and he kept going bravely.

At last the moon came up, and the noises died away, and he saw a red glow ahead. It was the charcoal-burners' fires! The men led him to a little hut, where someone inside was playing strange, wild music on a harp.

Inside, the hut was full of treasure. Jewels sparkled on the ceiling, and heaps of gold and silver lay on the floor. In the middle stood Regin, a little old man, wrinkled and grey, with sharp, wise eyes. He welcomed Siegfried, gave him a good supper, and played him to sleep on his harp.

That night Siegfried dreamed that he stood on a high mountain, with the whole beautiful world spread out below him. Then he saw the three Norns, who spin and weave the life of every person. Two of them wove a web of purple and gold, the web of Siegfried's life. But as it grew beautiful and strong, the third Norn tore it into pieces. The mountain shook, and Siegfried woke up.

In the morning he asked Regin for the charcoal. But Regin smiled a strange smile.

"Does Prince Siegfried come for charcoal, driving a donkey in a dirty coal cart?" he said. "Your family were the greatest kings on earth!"

Siegfried was angry. But he answered politely. "Mimer is my master, and my father taught me that even princes must obey their masters."

"And how long will you be Mimer's servant?" asked Regin.

"When Mimer lets me go, and Odin calls me," said Siegfried, "then I will go out into the world, like my family long ago, and do great deeds."

Regin said nothing. He picked up his harp and played, and the music was so magical that Siegfried forgot everything else. When it ended, Regin had changed. Now he had a thick beard and long black hair, and he was taller than before.

"Don't be afraid, my boy," he said. "Regin and Mimer are the same person. I am your master, in disguise. The day has come for you to go out into the world, and I give you leave."

Siegfried was so surprised that he could not speak.

"Go out in your own strength, with Odin's help," said his master. "Put right what is wrong. Help the weak. And do not come home until the whole world knows about your good deeds. Put on these fine clothes, take your sword Balmung, and go north. By the sea you will find Gripir, the last of the giants. Ask him for a horse."

So Siegfried set off. He crossed the great forest, the bare moors and the black mountains, until he came to wild land beside the roaring sea. There, in green valleys between snowy mountains, herds of cattle, deer and wild horses were grazing. These were the fields of Gripir.

High on the mountain stood Gripir's house, made of enormous boulders, with eagles nesting around it. Siegfried climbed up and walked straight in. On a throne carved from walrus tusks sat Gripir, an old giant with a white beard that reached almost to the floor.

"Welcome, Siegfried!" he said kindly. "I know who you are, and I know what the Norns have planned for you. Come and sit beside me."

They talked all night long, the strong young man and the very old giant. In the morning Gripir took Siegfried to the top of the highest mountain. Siegfried looked north and saw the dark mountains of Norway. He looked east and saw a forest that went on forever. He looked south and saw the river Rhine winding like a great white snake, and his father's little kingdom, like a green speck by the sea. And he looked west and saw nothing but the rolling ocean, and far away, a green island half hidden in the mist.

"The world is so wide," said Siegfried, "and a person's life is so short!"

"The whole world is in front of you," said Gripir. "Go down to my fields and choose a horse."

Siegfried ran down the mountain to where the horses were grazing. But they were all so strong and beautiful that he could not choose. Then a tall stranger was standing beside him. He had one bright eye, a sky-blue hood covered in golden stars, and an ash-grey cloak.

"There is one horse here better than all the rest," said the stranger. "They say he came from Odin's own fields. Drive the horses into the river, and you will see which one he is."

So they drove the herd into the rushing river. The current was strong. Some horses turned back, and others were swept away. Only one swam all the way across, and then swam back again, and climbed out and stood beside Siegfried.

"There is your horse," said the stranger. "His name is Greyfell."

His mane shone like the rays of the sun, and his coat was as white as fresh snow. Siegfried turned to thank the stranger, but he was gone. Then Siegfried knew that he had been talking to Odin.

He jumped onto Greyfell's back. "Where shall I go?" he called to Gripir.

"Ride back to Regin, your master," said Gripir. "He will tell you about a wrong that must be put right."

So Siegfried rode away south on the shining horse Greyfell.
