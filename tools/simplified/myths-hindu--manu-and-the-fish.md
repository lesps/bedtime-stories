namesNote: From the Mahabharata. In this telling the fish is Brahma, the creator; in later tellings, such as the Matsya Purana, the fish is Vishnu, in his avatar Matsya. The seven Rishis are seven great sages.

Long ago there lived a great sage called Manu, the son of the Sun, and he was as glorious as Brahma himself. In a forest called Visala he stood on one leg, with his hand raised, and prayed and fasted for ten thousand years.

One day, while he stood there beside the river Chirini, with his clothes wet and his hair all tangled, a little fish swam up to the bank and spoke to him. "Good sir," it said, "I am a little fish, and I am afraid of the big ones. Among us it is always the rule that the strong fish eat the weak. Please save me, and one day I will repay you."

Manu was filled with pity, and he lifted the fish out of the water with his own hands. Its body shone like moonlight. He put it in an earthen pot full of water, and looked after it like his own child, and it grew. After a long time it grew so big that there was no room for it in the pot. "Good sir," it said, "please find me a better home."

So Manu carried it to a huge pond, miles long and miles wide, and there it grew for many years, until even the pond was too small for it to play in. "Father," it said, "take me to the river Ganga, the beloved of the Ocean." So Manu carried it to the Ganga, and put it into the river with his own hands. There it grew again, until it said, "I am too big to move about in the Ganga. Please take me quickly to the sea!" So Manu carried it to the sea. And though the fish was so huge, Manu carried it easily, and it was pleasant to touch and to smell.

When Manu put it into the sea, the fish smiled and said, "You have looked after me so carefully. Now listen to what you must do. The end of the world is near. Everything that moves and everything that does not move will soon be destroyed. Build a strong ship, and give it a long rope. Get into it with the seven Rishis, and take with you all the kinds of seeds that the wise men named long ago, and keep them safe. Then wait for me. I will come to you with horns on my head, and that is how you will know me. Without my help, you cannot escape the terrible flood."

"I believe you," said Manu, "and I will do as you say."

So Manu gathered all the seeds, and set sail on the stormy sea in a fine ship. Then he thought of the fish, and the fish knew his thoughts and came, with horns on its head. When Manu saw the horned fish rising out of the sea like a rock, he threw the rope over its horns. And the fish pulled the ship with great strength across the salty sea. The ship was tossed by the storm and rocked from side to side, and no land could be seen, nor north nor south nor east nor west. There was water everywhere; the water covered even the sky. In all the flooded world nothing could be seen but Manu, the seven Rishis and the fish.

For many long years the fish pulled the ship through the flood, until it came to the highest peak of the Himalayas. Then the fish told them to tie the ship to the peak, and they did. That peak is still called Naubandhana, which means "the harbour". Then the fish said to the Rishis, "I am Brahma, the Lord of all creatures; there is no one greater than me. I took the shape of a fish to save you from the flood. Now Manu will make all creatures again: gods and demons and people, everything that moves and everything that does not."

Then the fish vanished. Manu wanted to make the world again, but at first he was confused and could not do it. So he prayed and fasted for a long time, and then he made all creatures again, each in its proper order.
