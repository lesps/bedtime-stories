title: The Banyan Deer
adaptedFrom: cowell-jataka--the-banyan-deer

namesNote: The Jātaka tells of the Buddha's earlier lives, before he became the Buddha. Benares is the old name of Varanasi, on the river Ganges.

Long ago, when Brahmadatta was king of Benares, the Buddha-to-be was born as a deer. He was golden all over. His eyes shone like jewels, his horns gleamed like silver, and his hooves looked as if they had been polished. He was the king of five hundred deer, and he was called the King of the Banyan Deer. Not far away lived another golden deer, called Branch, who was the king of five hundred deer too.

In those days the king of Benares loved hunting. He had to have meat at every meal, and every day he made all his people stop their work and go hunting with him. At last the people said to one another, "This king of ours stops all our work. Let us drive some deer into his park, and then he can hunt them there and leave us alone."

So they planted grass in the king's park for the deer to eat, and put out water for them to drink, and opened the gate wide. Then they went out into the forest with sticks, and made a great ring around it. They beat the bushes and the ground, and banged their swords and spears together, and made such a din that all the deer ran out of the forest and into the park, Banyan's herd and Branch's herd together. Then the people shut the gate. "Now, sire," they said to the king, "there are deer enough in your park. You can hunt them there."

The king went to look at the deer, and among them he saw the two golden deer. "Those two shall never be hunted," he said.

After that, every day, the king or his cook went into the park and shot a deer for the king's dinner. Whenever the deer saw the bow, they ran for their lives, and many of them were hurt before one was caught. So the deer went to the King of the Banyan Deer and told him.

The Banyan Deer sent for Branch. "Friend," he said, "so many of our deer are being hurt. They cannot escape the cook, but at least they need not be wounded. Let us take turns: one day one deer from my herd, and the next day one from yours. The deer whose turn it is will go to the cook all by itself, and the others will be left in peace."

Branch agreed, and so it was done. Every day one deer went to the cook, and no other deer was hurt.

Then one day the turn fell on a mother deer from Branch's herd who was soon going to have a baby. She went to Branch. "Lord," she said, "I am going to have a baby. When my little one is born, we can both take our turns. Please let my turn pass by me this time."

"No," said Branch. "I cannot give your turn to someone else. You must take what comes. Go away!"

So the mother deer went to the King of the Banyan Deer, and told him her story. "Very well," he said. "Go home. I will see that your turn passes you by." And he went to the cook's place himself, and lay down there.

When the cook saw him, he cried out, "Why, here's the king of the deer, whom the king promised never to hunt! What does this mean?" And he ran off to tell the king.

The king came at once in his chariot. "My friend, king of the deer," he said, "did I not promise you your life? Why are you lying here?"

"Sire," said the Banyan Deer, "a mother deer came to me who is soon to have a baby, and she begged me to let her turn pass to another. I could not give her turn to someone else, so I have come to take it myself. That is all."

"Golden king of the deer," said the king, "I have never seen such kindness and love, not even among men. Get up! I spare your life, and hers too."

"Two of us will be safe," said the Banyan Deer. "But what about all the others, O king?"

"I spare their lives too."

"Then the deer in your park will be safe. But what about all the other deer?"

"I spare their lives too."

"Then the deer will be safe. But what about all the other animals with four feet?"

"I spare them too."

"Then the four-footed animals will be safe. But what about the birds?"

"I spare them too."

"Then the birds will be safe. But what about the fishes in the water?"

"I spare the fishes too," said the king.

So the King of the Banyan Deer won safety for every living creature. Then he stood up, and taught the king how to live a good life. "Do what is right, great king," he said. "Be good and fair to your mother and father, your children, and all your people." He stayed in the park for a few days to teach the king, and then he led his herd back into the forest.

Soon the mother deer had her baby, a little fawn as lovely as a lotus bud. The fawn liked to play with Branch's herd. But his mother said, "My child, don't go about with Branch's herd. Stay with the Banyan deer. It is better to live a short life with Banyan than a long life with Branch."

Now that the deer were safe, they began to eat the farmers' crops. The farmers did not dare to chase them away, because the king had promised to protect them. So they went to the king and complained. "When the Banyan Deer won my favour, I made him a promise," said the king. "I would rather lose my kingdom than break my word. No one in my kingdom may harm the deer."

But when the King of the Banyan Deer heard about it, he called his herd together. "From now on," he said, "you must not eat other people's crops." And he sent a message to the farmers: "You need not build fences around your fields any more. Just tie leaves around the edges, to mark them." From then on, the farmers marked their fields with leaves, and no deer ever stepped into a field that was marked that way.

So the King of the Banyan Deer taught his herd all his life long, and the king of Benares followed his teaching too.

Long afterwards, the Buddha told this story, and he said, "In those days, I myself was the King of the Banyan Deer."
