Kalev, the old king of Estonia, had decided that only one of his sons should rule after him. So one morning, before sunrise, his three youngest sons set out to the south, to decide which of them it would be. The youngest of the three was Kalevipoeg, which means the son of Kalev.

On the third day they came to a little lake with steep banks. Water birds were playing on it, and on the far side, the holy forest of the god Taara was shining in the sunset.

"This is the place where we will decide," said the eldest brother. Each of them chose a big stone. Whoever could throw his stone right across the lake to the solid ground on the other side would be their father's heir. And the other two would go out into the world to seek their fortunes.

The eldest brother threw first. His stone flew from his hand as fast as a bird. But suddenly it changed direction and splashed down into the middle of the lake, and the water covered it up.

Then the second brother threw his stone. It whistled through the air like an arrow, and went up so high it almost disappeared. But then it came down on the far shore, right at the water's edge, and sank halfway into the mud.

Then it was Kalevipoeg's turn. Although he was the youngest, he was much taller and stronger than his brothers. He thought sadly for a moment about his father, who had died before he was born. Then he threw his stone. It didn't go up as high as his brother's, but it flew far, and then it skipped and bounced right across the lake, and landed on the solid ground beyond.

"Let's go and look for the stones," said the eldest brother. They waded straight across the lake, and in the deepest part the water only came up a little above their knees. The eldest brother's stone had disappeared under the water. The second brother's stone was half sunk in the mud at the edge. But Kalevipoeg's stone was lying on the grass, far beyond the lake.

"The gods have chosen our youngest brother to be king," said the eldest. Then the two older brothers bathed Kalevipoeg and dressed him as a king. And they said a loving goodbye to him, and went cheerfully off into the world.

But Kalevipoeg sat on a rock for a long time, feeling sad that his young days were over, and that now he would have to manage all on his own. At last he threw a silver coin into the water, as a gift to the gods.

The new king had to plough the land and defend it too. So he set to work with his sword at his side, ploughing from early morning until late at night, and planting the country with corn and grass and trees and berries.

One hot day at noon, his white horse was so tired that Kalevipoeg unharnessed him from the plough, tied his front legs loosely together so he could not wander too far, and let him graze. Then Kalevipoeg lay down in the grass and fell asleep. He was so big that his head rested on top of a hill, and his body and legs stretched far out over the plain below. The sweat ran from his forehead and sank into the ground, and a spring of water came up there. It is a magic spring. Whoever drinks from it grows strong. Weak children become strong, sick people become well, it heals sore eyes, and girls who drink it have rosy cheeks all their lives.

While Kalevipoeg slept, he dreamed that wolves were attacking his horse. And it was true. The horse had wandered off, and a pack of wild animals, wolves and bears and foxes, came out of the forest. Because his legs were tied, the horse could not run away. He fought bravely with his hooves and his head, but there were too many of them, and at last he fell.

And the land still remembers him. Where he fell, the ground is hollow, and the wolves he fought became little hills. His blood made a red lake, and his bones became hills, his hair became rushes, and his tail became hazel bushes.
