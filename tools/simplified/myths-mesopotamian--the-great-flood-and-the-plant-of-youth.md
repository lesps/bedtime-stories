Gilgamesh looked at Utnapishtim. "You look just like me," he said. "You are no different from me at all. How did you come to join the gods, and live for ever?"

"I will tell you a secret, Gilgamesh," said Utnapishtim, "a secret of the gods.

"Long ago, I lived in the city of Shuruppak, on the bank of the river Euphrates. It was a very old city. And the great gods decided to send a great storm and a flood. But Ea, the wise god, came to the hut of reeds where I was sleeping, and spoke to its walls, so that I would hear.

"'House of reeds, house of reeds! Wall, wall! Listen, man of Shuruppak! Pull down your house and build a ship! Leave your riches and save your life! Take grain of every kind into the ship. Measure it carefully, as wide as it is long, and give it a roof.'

"'I understand, my lord,' I said. 'I will do it. But what shall I tell the people of the city?'

"'Tell them this,' said Ea. 'The god Enlil is angry with me, so I can't live in your city any more. I am going down to the deep sea, to live with my lord Ea.'

"So I built the ship. The weak brought tar, and the strong brought whatever was needed. Its walls were a hundred and twenty cubits high. I divided it into seven parts outside and nine parts inside. I poured tar and pitch all over it to keep the water out. And I fed the workers well. I gave them oxen and sheep to eat, and beer and wine to drink, as if it were water from the river, and we feasted as if it were New Year's Day. And before the sun went down, the ship was finished.

"I loaded into it all my silver and all my gold, and all my grain. I brought my whole family on board, and the animals of the field, and craftsmen of every sort.

"Then Shamash the sun god gave me a sign. 'In the evening, a great rain will fall,' he said. 'Then go into the ship and shut the door.'

"The evening came, and the rain began to fall. When I saw the storm coming, I was terrified. I went into the ship and shut the door. And I gave the great ship into the hands of our sailor, Puzur-Amurri.

"At dawn, a black cloud rose up from the edge of the sky. The god Adad thundered inside it. Other gods marched over the hills and plains ahead of the storm, and the gods of the earth held up their flaming torches, until the whole land glowed. Then every light was turned to darkness.

"All day long the flood came down. The water rushed up to the mountains. It swept over the people like a battle. Brother could not see brother.

"Even the gods were frightened of the flood. They fled up to the heaven of Anu, and they crouched like dogs against the wall. The goddess Ishtar cried out in sorrow. 'Did I give birth to my people,' she said, 'just for them to fill the sea like little fishes?' And the other gods wept with her.

"For six days and six nights the storm raged. On the seventh day the flood grew calm, and the sea went down, and the wind and the rain stopped.

"I opened the air-hole, and the light fell on my face. I looked out over the sea. Everything was still. All the people were gone, and the land was as flat as a roof. I sat down and wept, and the tears ran down my cheeks. Everywhere I looked there was only sea.

"After twelve days, a piece of land appeared, and the ship came to rest on the mountain of Nisir. The mountain held the ship fast, and it could not move. One day, two days, three days, four days, five days, six days, the mountain held it fast.

"On the seventh day I brought out a dove and let her go. The dove flew away, but she came back, because there was nowhere to land. Then I brought out a swallow and let her go. The swallow flew away, but she came back, because there was nowhere to land. Then I brought out a raven and let her go. The raven flew away, and she saw that the water was going down. She ate, and she pecked at the ground, and she croaked, and she did not come back.

"Then I let everything out of the ship, to the four winds. I made an offering to the gods on top of the mountain. I burned reeds and cedarwood and myrtle, and the gods smelled the sweet smell, and they gathered round.

"Ishtar came, and she held up the necklace of blue jewels that Anu had made for her. 'O gods,' she said, 'as surely as I will never forget the jewels round my neck, I will never forget these days! Let the gods come to the offering. But don't let Enlil come, because he would not listen, and he made the flood, and he destroyed my people.'

"Then Enlil came, and he saw the ship, and he was very angry. 'How did any living being escape?' he shouted. 'No man was meant to live through this!'

"Then the god Ninurta spoke. 'Who but Ea could have made this plan? Ea knows everything.'

"And Ea spoke to Enlil. 'Great lord,' he said, 'how could you send a flood, without listening to anyone? If someone does wrong, punish the one who did wrong. But be merciful. Don't destroy everyone. Instead of a flood, it would have been better to send a famine. And I did not tell anyone the secret of the gods. I only sent a dream to Atrahasis, and so he heard the secret.' And Atrahasis was me.

"Then Ea came up into the ship. He took me by the hand and led me out, and my wife with me. He made us kneel side by side, facing each other, and he stood between us and blessed us. 'Before now, Utnapishtim was only a man,' he said. 'But now Utnapishtim and his wife shall be like the gods. They shall live far away, at the mouth of the rivers.' And so they brought me here, far away, to live at the mouth of the rivers."

When Utnapishtim had finished his story, he said, "But you, Gilgamesh, which of the gods will give you the life you are looking for? Come now, see if you can stay awake for six days and seven nights."

But as soon as Gilgamesh sat down, he grew drowsy, and he fell fast asleep.

Utnapishtim smiled, and pointed him out to his wife. But she felt sorry for the tired man. "Help him to go home," she said.

"Bake him some bread," said Utnapishtim. So every day for six days she baked a loaf and laid it beside Gilgamesh where he lay sleeping on the ship. And on the seventh day, Utnapishtim touched him, and Gilgamesh woke up with a start. "I fell asleep," he said. "Sleep was too strong for me."

Gilgamesh was still afraid of death. "What shall I do?" he asked. "Where shall I go?"

Utnapishtim told him to go home with Urshanabi the boatman. But before Gilgamesh left, Utnapishtim told him about a plant that grew at the bottom of the sea, and Gilgamesh believed that it would let him live for ever.

So Gilgamesh tied heavy stones to his feet, and let himself down into the sea through a hole in the floor of the boat. When he reached the bottom, he found the plant and picked it, and came back up into the boat.

"Look, Urshanabi!" he said. "This is a wonderful plant. Its name is The Old Man Grows Young Again. I will eat it and be young again. And I will take it home with me to Uruk."

But the plant never reached Uruk. On the way home, Gilgamesh and Urshanabi came to a pool of cold water, and Gilgamesh dived in to bathe. And while he was in the water, a snake smelled the plant, and found it, and swallowed it.

When Gilgamesh saw what had happened, he cried out, and he sat down and wept, and the tears ran down his cheeks. All his hard work had been for nothing.

Tired and sad, he went on with his friend Urshanabi, and at last they came to the great walls of Uruk. Then Gilgamesh said, "Urshanabi, climb up on the wall. Look at the bricks, from the bottom to the very top, and see whether the work I planned has been done while I was away."
