On the island of Cyprus there lived a sculptor named Pygmalion. He lived alone, and he spent all his days carving beautiful things.

One day he took a big block of white ivory and began to carve a statue of a young woman. He worked on it for days and days. He carved her face, her hair, her hands. He was so skillful that when he finished, she looked almost alive. She looked as if she might take a breath, or blink, or step down at any moment.

Pygmalion loved the statue. He stroked its hand, as if to check whether it was warm. He brought it presents: shiny shells, smooth pebbles, little birds and bright flowers. He dressed it in a fine robe and put rings on its fingers. He gave it a soft pillow to lean on. He talked to it every day. But of course it was only ivory, and it never answered.

Every year, the people of Cyprus held a great festival for Aphrodite, the goddess of love. When the day came, Pygmalion went to her temple with everyone else. He stood before the altar, and he prayed in a quiet, shy voice.

"Goddess," he said, "please give me a wife who is just like my ivory statue."

Aphrodite heard him. She knew what he really meant. And as a sign that she would help, the flame on her altar leaped up into the air, once, twice, three times.

Pygmalion hurried home. He went to his statue and took its hand. It felt warm. He touched its cheek, and the hard ivory felt soft. He could feel a heartbeat. The statue's cheeks turned pink. Her eyes opened, and she looked at him and smiled.

The statue had come to life.

Pygmalion thanked Aphrodite with all his heart. He and the young woman were married, and Aphrodite herself came to the wedding. Later they had a son named Paphos, and a city on Cyprus was named after him.
