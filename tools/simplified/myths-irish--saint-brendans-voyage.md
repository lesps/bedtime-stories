Saint Brendan was a real person. He was an Irish monk who lived about fifteen hundred years ago, and he started many monasteries, and Christians still honour him today. He was also a great sailor. And long after he died, people told this wonderful story about his voyage across the ocean.

One evening, a holy man called Barrind came to visit Brendan at his monastery. He told Brendan about a voyage he had made far out into the western ocean. "We sailed through a thick fog," he said, "and when we came out of it, we were in a land full of light. There were flowers everywhere, and every tree was heavy with fruit. It never grew dark there. A young man met us there and told us it was the Promised Land of the Saints. We thought we had been there for fifteen days. But when we came home, we found that we had been away for a whole year."

After Barrind had gone, Brendan chose fourteen of his monks. "I want to go and look for the Promised Land of the Saints," he told them. "Will you come with me?"

"Father," they said, "we will go wherever you go."

So they built a boat. They made a frame of wood, and covered it with ox hides, and rubbed the joins with fat to keep out the water. They put a mast and a sail in it, and food for forty days. And they set off across the ocean, towards the setting sun.

Their first great adventure came at Easter time. They came to an island where there were flocks of white sheep, every one of them bigger than an ox. A kind man lived there, who brought them bread and told them where to go next. "Spend Easter Sunday on that little island over there," he said.

The island he pointed to was bare and black, with no grass or trees on it at all, and no sand on the shore. The monks landed and pulled their boat up. Brendan stayed in the boat to pray, but the monks lit a fire and put a pot on it to cook their dinner. Then, just as the pot began to boil, the whole island began to move!

The monks ran for the boat, and Brendan pulled them in. And as they sailed away, they could still see their fire burning, two miles off, moving away across the sea.

"Don't be afraid," said Brendan. "God showed me in the night what this is. It isn't an island at all. It is a fish, the biggest fish in all the ocean. Its name is Jasconius, and it is always trying to put its tail into its mouth, but it is too long."

Next they came to an island where there was a great tree, so covered in white birds that you could hardly see its leaves and branches. When the monks began to sing their evening prayers, all the birds sang with them, beating their wings, in voices as sweet as music. The monks stayed on that island from Easter until the summer, and the birds sang the prayers with them every day and every night.

At Christmas they came to an island where a community of old monks lived, who never spoke a word, except to sing their prayers. In their church there were lamps that lit themselves at the hour of prayer, and burned all night, and yet never burned down.

And so they sailed on. One day they saw a great pillar standing up in the middle of the sea. It was made of clear crystal, and it was so tall that its top was hidden in the sky. It was covered with a sort of net, with holes so big that their boat could sail through them. The sun shone through the crystal, and the water all around it was as clear as glass. They sailed all around it, and through it, and gave thanks to God for its beauty.

Another day they came near a rocky island covered in forges, full of smoke, with the sound of bellows blowing and hammers banging on anvils, like thunder. "Let's get away from here," said Brendan. But one of the islanders came running down to the shore and threw a great lump of burning slag at them. It flew over their heads and fell into the sea behind them, and the sea boiled and smoked where it fell. And as they sailed away, they looked back, and the whole island seemed to be on fire.

On a tiny rocky island they met an old hermit called Paul, covered all over in his own long white hair, which was all he wore. "I have lived here for many years," he told them. "For thirty years an otter brought me a fish every three days, and a bundle of sticks to make a fire. Now God has given me a spring of water, and I need nothing else."

For seven years Brendan and his monks sailed the ocean. Every year they came back to the same places at the same times: they spent Easter on the back of the great fish Jasconius, and the summer with the singing birds, and Christmas with the silent monks.

And at the end of seven years, the kind man from the island of sheep came with them to guide them. "Now," he said, "you will find what you have been looking for."

They sailed into a thick fog, so thick they could hardly see one another. They sailed in it for an hour. And then, suddenly, a great light shone all around them, and the boat came to rest on a shore.

It was a land of wonderful light. Every tree was heavy with fruit, as if it were always autumn. For forty days they walked through it, and it never grew dark, and they never came to the end of it. At last they came to a great river, and there a young man came to meet them. He hugged each of them, and called each one by his name.

"This is the land you have been looking for for seven years," he said to Brendan. "God made you wait so long so that you could see all the wonders of His great ocean first. Now take some of the fruit and the precious stones of this land, and go home. You will not live much longer, Brendan. But one day, many years from now, this land will be shown to the people who come after you."

So Brendan and his monks filled their boat with fruit and precious stones, and said goodbye to the young man, and sailed back through the fog. And they came home safely to Ireland, and told the other monks everything they had seen. And not long afterwards, Brendan died, in peace.

Saint Brendan is still honoured in Ireland, and his feast day is the sixteenth of May. Some people say that the land he found was America. Others say it was heaven. Nobody knows for sure.
