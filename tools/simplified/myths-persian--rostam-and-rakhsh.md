title: Rostam and Rakhsh
adaptedFrom: zimmern-kings--rustem-and-rakush

namesNote: Zimmern spells their names Rustem and Rakush, and Rostam's grandfather's Saum. Rakhsh means "lightning". Turan was the land to the north-east of Iran, and Afrasiyab its king.

When Zal was grown up, he married a princess named Rudabeh. Before their son was born, Rudabeh was very ill, and she could not rest by day or night. Then Zal remembered the Simurgh, who had brought him up, and the feather she had given him to use when he was in need. He threw the feather into the fire, as she had told him. At once there was a sound of rushing wings, the sky grew dark, and the bird of God stood before Zal.

"My son," she said, "why are you so troubled? Why are your eyes wet with tears?"

Zal told her his trouble, and she told him to be cheerful. "Your nurse, who looked after you when your father threw you out, has come to help you once again." And she told him what to do, and flew back to her nest. Zal did as she said, and a fine strong son was born. When Rudabeh saw the baby, she smiled and said, "He shall be called Rostam, which means 'set free', for I am set free from my pain."

The whole land was glad that Zal had a son, and there was feasting everywhere. Messengers rode to Zal's father, Sam, with the good news. They brought him a doll of Rostam sewn from silk, with a little club in its hands, riding on a camel. When Sam saw it, his heart leaped, and he gave thanks to God.

When Rostam was eight years old, Sam heard how tall and strong he was, and he longed to see him. So he travelled to Zabulistan with a great army. Rostam rode out to meet his grandfather on a war elephant, and when he saw Sam, he bowed down and asked for his blessing. And Sam blessed him.

"I am glad to be your grandson," said Rostam. "I don't care for feasts, or sleep, or rest. All I want is to be brave. Give me a horse and a saddle, a coat of armour and a helmet, for I love the bow and arrow. I will defeat your enemies, and may I be as brave as you."

Sam was amazed, and blessed him again, and he could not stop looking at the boy. He stayed for a whole month.

Two years later, Rostam was woken in the night by a terrible roaring that shook the house to its foundations. The king's white elephant had broken its chain in a rage, and everyone was in danger! Rostam jumped out of bed and ran to fight it, but the guards would not let him past. "How could we face the king," they said, "if you were hurt?"

But Rostam would not listen. He pushed his way past them, and smashed the bars of the door with his fists. Outside, all the warriors were afraid of the mad elephant, and Rostam was ashamed of them. With a loud shout he ran at the elephant, and when it raised its trunk to strike him, he hit it on the head with his club, and it fell dead. Then he went back to bed and slept until morning. The news of what he had done spread far and wide, and everyone rejoiced because a hero had been born in Iran.

Years went by, with many wars. When Afrasiyab, the king of Turan, attacked Iran once more, the people turned to Zal for help. But Zal was old now, so he called for his son, who was still very young.

"My son," said Zal, "your lips still smell of milk, and you should be thinking of play. But these are dangerous days, and Iran looks to you. I must send you out to fight against heroes."

"You know, Father, that I would rather go to war than play," said Rostam. "So give me a strong horse, and the mace of my grandfather Sam, and let me go out against the armies of evil."

Zal's heart laughed when he heard such brave words. He ordered all the herds of horses from Zabulistan and Kabul to be brought before his son, so that he could choose his war horse. One by one they were led past Rostam, and he pressed his mighty hand on each one's back, to see whether it could carry him. But every horse trembled under his hand, and sank down on its haunches.

Then, among the herds from Kabul, he saw a mighty mare, and following her was a colt just like its mother, with the chest and shoulders of a lion. It looked as strong as an elephant, and its colour was like rose petals scattered on saffron. Rostam looked the colt over. Then he made a loop in his rope and threw it over the colt, and caught it, though the mare fought hard to protect her foal.

The keeper of the herd came up. "Mighty young man," he said, "I advise you not to take someone else's horse."

"Whose horse is it?" said Rostam. "I can't see any mark on it."

"We don't know who its master is," said the keeper. "But people tell stories about it all over the land, and they call it Rostam's Rakhsh. And I warn you, the mare will never let you ride it. It has been ready for a saddle for three years, but she has never let anyone climb on its back."

When he heard this, Rostam leaped onto the colt with one great bound. The mare ran at him, to pull him down. But when she heard his voice, she let him be. And the rose-coloured horse carried Rostam over the plains like the wind.

When he came back, Rostam said to the keeper, "Tell me, what is the price of this dragon of a horse?"

"If you are Rostam," said the keeper, "ride him, and save Iran from its troubles. His price is the land of Iran, and on his back you will save the world."

So Rostam was overjoyed with Rakhsh, whose name means "lightning", and Zal was glad with him. And they made ready to stand against Afrasiyab.
