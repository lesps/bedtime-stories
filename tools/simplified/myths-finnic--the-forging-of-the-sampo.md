---
title: The Forging of the Sampo

Once, Väinämöinen was lost far away in the cold, dark Northland, which is called Pohjola. The Northland was ruled by an old witch called Louhi. She helped him, and in return she asked him for something.

"Can you make me the Sampo?" she asked. "It is a magic mill, with a lid of many colours. On one side it grinds out flour, on another side salt, and on the third side it makes money. Whoever makes it for me shall marry my beautiful daughter."

"I can't make the Sampo," said Väinämöinen, "but if you help me get home, I will send you my friend Ilmarinen. He is the greatest smith in the world. It was he who made the sky, and he made it so well that you can't see a single hammer mark on it."

So Louhi sent Väinämöinen home in her sledge. On the way he hurt his knee badly with an axe, but a wise old man healed it for him. Then Väinämöinen hurried home to Kalevala.

When he reached the border of his own land, he stopped and sang a magic song. And as he sang, a fir tree began to grow out of the ground. It grew and grew until its top was above the clouds and reached right up to the stars. Then Väinämöinen sang another magic song, and the moon got caught in the tree's branches. And with another song, he caught the stars of the Great Bear in the top of the tree too.

Then he drove on. He was sad, because he had promised to send his friend Ilmarinen to the Northland without asking him first. He came to Ilmarinen's workshop, and went in.

"Ilmarinen," he said, "in the Northland there is a maiden who is the most beautiful girl in the world. Her forehead shines like the rainbow, and her face is as fair as the moonlight. Go and see her! Make the magic Sampo for her mother, and you can marry her."

"Oh, you crafty Väinämöinen," said Ilmarinen. "I know you've promised me to them to save yourself. But I will never go to that gloomy country, not for all the girls in the Northland."

"Then I can tell you about an even greater wonder," said Väinämöinen. "On the border of our own country there is a giant fir tree. Its top is higher than the clouds, and the moon and the Great Bear are shining in its branches."

"I won't believe that until I see it with my own eyes," said Ilmarinen.

"Then come and look," said Väinämöinen.

So they went to the tree, and Väinämöinen asked Ilmarinen to climb up and bring down the moon and stars. And Ilmarinen began to climb. But as he climbed, the tree spoke to him. "Foolish hero," it said, "why are you trying to steal the moon from my branches?" And at that moment Väinämöinen sang a magic song, and called up a great storm wind. "Storm wind," he sang, "carry Ilmarinen away in your boat of clouds, to the dark Northland!"

So the wind heaped up the clouds into a boat, and snatched Ilmarinen out of the tree, and rushed away north with him. On and on he sailed, higher than the moon, until at last the wind set him down in Louhi's courtyard.

Old toothless Louhi saw him land. "Who are you, riding on the storm wind?" she asked. "Have you ever met the great smith Ilmarinen? I have been waiting for him for a long time, to make the Sampo for me."

"I know him very well," he said, "for I am Ilmarinen."

Louhi hurried into the house, and told her youngest daughter to put on her finest clothes. So the maiden put on her loveliest silk dresses, with a copper band round her forehead, a golden belt round her waist, pearls round her neck, and gold and silver threads twisted in her hair. With her rosy cheeks and sparkling eyes, she was lovelier than any other girl in the Northland.

Louhi gave Ilmarinen a great feast, and the best seat at the table. Then she asked him to make the Sampo, and promised him her daughter if he did. And when Ilmarinen saw how beautiful the daughter was, he promised.

But there was nowhere for him to work, not even a pair of bellows to blow his fire. For three days he looked around, until he found a huge rock that was just right. On the first day he built a chimney and lit a fire. On the second day he made his bellows. On the third day he finished his furnace. Then he put some magic metals into it, and Louhi's men worked the bellows for him.

On the first evening, Ilmarinen looked into the furnace and took out a magic bow, shining like the moon. But it was a cruel bow, which wanted to hurt someone every day. So he broke it and threw it back into the fire.

On the second evening he took out a beautiful purple boat. But wherever it went, it made people quarrel and fight. So he broke it and threw it back.

On the third evening he took out a magic cow with golden horns. But she was bad-tempered, and ran off into the forest, and spilled all her milk on the ground. So he threw her back too.

On the fourth evening he took out a wonderful plough, with a golden blade and silver handles. But it ploughed up the fields of barley and the best meadows. So he threw it back too.

Then Ilmarinen sent all the workmen away, and called up the winds with his magic to blow the fire for him. They came from the north and south and east and west, and they blew for three days, until flames leapt out of the windows, sparks flew out of the door, and the smoke rose up to the clouds. And on the third evening, Ilmarinen looked into the furnace and saw the magic Sampo growing there. He took it out and put it on his anvil, and hammered it into shape. On one side it ground out flour, on another side salt, and on the third side money. Its lid was all the colours of the rainbow. And as it rocked to and fro, it ground out one measure to eat, one measure to sell, and one measure to store away.

Old Louhi took the Sampo joyfully, and hid it deep in the hills of the Northland. She fastened it with nine great locks, and with her magic she made three roots grow around it, two deep under the mountains and one under the seashore.

Then Ilmarinen went to Louhi's lovely daughter, and asked if she was ready to be his wife now. But she said, "If I went away with you, all the birds would stop singing. No, I will never give up my freedom."

So Ilmarinen had made the Sampo for nothing. He was sad, and far from home. But Louhi gave him a copper boat, and sent the north wind to carry him home. And on the evening of the third day he reached Kalevala.

Väinämöinen met him there. "Did you make the Sampo?" he asked.

"Yes," said Ilmarinen. "I made the Sampo, with its lid of many colours. Louhi has the wonderful Sampo. But I have lost the beautiful maiden."
