That night Regin played a song on his harp, and it sounded like the winter wind in the dead trees. It was full of sadness.

"Why are you so sad," asked Siegfried, "when you are about to get everything you wished for?"

"Oh, I will do so much with that treasure!" said Regin, and his eyes flashed. "I will turn winter into summer. I will make the deserts bloom. I will make myself a god! And yet I am afraid. The ring has a curse on it. And the Norns have already decided what will happen to me."

"The Norns have woven the life of every person," said Siegfried. "Tomorrow we go to the Glittering Heath, and things will happen as the Norns have said."

So in the morning they set out, Siegfried riding Greyfell and Regin trudging beside him. For seven days they travelled through the thick forest, and at night the wolves howled around them. But no wild animal dared come near, because of the bright light that shone from Greyfell's mane.

Then they came to a dreadful land of black rocks and deep cracks in the ground. Nothing lived there, not even an insect or a blade of grass. It was dry and burning hot, and there was no shade and no water anywhere. But Siegfried kept going. At last they came to a great dark mountain, like a wall.

"Beyond this mountain is the Glittering Heath!" cried Regin.

They climbed it all day. When they reached the top, the moon was rising, round and red. Below them was a cold, white, still river. Beyond it was a bare plain. And far away on the plain was a ring of flickering, glowing lights, and something huge and horrible moving about inside it.

"It is Fafnir!" whispered Regin, and his knees shook. "He is wearing the Helmet of Dread! Shall we go back home, where it is safe?"

"Only cowards give up something they have started," said Siegfried. "Go home if you are afraid. But I am going on. Before the sun sets tomorrow, the deed will be done."

He ran down the mountain to the white river. It was wide and deep, and the air above it smelled of poison. As Siegfried wondered how to cross, a little boat came gliding out of the mist.

"Who are you," called the boatman, "who dares to come to this land of fear?"

"I am Siegfried, and I have come to kill Fafnir."

"Get in, and I will take you across," said the boatman. The boat moved over the water with no oars and no wind. "How will you fight the dragon?" he asked.

"With my sword Balmung."

"But he breathes poison, and his eyes shoot lightning, and nobody is as strong as he is," said the boatman. "Listen to me. On the other side you will find a deep track worn into the ground. That is the path Fafnir takes every morning when he comes down to the river to drink. Dig a deep, narrow pit in that path, and hide in it. When Fafnir crawls over you in the morning, let him feel the edge of your sword."

The boat touched the shore, and Siegfried jumped out. He turned to say thank you, but the boat and the boatman were gone. Only a little white mist floated away over the water. Then Siegfried remembered that the boatman had worn a blue hood covered with golden stars, and that he had only one shining eye. And he knew that he had met Odin again.

Siegfried found the dragon's path, a deep, smooth, slimy groove winding away over the heath. With his sword he dug a deep pit in it. And as the sky began to grow grey in the morning, he climbed into the pit and waited.

He did not have to wait long. Soon he heard the dragon coming, with a noise like many feet tramping and many chains clanking. Siegfried peeped out. There was Fafnir, rushing down the path, with red eyes and an open mouth and fire coming out of his nose. His sharp claws dug into the earth. His great bat-like wings flapped and dragged on the ground, with a sound like thunder.

Siegfried crouched low in the pit, holding his sword ready. The red light from the dragon's nose shone into the pit. There was a roaring and rushing like a storm. Then a great black shape passed over him, and everything went dark.

Now was Siegfried's chance! He thrust his sword Balmung up into the dragon's heart.

The dragon stopped, halfway over the pit. His terrible head dropped to the ground. His wings flapped once, and then lay still. Fafnir was dead. His thick black blood poured down into the pit, and Siegfried was covered in it from head to foot. He had to jump out quickly before the pit filled up.

Then the sun came up, shining on the mountaintops and the river. A gentle wind blew. And for the first time in hundreds of years, birds sang on the Glittering Heath, and insects buzzed, and water rippled. The monster was gone, and the land had woken up.

Greyfell came trotting across the river to stand beside Siegfried. Two black ravens landed nearby, and Siegfried knew them. They were Odin's ravens, Thought and Memory.

"The deed is done," said one raven. "Why does the hero wait?"

"The world is wide," said the other. "Fame is waiting for the hero."

"What if he wins the dwarf's gold?" said the first. "There is no honor in that. Let him find fame by doing better things."

Then the second raven flew close to Siegfried's ear and whispered, "Watch out for Regin, your master. His heart has turned bad. He will try to hurt you."

And the ravens flew away to tell Odin the news.

Then Regin came across the heath. But he looked strange. His face was cold, and his eyes glittered like a snake's, like someone walking in a dream.

"It's mine," he muttered. "It's all mine now. All the gold and all the wisdom in the world. I will keep it all, and I will share it with no one." Then he saw Siegfried, and his face grew dark. "Why are you in my way? I am the master of this treasure, and you are my servant! You have killed my brother!"

"We did it together," said Siegfried calmly. "I have rid the world of a monster. Take the treasure and let us each go our own way."

But Regin had gone mad with greed. He rushed at Siegfried to strike him down. But his foot slipped, and he fell forward onto the sharp edge of Siegfried's sword. And Regin never got up again. The dwarf's curse had come true for him, as the Norns had said.

Siegfried turned away, full of horror, and climbed onto Greyfell.

"This is a terrible place," he said, "and it is not the way to glory. Let the treasure stay here on the Glittering Heath. I will go away, and the world will know me for better deeds than this."

And Greyfell carried him so fast over the deserts and mountains that by nightfall they stood on the shore of the great North Sea. All night Siegfried sat on the warm sand, watching the stars and the moon.

At dawn a white ship came sailing over the water, with an old harper sitting at the front, playing sweet music. The ship was full of flowers of every color, and thousands of singing birds flew around it.

"Hello, Siegfried!" called the harper. "Where are you going on this summer day?"

"I have come from a land of horror," said Siegfried, "and I want to go somewhere brighter."

"Then come with me," said the harper, "and help me wake up the earth after its long winter sleep." And he played such beautiful music that Siegfried stood still to listen.

"Who are you?" asked Siegfried. "Tell me, and I will go to the ends of the earth with you."

"I am Bragi," said the harper, smiling. Bragi was the god of music and poetry. When he played his golden harp, wild animals crept close to listen, the waves were still, and even the elves forgot their treasures and danced.

So Siegfried went on board with Bragi, and Greyfell followed. Bragi played his harp, and the white sails rose up the masts by themselves, and a warm south wind began to blow. And the little ship sailed happily away over the sea.
