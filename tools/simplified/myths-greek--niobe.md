Niobe was the queen of the city of Thebes. She was rich, she was beautiful, and her husband was a famous king. But what she was proudest of was her children. She had seven sons and seven daughters, and they were all strong and handsome.

Every year, the people of Thebes held a festival for the goddess Leto and her two children, the twins Apollo and Artemis. They wore laurel crowns and brought gifts to Leto's temple.

One year, Niobe came to the festival in her finest robes. She looked at the crowd and laughed.

"Why do you bring gifts to Leto?" she said. "I am a queen. I am more beautiful than Leto. And Leto has only two children. I have fourteen! I am far greater than she is. Stop this festival and go home."

The people were afraid of their queen, so they took off their laurel crowns and went home. But they whispered prayers to Leto as they went.

Leto was angry and hurt. She called her children. "Niobe says she is greater than I am," she said, "because she has more children."

"Say no more, Mother," said Apollo. Artemis nodded. The twins were both great archers. They flew down to Thebes, hidden in clouds, with their silver bows.

Outside the city, Niobe's sons were riding their horses and playing games. One by one, the twins' arrows flew down from the clouds. One by one, the young men fell, until all seven of Niobe's sons were gone.

When Niobe heard the news, she ran out and knelt by her sons. She was still proud. "Cruel Leto," she cried, "you have taken my sons. But I still have more children than you do!"

Then the bowstrings sang again. One by one, her daughters fell too. At last only the youngest was left. Niobe wrapped her arms around her. "Please," she begged, "leave me just this one." But even as she spoke, the youngest was gone too.

Niobe sat down among her children. She did not move. She did not speak. Her face turned pale and cold. Her eyes stopped moving. Her hair stopped blowing in the wind. Her arms and legs turned hard and gray. Niobe had turned to stone.

A whirlwind carried the stone away to a mountain far from Thebes. It is still there. And people say that even now, water trickles down the face of the rock, like tears that never stop.

The old Greeks told this story to remind people that it is dangerous to be too proud, and most dangerous of all to boast that you are greater than the gods.
