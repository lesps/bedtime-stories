basedOn: the Silappatikaram, "The Tale of the Anklet", a Tamil epic poem by the poet Ilango Adigal, written down perhaps 1,500 years ago
workId: tamil-kannagi

namesNote: Puhar was a great port of the Chola kings, and Madurai the city of the Pandya kings. Kannagi is still worshipped as a goddess in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, and in Sri Lanka, where she is called Pattini.

Long ago, in the rich port city of Puhar, where ships came from across the sea, there lived a merchant's son called Kovalan and his young wife, Kannagi. At their wedding the whole city sang and danced, and for a time they were very happy.

But then Kovalan fell in love with a famous dancer called Madhavi. He went to live with her, and left Kannagi alone. He spent all his riches on Madhavi, and Kannagi waited for him, sad and lonely, and never stopped hoping that he would come back.

At last Kovalan quarrelled with Madhavi, and came home. He had nothing left. "I have wasted everything we had," he said, "and I am ashamed."

But Kannagi forgave him. "I still have my anklets," she said. They were a pair of golden anklets, and inside each one there were rubies, which rattled when she walked. "Take them, and sell them, and we will start again."

"Then let us go to Madurai, the city of the Pandya king," said Kovalan. "There I will sell one of your anklets, and become a merchant again."

So Kovalan and Kannagi left Puhar before dawn, and walked for many days through forests and fields and dry, rocky lands, with a kind old holy woman to guide them. At last they came to the great city of Madurai, with its high walls and its palace and its busy markets.

Kovalan left Kannagi in a cowherd's house outside the city, and took one of her anklets to the market to sell. There he met the king's goldsmith.

Now this goldsmith was a thief. He had stolen an anklet that belonged to the queen of Madurai. When he saw Kovalan's anklet, which looked just like the queen's, he had a wicked idea. He went to the king and said, "I have found the man who stole the queen's anklet!"

The king did not stop to ask any questions. "Kill the thief," he ordered, "and bring me the anklet." And so the king's guards killed Kovalan, though he had done nothing wrong.

When Kannagi heard what had happened, she was filled with grief and fury. She took the other anklet in her hand, and went straight to the king's palace, with her hair hanging loose and her eyes full of tears. "Tell the king," she said to the guard at the gate, "that a woman who has lost her husband is waiting with an anklet in her hand."

She stood before the king and queen. "O King," she said, "your guards have killed my husband, Kovalan, for stealing an anklet that was mine."

"It is no injustice to kill a thief," said the king.

"Then tell me," said Kannagi, "what was inside the queen's anklet?"

"Pearls," said the king.

"Mine are filled with rubies," said Kannagi. And she broke the anklet in her hand. Out came the rubies, rolling across the floor, and one of them flew up and struck the king's face.

Then the king saw what he had done. "I listened to the lies of a goldsmith," he cried. "I am the thief! I have failed to give justice to my people. Let me die!" And he fell down from his throne, dead of shame. And the queen, who loved him, died too.

But Kannagi's anger was not over. She called out in the street that the city which had killed her husband must burn. And the god of fire came, and Madurai went up in flames. But the fire spared all the good people, the holy men, the old and the sick, the women and the children, and the cows.

Then the goddess of Madurai came to Kannagi, and told her that all this had been bound to happen, because of a wrong done in a life long ago. And Kannagi's anger left her.

Kannagi walked out of the city, and wandered west into the mountains. On the fourteenth day, as she stood on a hilltop, Kovalan came down from the sky in a heavenly chariot, shining like the gods, and took her up into heaven with him.

When the king of the Cheras heard her story, he built a temple to Kannagi. And people have worshipped her ever since, as a goddess of faithful love.
