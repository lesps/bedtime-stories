Long, long ago, a wonderful people came to Ireland. They were called the Tuatha Dé Danann, the people of the goddess Danu. You say it "TOO-ha day DAN-an". Before they came to Ireland, they had lived in four cities in the islands of the north of the world, where they learned magic and wisdom and all kinds of skills. And from those four cities they brought four treasures with them.

From the city of Falias they brought the Stone of Fál. It was set up at Tara, and it would cry out aloud when the true king of Ireland stood on it.

From the city of Gorias they brought a spear. No one could ever win a battle against it, or against the one who held it.

From the city of Findias they brought the sword of their king, Nuada. Once it was drawn from its sheath, no enemy could escape it.

And from the city of Murias they brought the cauldron of the Dagda, their great father-god. Nobody ever went away from that cauldron hungry.

When they arrived in Ireland, they had to fight the people who were already living there, the Fir Bolg, in a great battle on the plain of Moytura. The Tuatha Dé Danann won. But in the battle, their king, Nuada, lost his hand.

Now there was a law that the king of the Tuatha Dé Danann must not have any blemish on his body. So Nuada could not be king any more, even though he had been a good king. Their healer, Dian Cecht, made him a new hand out of silver, which could move just like a real one. From then on he was called Nuada of the Silver Hand. But still he could not be king.

So the Tuatha Dé Danann chose a new king called Bres. Bres was very handsome. His mother was one of their own people, but his father was a king of the Fomorians, a fierce people from across the sea. They hoped that, with Bres as their king, there would be peace with the Fomorians.

But Bres was a bad king. He was mean. When people came to his hall, their knives were not greasy from the meat, and their breath did not smell of ale, because he gave them so little to eat and drink. There were no poets or harpers or jugglers to entertain them. And the Fomorians made the Tuatha Dé Danann pay heavy taxes, and do hard work. Ogma, their greatest champion, had to carry firewood. The Dagda himself had to dig the ditches around a fort for Bres.

One day a poet called Cairpre came to visit Bres. He was put in a little dark house with no fire and no bed, and given three small dry cakes on a plate. In the morning, Cairpre stood up and made a poem about Bres:

"No food on his plate, no milk from his cows, no shelter for a traveller when night comes, no money to pay the storytellers: that is what Bres is like!"

It was the first poem of mockery ever made in Ireland. And after that, everything went wrong for Bres.

Meanwhile, Dian Cecht's son, Miach, who was an even greater healer than his father, took off Nuada's silver hand and put his own hand back on, joint to joint and sinew to sinew. In three times three days it was whole again. Now Nuada had no blemish, and the Tuatha Dé Danann made him their king once more. Bres went off in a rage to the Fomorians, to gather a great army and make war on them.

While King Nuada was holding a feast at Tara, a young warrior came to the gate. He was handsome and fine, like a king. His name was Lugh.

"Who are you?" asked the doorkeeper. "And what can you do? Nobody comes into Tara unless he is a master of some skill."

"I am a carpenter," said Lugh.

"We don't need you. We have a carpenter already."

"I am a smith."

"We have a smith already."

"I am a champion."

"We have one already. Ogma is our champion."

"I am a harper."

"We have a harper already."

"I am a warrior."

"We have one already."

"I am a poet, and a storyteller."

"We have one already."

"I am a magician."

"We have plenty of magicians."

"I am a healer."

"We have Dian Cecht."

"I am a cupbearer."

"We have cupbearers already."

"I am a worker in bronze."

"We have one already."

"Then go and ask the king," said Lugh, "if he has one single man who can do all these things. If he has, I won't come in."

So the doorkeeper went and told the king. "There is a young man at the gate," he said, "who calls himself the master of all the skills."

"Let's test him," said the king. And he sent out his best players of fidchell, a game like chess. Lugh beat every one of them. So the king said, "Let him in."

Lugh came in and sat down in the seat of the wisest man. Then the champion Ogma decided to show his strength. He picked up a huge flagstone, so heavy that it would take eighty pairs of oxen to move it, and he threw it right through the wall of the hall and out of Tara. But Lugh went out, picked it up, and threw it back into the middle of the hall, and he mended the wall where it had gone through.

"Play the harp for us," they said.

So Lugh took the harp. First he played the music of sleep, and the king and all the people fell asleep, and slept until the same time the next day. Then he played the music of sorrow, and they all wept. Then he played the music of joy, and they all laughed and were glad.

When King Nuada saw all that Lugh could do, he thought, "Perhaps this young man can free us from the Fomorians." And he got down from his throne and let Lugh sit on it instead, for thirteen days, to plan the war.

Lugh called together all the skilled people of the Tuatha Dé Danann and asked them what they would do in the battle. "I will make spearheads that never miss," said Goibniu the smith. "I will heal every warrior who is wounded," said Dian Cecht. "I will put him in my healing well, and he will come out as well as before." And each of the others said what they would do, with their skills and their magic.

So the second great battle of Moytura began. It was a terrible battle, against the huge army of the Fomorians. The Tuatha Dé Danann tried to keep Lugh out of it, because they could not bear to lose him. But he slipped away from his guards and went to the front of the army.

The Fomorians had a terrible champion called Balor. He had an evil eye. He only ever opened it on the battlefield, and it took four men to lift its eyelid, with a handle. Any army that looked into that eye was overcome by it, even if there were thousands and thousands of men. In the battle, Balor struck down King Nuada. Then, when Balor's men began to lift his eyelid, Lugh put a stone in his sling and let it fly. The stone hit the eye and drove it right through to the back of Balor's head, so that it looked at his own army instead. And Balor fell.

Then the Fomorians were beaten, and they fled back to the sea. Bres was taken prisoner. "Spare my life," he begged, "and I will teach you how to farm, when to plough and when to sow and when to reap." And the Tuatha Dé Danann spared him for that.

And when the battle was over, the goddess Morrigan stood on the high places of Ireland, and called out the news of the victory to all the hills and rivers and to all the people. And she sang:

"Peace up to the sky. Sky down to the earth. Earth under the sky. Strength in everyone."
