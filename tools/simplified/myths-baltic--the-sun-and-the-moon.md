In Lithuania and Latvia, people used to sing short songs called dainas. Thousands of them have been remembered. Some are about planting and harvest, some are about weddings, and some are about the Sun and the Moon and the stars, who were thought of as a family living in the sky. This story comes from those songs.

The Sun, Saulė, is a mother. Every morning she rises and drives across the sky in her shining chariot, and every evening she goes down beyond the sea. And the Moon, Mėnulis, is a man.

Long, long ago, in the very first spring, the Moon married the Sun. But the Sun was always busy. She rose very early in the morning, and worked hard all day long, giving light and warmth to the whole world. And the Moon didn't like being left on his own.

So the Moon went off and wandered about the sky by himself. And as he wandered, he met Aušrinė, the Morning Star, the brightest and most beautiful star in the sky, who rises just before the dawn. And the Moon fell in love with her.

But Perkūnas, the god of thunder, saw what the Moon had done. And he was very angry. He took his sword and struck the Moon, and cut him in two.

"Why did you leave the Sun?" thundered Perkūnas. "Why did you fall in love with the Morning Star? Why do you wander alone at night?"

And that, the old songs say, is why the Moon is not always round. Sometimes we see the whole Moon, and sometimes only half of him, or just a thin slice. And that is why the Sun and the Moon are never seen together. The Sun shines in the day, and the Moon wanders alone at night, and they live apart for ever.

In Latvia, they sing the story a little differently. There, the Sun is called Saule, and the Moon is Mēness. They sing that the Moon stole away the bride who had been promised to the Morning Star. And it was the Sun herself who was so angry that she took her sharp sword and cut the Moon to pieces.

But whichever way you sing it, the Moon was cut, and ever since then he has been growing and shrinking in the night sky, and the Sun and the Moon have never shone together again.
