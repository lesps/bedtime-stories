title: The Singing Contest
adaptedFrom: eivind-finnish--wainamoinen-and-youkahainen

After the world was made, Väinämöinen lived happily in the land of Kalevala. In the evenings he sang songs about the old days, and about how the world was made, until everyone everywhere had heard of him, and said he was the greatest singer in the world.

Far away in the cold, dark Northland there lived a young man called Joukahainen. He was a singer and a magician too, and he was very proud. One day, at a feast, somebody told him that Väinämöinen was a sweeter singer and a more powerful magician than anyone else in the world. Joukahainen was so jealous that he decided to go south at once and beat Väinämöinen in a contest.

His mother begged him not to go, but he would not listen. "I'll sing such magic songs," he boasted, "that I'll turn old Väinämöinen into stone!" So he harnessed his horse to his golden sledge, cracked his whip, and dashed off towards Kalevala. On the evening of the third day he came near Väinämöinen's home. And there was Väinämöinen himself, driving along the road towards him.

Joukahainen was far too proud to move out of the way for anybody. So the two sledges crashed into each other and smashed to pieces, and the harnesses got all tangled up together.

"Who are you, foolish boy," said Väinämöinen, "who drives so badly that you have smashed my sledge?"

"I am Joukahainen," said the young man proudly, "and I have come to beat old Väinämöinen at singing and magic."

So Väinämöinen told him who he was, and agreed to the contest. But Joukahainen soon found that he was no match for the old singer. At last he shouted angrily, "If I can't beat you at singing and magic, I'll beat you with my sword!"

"I won't fight anyone as weak as you," said Väinämöinen.

"You're a coward!" cried Joukahainen. "You're afraid to fight!"

At last Väinämöinen grew so angry that he could not hold himself back any longer, and he began to sing. He sang such wonderful magic songs that the mountains and rocks began to shake, and the sea heaved as if there were a great storm. Joukahainen stood still, unable to move. And as Väinämöinen sang, Joukahainen's sledge turned into a heap of brushwood, and the reins into willow branches, and his whip into a reed. His horse turned into a rock in the water, and the harness into seaweed. His painted bow became a rainbow in the sky, his arrows flew away as hawks and eagles, and his dog turned to stone at his feet. His cap turned into a curl of mist, his clothes into white clouds, and his jewelled belt into stars.

And then the magic began to work on Joukahainen himself. Slowly, slowly, he began to sink into a quicksand, and however hard he struggled, he could not get out. When he had sunk up to his waist, he began to beg for mercy.

"Oh, great Väinämöinen, you are the greatest magician of all! Let me out of this quicksand, and I'll give you two magic bows."

But Väinämöinen didn't want the bows, and he sang Joukahainen down deeper. Joukahainen offered him two magic boats, and then two magic horses, and then all his gold and silver and his harvests. But Väinämöinen would not even listen. At last Joukahainen had sunk so deep that the mud and water were coming up to his mouth. And he cried out, "Oh, mighty Väinämöinen, if you let me go, I'll give you my sister Aino to be your wife!"

That was what Väinämöinen had been waiting for, because Aino was famous for her beauty and her kindness. So he set Joukahainen free, and gave him back his sledge and everything else, just as they had been before. And Joukahainen jumped into his sledge and drove off home without a word.

He drove so carelessly that he smashed his sledge against his own gateposts, and left it lying there, and walked into the house with his head hanging down. At first he would not answer anybody. But at last he said, "Mother, I have good reason to be sad. I have had to promise my dear sister Aino to old Väinämöinen, to be his wife."

But his mother jumped up and clapped her hands. "That's nothing to be sad about, my son!" she said. "For years I have hoped that Aino would have a husband as brave and wise as Väinämöinen."

So the mother told Aino the news. But Aino wept for three days and three nights, and nobody could comfort her. "Why has this sorrow come to me, mother?" she cried. "Now I won't be able to wear jewels in my golden hair any more. I'll have to hide it under the ugly cap that wives wear. All the golden sunshine and the silver moonlight will go out of my life."

But her mother tried to comfort her. She told her that the sun and moon would shine even more brightly in her new home, and that Kalevala was a land of flowers.
