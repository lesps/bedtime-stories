title: The Seven Labours of Rostam

namesNote: In Persian these are Rostam's Haft Khan, his seven trials. Zimmern spells the names Rustem, Rakush, Kai Kaous, Aulad and Arzang. A div (Zimmern's Deev) is a demon. A farsang is about three and a half miles.
---

Kay Kavus, the king of Iran, had marched into Mazandaran, the land of the divs, the demons, even though Zal had warned him not to go. There the White Div spread a black cloud over his army, and when morning came, the king and all his men were blind. The White Div kept them prisoner, and Kay Kavus sent a message to Zal, begging for help.

When Zal read the message, he bit his hands in dismay. Then he called Rostam. "The time has come to saddle Rakhsh," he said, "and set the world right with your sword. I am two hundred years old, and too weak to fight demons now. But you are young and strong. Put on your leopard skin, and set Iran free."

"My sword is ready," said Rostam, "and I will go. No one walks into the mouth of a hungry lion for fun. But if God is with me, I will beat the demons."

Zal blessed him, and told him the way. "There are two roads to Mazandaran," he said, "and both are dangerous. The one Kay Kavus took is safer, but it is long, and we must be quick. So take the shorter road, even though it is full of terrible things. And may God bring you safely back to me."

Rostam jumped on Rakhsh. But his mother, Rudabeh, came out weeping, and tried to stop him from going against the demons. Rostam comforted her. He told her that he had not chosen this adventure himself, and that she should put her hope in God. At last she let him go, though her eyes were red with tears for many days.

Rakhsh flew along so fast that he covered two days' journey in twelve hours. In the evening Rostam caught a wild donkey, roasted it on a fire, and ate it. Then he took off Rakhsh's saddle, and lay down to sleep among the reeds, not afraid of wild beasts or demons.

But a fierce lion lived in those reeds. When it came home and saw the big man and his horse, it thought, "First I'll beat the horse, and then the rider will be easy." And it sprang at Rakhsh. But Rakhsh fought back. He struck the lion's head with his hooves, and bit it with his sharp teeth, and trampled it until it died. The noise woke Rostam, and when he saw the dead lion and Rakhsh standing beside it, he knew what had happened. "You silly horse!" he said. "Who told you to fight lions? Why didn't you wake me? If you had been beaten, who would have carried me to Mazandaran to save the king?" Then he went back to sleep, but Rakhsh was sad. That was the first labour.

The next day they rode all day through a desert. The sun burned down, the sand was like fire, and horse and rider were nearly dying of thirst. Rostam could find no water anywhere. So he got ready to die, and prayed to God to remember the king, and lay down to wait for the end. But just then a fat, healthy ram walked past. "Its drinking place can't be far away," Rostam said to himself. He got up and followed the ram, leading Rakhsh, and it led them to a spring of cool, clear water. Rostam drank and drank, and gave Rakhsh water too, and washed him. When they felt better, he looked for the ram, but it was nowhere to be found. Then Rostam knew that God had worked a wonder for him, and he knelt on the ground and gave thanks. That was the second labour.

That night, before he went to sleep, Rostam said to Rakhsh, "Don't go fighting anything while I sleep. If an enemy comes, come and neigh in my ear, and I will wake up and help you."

In the middle of the night a fierce, fiery dragon came out of its den. Even the demons were afraid of it. It was amazed to see a man sleeping there, and it came towards them, breathing poison. Rakhsh stamped his hooves and swished his tail, and the noise woke Rostam. But the dragon vanished, and Rostam saw nothing. "Why did you wake me for nothing?" he grumbled, and went back to sleep.

The dragon came again, and again Rakhsh woke Rostam, and again the dragon vanished before Rostam could see it. When this had happened three times, Rostam was furious. He shouted at Rakhsh, and said that if he did it again, he would leave him behind and walk to Mazandaran on foot.

Then Rostam pulled his leopard skin around him and lay down again. Poor Rakhsh did not know what to do. When the dragon came out once more, ready to spring, Rakhsh was afraid, but he was brave, and he went to Rostam once more, and stamped and neighed and woke him. Rostam jumped up in a rage, but this time he saw the dragon, and he knew that Rakhsh had been right. He put on his armour, drew his sword, and went out to fight.

"Who are you," said the dragon, "who dares to come against me? Your mother will weep for you!"

"I am Rostam, the son of Zal," he answered, "and I am an army all by myself!"

The dragon laughed, and wrapped itself around Rostam to crush him, and it looked as if the hero's end had come. But Rakhsh, seeing his master in danger, sprang at the dragon from behind and tore at it, and Rostam drove his sword into it, and together they rid the world of it. Then Rostam praised Rakhsh, and washed him at the spring, and thanked God. That was the third labour.

They rode on into the land of the witches and wizards, and in the evening they came to a green, shady valley with a stream running through it. Beside a spring a table was laid with wine and all sorts of good food. Rostam was delighted to find a feast in the desert. He did not know that it belonged to the witches and wizards, who had run away when they saw him coming. He ate and drank, and then he picked up a lute that was lying there, and sang a song about himself: how Rostam never had feasts or holidays, how his garden was the desert and his games were battles, and how he fought not knights but dragons and demons.

One of the witches heard his song. She turned herself into a beautiful young woman, and came and sat beside him, and asked his name. Rostam was pleased with her company, and he poured her a cup of wine, and told her to drink to God. But when the witch heard the name of God, she began to tremble, and her face changed, and Rostam saw her as she really was. At once he caught her with his rope, and that was the end of her. All the other witches and wizards were too frightened to come near him, and Rostam rode away. That was the fourth labour.

Rostam rode on until he came to a land where the sun never shone and there were no stars, and he could not see the path. So he let Rakhsh lead the way. They stumbled on through the darkness, and at last they came out into the light, into a green land of fields full of young crops. Rostam let Rakhsh graze, and lay down to sleep.

But Rakhsh wandered into a field that had been sown, and the man who looked after it was angry. He ran to Rostam, beat the soles of his feet with a stick to wake him, and shouted at him for letting his horse into the field. Rostam lost his temper, and seized the man by the ears so hard that he ran away howling, all the way to Olad, the ruler of that land. Olad was angry too, and he came out with an army to find Rostam, swearing to destroy him.

"I am the thundercloud that sends out lightning," said Rostam. "If you heard my name, your blood would freeze. You have come against me with an army. Watch how I scatter it like the wind!"

And Rostam fell on Olad's warriors and beat them, and the whole army was put to flight by one man. Olad ran away crying, but Rostam caught him with his rope, tied him up, and said, "Tell me the truth, and I will set you free. And when I have beaten the demons, I will make you king of Mazandaran. Tell me where the White Div lives, and where I can find the king and his men, and how I can set them free."

Olad told him that it was a hundred farsangs to where Kay Kavus lay a prisoner, and another hundred to the mountain pass where the White Div lived. The passes were guarded by lions and wizards and mighty warriors, and no one had ever got through. He begged Rostam to give up.

But Rostam smiled. "Be my guide," he said, "and you will see one elephant beat all the power of evil." That was the fifth labour.

So Rostam leaped onto Rakhsh, and Olad ran beside him, still tied up, and they went like the wind, never stopping day or night, until they came to the place where Kay Kavus had been struck blind. They could see the watch fires of Mazandaran. Rostam tied Olad to a tree and slept, and in the morning he rode cheerfully towards the city of the demons. When he came near the tents of Arzhang, the general of the demons' army, he gave a shout that shook the mountains. Arzhang rushed out of his tent to fight him, but in Rostam's grip he was like an insect, and Rostam killed him. When the demons' army saw it, they were terrified, and they ran away in such confusion that they fell over each other. That was the sixth labour.

Then Rostam untied Olad, and told him to lead the way to the city where Kay Kavus was held. As they came near, Rakhsh gave a neigh so loud that it reached the king in his prison. Kay Kavus knew that sound at once. "Help has come!" he told his men. But they would not believe him; they thought sorrow had driven him out of his mind. Then Rostam himself strode in, and when they heard his voice and his step, they were sorry they had doubted. Kay Kavus hugged Rostam and blessed him.

"We cannot waste time, my champion," said the king. "When the White Div hears that Arzhang is beaten, he will come with all his demons, and even you cannot stand against them all. Go to the Seven Mountains, and beat the White Div before he hears that you are coming. I cannot help you, for we are all blind. A wise man has told me that only the blood of the White Div's heart can give us back our sight. Bring it to me, my son, and may God be kind to you."

So Rostam rode off, with Olad beside him to show the way. When they had crossed the Seven Mountains and come to the very gates of the demons' land, Rostam said, "You have always led me right, Olad. Now tell me how to beat the demons."

"Wait until the sun is high," said Olad. "When it is hot, the demons lie down to sleep, and then you can beat them easily."

So Rostam waited by the roadside, and to make sure Olad did not run away, he tied him up with his rope and sat on the end. When the sun was high, he drew his sword, shouted his name like a thunderbolt, and charged in among the demons before they were properly awake. No one could stand against him, and he scattered the guards. Then he came to the cave of the White Div.

Rostam went into the rocky cave. It was dark and stuffy and smelled horrible, and he could not see where he was going, but he went on without fear. At the end of the cave he found a great heap like a mountain. It was the White Div, having his midday sleep. Rostam woke him up. The demon was astonished at his daring, and leaped at him, and threw a stone as big as a small mountain. Rostam's heart shook, and he said to himself, "If I live through today, I'll live for ever." Then he fell on the demon, and they fought long and hard, wrestling until they were both running with sweat and blood. Then Rostam prayed to God, and God gave him strength, and at last Rostam beat the White Div and killed him, and took his heart. That was the seventh labour.

He went back to Olad and told him what he had done. "Brave lion," said Olad, "please untie me now, for your rope is cutting into me. And remember that you promised me a reward."

"I will keep my promise," said Rostam. "But I still have work to do: I must beat the king of Mazandaran first. When that is done, you shall have your reward."

Then they went back to Kay Kavus. When the king heard that Rostam had come back victorious, he shouted for joy, and all his men shouted with him. Rostam put drops of the White Div's blood into their eyes, and the eyes of Kay Kavus and all his men were opened, and they saw the light of day once more. Then they overcame the demons who had been guarding them. But when that was done, Kay Kavus told them not to shed any more blood.
