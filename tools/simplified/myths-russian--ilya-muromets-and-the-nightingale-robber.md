Long ago, in the village of Karacharovo near the city of Murom, there lived a peasant who had one dear son, called Ilya. For thirty years Ilya sat at home and never walked. But when the thirty years were over, he began to walk, and he grew mighty strong.

Ilya made himself armour and a steel lance, and got himself a fine horse, fit for a hero. Then he asked his father and mother for their blessing. "Let me go to the great city of Kiev," he said, "to pray to God, and to serve the Prince of Kiev."

His parents gave him their blessing, and made him promise: "On your journey, never hurt anyone for no reason."

So Ilya set off. In the dark forest, he came upon a camp of robbers. They saw his wonderful horse and wanted it for themselves. So they rushed at him, ten at a time, then twenty at a time. Ilya stopped his horse, put an arrow to his bow, and shot it into the ground at their feet. It went fifty feet deep into the earth!

The robbers were terrified. They fell on their knees. "Mighty hero, forgive us!" they cried. "We'll give you whatever you want: fine clothes, or herds of horses."

Ilya smiled. "I don't need anything," he said. "But if you want to live long, never try anything like this again."

He rode on. Near the city of Chernigov he found a huge army surrounding the city, getting ready to burn it down. Ilya was afraid, but he trusted in God, and charged at the army with his steel lance, and drove it away, and saved the city. The people of Chernigov came out to thank him, and gave him a great feast.

From Chernigov, Ilya took the straight road to Kiev. But for thirty years nobody had been able to travel that road, because of the Nightingale Robber. He did not attack travellers with a sword or a spear. He only whistled his robber's whistle. But that was enough to stop everyone.

Ilya rode on through the Bryansk forest, over the swamps, and across the river Smorodina. The Nightingale Robber heard him coming, and he whistled. But Ilya's brave heart was not afraid. He rode on, and the Nightingale Robber whistled even louder, so loud that Ilya's horse stumbled.

At last Ilya came to the Robber's nest, which was built on top of twelve oak trees. The Nightingale Robber sat in his nest, and when he saw Ilya coming, he whistled with all his might. But Ilya took his bow and shot an arrow into the nest, and it struck the Robber in the eye. The Nightingale Robber fell out of his nest like a sheaf of oats. Ilya tied him to his stirrup, and rode on to Kiev.

On the way, he passed the Robber's house. The Robber's three daughters were looking out of the window. "Here comes father," cried the youngest, "bringing a prisoner tied to his stirrup!" But the eldest looked and cried, "That's not father! It's a stranger, dragging our father along!" And they told their husbands to go out and fight him.

But the Nightingale Robber called out to his sons-in-law, "Don't fight this hero, or he'll beat you all. Better to say you're sorry, and invite him in for a drink." So they did, and Ilya came to no harm.

When Ilya reached Kiev, he went straight to the Prince's palace and bowed to him.

"What is your name, young man?" asked the Prince. "Where are you from?"

"They call me Ilya, son of Ivan," he said. "I come from the city of Murom, from the village of Karacharovo. On the way I saved the city of Chernigov from a great army, and I caught the Nightingale Robber, and brought him here tied to my stirrup."

The Prince was angry. "Why are you telling such tall tales?" he said.

But two of the Prince's knights, Alyosha Popovich and Dobrynya Nikitich, ran out to look. And they told the Prince it was true.

The Prince wanted to hear the Robber whistle. So Ilya wrapped the Prince and Princess in a sable fur coat, and held them tight, and told the Robber to whistle only half as loud as he could. But the Nightingale Robber whistled with all his might, and all the knights fell to the ground. And so Ilya made sure that the Nightingale Robber would never whistle again.

Then Ilya and Dobrynya swore to be brothers, and rode out together over the plains, looking for adventures. For three months they found no enemy worth fighting. Then they met a wandering beggar, singing psalms. His shirt weighed as much as three men, and his hat as much as two, and his walking stick was as long as a tall tree.

"Ilya Muromets!" said the beggar. "Don't you remember me? We went to school together. Don't you know that something terrible has happened in Kiev? A huge, wicked warrior called Idolishche has come. His head is as big as a cauldron, and he eats a whole ox at a time, and drinks a whole barrel at a time. And the Prince is upset with you for leaving him in such trouble."

So Ilya put on the beggar's clothes, and rode straight back to the Prince's palace. "Good Prince," he called, "give a poor beggar something to eat!"

"Come in, beggar," said the Prince. "I'll give you food and drink."

So Ilya went in and stood by the stove and looked around. There was Idolishche. They brought him a whole roast ox, and he ate it all, right down to the bones. Then they brought him a great cauldron of beer, which took twenty men to carry, and he drank it all in one go.

"My father had a greedy mare," said Ilya. "She guzzled until she could not breathe."

Idolishche was furious. "How dare you, beggar? I could crush you in my hand! Even if you were Ilya Muromets himself, I wouldn't be afraid of you."

"Well," said Ilya, "this is what Ilya Muromets is like!" And he took off his cap and gave Idolishche just a light tap on the head with it, and Idolishche went crashing almost through the palace wall. Then Ilya threw him out.

Then the Prince honoured Ilya, and praised him, and made him one of the mighty knights of his court.
