The Sampo had made the Northland rich, and old Louhi kept it locked away in the hills. So Väinämöinen said to Ilmarinen, "Let's go to the Northland and bring the Sampo back to Kalevala."

"That won't be easy," said Ilmarinen. "Louhi has fastened it with nine great locks, and three roots have grown around it, under the mountain and the water and the sand."

But Väinämöinen talked him into it. So Ilmarinen went to his workshop and made Väinämöinen a magic sword, so strong that it could cut through the hardest stone. Then the two heroes put on their armour, and set off north in their sledges along the seashore.

They had not gone far when they heard someone crying. They followed the sound, and found a ship lying all alone on the sand.

"Why are you crying?" Väinämöinen asked the ship.

"Because I have to lie here doing nothing," said the ship. "I was built to sail out full of warriors, but they have left me here to rot."

"You shall lie here no longer," said Väinämöinen. "We will sail in you to the Northland."

So they launched the ship and climbed aboard. Väinämöinen sang his magic songs, and at once one side of the ship was full of young men with beards, and the other side was full of girls, and in the middle sat strong old heroes with grey beards. First the young men rowed, but they could not move the ship. Then the girls rowed, but they could not move it either. Then the old heroes rowed, but still the ship would not move. Then Ilmarinen took the oars himself, and at once the ship shot through the water, with old Väinämöinen steering.

Soon they came to an island, where a man was working on a fishing boat. "Where are you going?" he called.

"Silly Lemminkäinen, don't you know us?" said Väinämöinen. For it was Lemminkäinen.

When Lemminkäinen heard that they were going to the Northland to fetch the Sampo, he begged to come too. So they took him on board. Soon they came to a dangerous place, with girls singing on the shore, and hidden rocks and whirlpools all around. But Lemminkäinen knew the magic song to make the girls calm the whirlpools, and Väinämöinen steered the ship safely through.

But as soon as they were out in open water again, the ship stopped dead, as if it had dropped anchor. They pushed with a long pole, but it would not move. So Väinämöinen told Lemminkäinen to look underneath the ship. And there was a gigantic pike, and the ship was stuck fast on its back!

Lemminkäinen drew his sword and struck at the pike, but he missed, and fell overboard, and they had to pull him out, dripping wet. Then Ilmarinen struck at it, but his sword broke into pieces. At last Väinämöinen drew his magic sword, and with one blow he caught the great fish, and lifted it out of the water.

Now the ship was free. The heroes were tired, so they rowed to the shore, and the girls on board cooked the pike, and there was a delicious feast with plenty for everyone. Afterwards the fish bones lay in a heap on the rocks.

Väinämöinen looked at the bones for a long time. "Wonderful things could be made from these bones," he said, "if only I could find someone clever enough to make a kantele." But nobody understood what he meant, because nobody had ever heard of a kantele before. So in the end, Väinämöinen made it himself. He made the frame of the harp from the pike's jawbones, and the pegs from its teeth, and for the strings he used hairs from the tail of a magic horse.

When the first kantele was finished, it was so beautiful that everyone crowded round to look at it. Väinämöinen let them all try to play it, but every time anyone touched it, it made a horrible noise. "Give it to me," said Lemminkäinen, "I'll show you how to play it." But when he touched the strings, it sounded even worse. At last they said, "Let's throw it in the sea." But the kantele spoke. "I will never be thrown into the sea," it said. "I will bring great joy to Kalevala. Give me to the one who made me, and I will sing for him."

So they laid it at Väinämöinen's feet. The great magician rested the kantele on his knee, and tuned it until all the strings sounded sweetly together. Then he swept his hands across the strings, and the most wonderful music poured out.

Every living thing that heard it stopped to listen. Out of the forests came the bears and the wolves and the lynxes, and even Tapio, the god of the forest. Out of the sea came the fishes to the water's edge, and Ahti, the god of the sea. The daughters of the Sun and the Moon, who were spinning on the clouds, stopped and dropped their spindles.

For three whole days Väinämöinen played, until everyone who heard him wept. At last even Väinämöinen himself began to cry at the beauty of his own music. His tears ran down his long beard and over his clothes, and onto the ground in shining streams, and down into the sea.

Then the music stopped. "Can anyone fetch my tears back from the sea?" asked Väinämöinen. But nobody could.

Then a raven flew up and offered to try, and Väinämöinen promised it beautiful feathers if it could. But the raven failed. Then a duck came, and Väinämöinen made it the same promise. The duck dived down to the very bottom of the sea, and gathered up every one of Väinämöinen's tears. But when it brought them back, they had changed. They were not tears any more. They were the most beautiful pearls.

And that is how pearls were made, and that is why the blue duck has such lovely feathers.
