---
title: The Drowned Land of Cantre'r Gwaelod
basedOn: the Welsh legend of the Lowland Hundred, first found in a poem in the Black Book of Carmarthen (written in the 1200s) and told along Cardigan Bay ever since
workId: welsh-cantref-gwaelod

If you stand on the beach at Borth, in the west of Wales, and look out over the sea, you will see nothing but water stretching away towards Ireland. But long ago, so the story goes, there was land out there: a wide, green, flat country, full of fields and farms and towns. It was called Cantre'r Gwaelod, the Lowland Hundred, and it was the richest and most beautiful land in all of Wales.

Its king was Gwyddno Longshanks. He was the same Gwyddno who had a fish trap on the shore, in the story of Taliesin.

The Lowland Hundred was lower than the sea. So, to keep the sea out, the people had built a great wall of earth and stone, all along the coast. In the wall there were gates, called sluices. When the tide went out, the gates were opened, so that the water from the rivers could flow out into the sea. And when the tide came in, the gates had to be shut, to keep the sea out.

The man whose job it was to look after the wall and the gates was called Seithennin. But Seithennin was not a careful man. He loved feasting, and he loved drinking wine even more.

One stormy night, the king held a great feast in his palace. There was music and singing, and the wine flowed, and Seithennin drank more than anyone. He drank until he fell asleep. And he forgot to close the gates.

Outside, the wind was howling, and the tide was rising higher and higher. The great waves came rolling in from the sea and found the gates wide open. The water poured in through them, and it burst through the wall, and it rushed across the green fields of the Lowland Hundred. It swept over the farms and the villages and the towns, and nobody could stop it.

King Gwyddno woke up and saw the water coming. He and a few of his people ran for the hills, and they got away just in time. But when morning came, and they looked back from the hills, the Lowland Hundred was gone. Where the fields and the towns had been, there was nothing but the grey sea. And it has been sea ever since.

There is an older way of telling the story, too, in an old Welsh poem written down more than seven hundred years ago. In that story, there was a magic well in the land, and a young woman called Mererid was its keeper. One night, after a feast, she let the well overflow, and its water rose up and drowned the whole country. And the poem calls out to Seithennin to come and look at the fury of the sea, which has covered the land of Gwyddno.

Nobody knows if there ever really was a Lowland Hundred. But here is a strange thing. Sometimes, after a big storm, when the tide is very low, the sand on the beach at Borth is washed away, and underneath it you can see the stumps of an ancient forest, thousands of years old, sticking up out of the sea.

And the fishermen of Cardigan Bay say that on quiet nights, if you listen very carefully, you can hear the bells of the churches of the drowned land, still ringing under the sea.
