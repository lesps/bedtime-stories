Ravana was dead, and his good brother Vibhishana was king of Lanka. And Sita was brought to Rama at last, in front of the whole army.

But Sita had lived for a long time in Ravana's palace, and people were whispering about her. Rama's face was clouded, and he said nothing.

"Rama," said Sita, "why are you silent? Do you doubt me? Why did you cross the ocean and fight all those battles to save me, if you think I have not been true to you?" And when Rama still said nothing, she turned to Lakshmana. "Lakshmana, build me a fire," she said.

So Lakshmana, with his heart pounding, built a great fire. And Sita prayed to the gods. "If I have always been true to Rama, in all I have done and all I have thought," she said, "may Agni, the god of fire, protect me." And she stepped bravely into the flames, and disappeared.

All the people cried out and wept. But slowly the flames parted, and out stepped Agni, the god of fire himself, carrying Sita in his arms. Not a hair on her head was burned, not a flower in her hair was faded, not a thread of her dress was scorched.

"Take back your wife, Rama," said Agni. "Ravana carried her away, but she has always been true to you. She thought only of you. I, who see everything that everyone does, tell you that she is good and pure."

Then Rama's face lit up with joy. "I never doubted Sita," he said, "not from the day I first saw her. But now the whole world knows that she is true." And Rama held Sita in his arms, with tears in his eyes, and Sita hid her face against him.

Then Rama, Sita and Lakshmana climbed into the Pushpaka, the flying chariot pulled by swans, and flew home. As they flew, Rama pointed out the places they passed. "Look, my love," he said. "There is Lanka, on its three-peaked mountain. There is the bridge across the sea, which people will call Rama's Bridge for ever. There is Kishkindha, where Sugriva is king, and the lake of Pampa, where I wept for you. There is the forest where Jatayu fought for you. Look, there is the cottage where we lived, and the hill of Chitrakuta, where Bharata came to find us. There is the river Yamuna, and there is the great river Ganga. And look, Sita! There is Ayodhya, our home!"

In Ayodhya, Bharata heard that Rama was coming, and he was filled with joy. "Let the temples ring with music!" he said. "Let the priests make offerings, and the minstrels sing, and the soldiers line up at the gates. We are going to welcome our brother, our king!"

So the city was swept clean, and the streets were sprinkled with water and strewn with flowers. Flags waved from every tower and temple, and garlands hung from every house. Out came the elephants in their golden trappings, and the chariots and horses, and the soldiers with their banners. Rama's mothers, Queen Kausalya and Queen Sumitra, came in their litters, and the priests and the nobles came too. And in front of them all came Bharata, carrying Rama's sandals.

Then the flying chariot came sailing through the clear sky, and ten thousand voices shouted Rama's name. Old people and little children cheered and called out to him. The swans flew gently down, and the chariot landed. Bharata climbed up into it, and Rama hugged him tight. Then Bharata took Rama's sandals, which had sat on the throne all those years, and laid them at Rama's feet.

"These have kept your throne for you," he said. "Now I give you back your kingdom. You are home again, and you will be our king."

And there was joy in Ayodhya, with music in the royal hall. The fourteen years of sorrow were over. Rama and Sita sat on the jewelled throne, and the priests poured holy water over them, brought from rivers and hills and the great ocean. They placed the old king's crown on Rama's head. Rama's brother Shatrughna held the white royal umbrella over him, and Sugriva and Vibhishana waved fans beside him. The god of the wind sent Rama a golden garland, and the god Indra sent him a garland of pearls. Heavenly musicians played, and heavenly dancers danced, and all the people cheered for their king.

And the old storytellers say that while Rama was king, no one died too young, and no one fell ill. There were no robbers and no cheats. Neighbours loved one another, and the people loved their king. The trees were heavy with fruit, the rain came at the right time, and the fields were always full of good harvests.
