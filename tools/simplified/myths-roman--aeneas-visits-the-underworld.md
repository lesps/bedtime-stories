At last the Trojans reached the coast of Italy, near a town called Cumae. While his men lit fires and fetched water, Aeneas climbed up to the great cave of the Sibyl.

The Sibyl was a priestess of the god Apollo. She was very old and very wise, and she could see the future. Her cave was cut deep into the rock, with a hundred doorways, and when she spoke, her voice came rushing out of all hundred at once.

When Aeneas came in, the spirit of Apollo took hold of the Sibyl. Her face changed. Her hair flew wild. She seemed to grow taller, and her voice was no longer a human voice.

"You have escaped the dangers of the sea," she cried, "but worse dangers wait for you on land. The Trojans will reach their kingdom, but I see war, terrible war, and the river Tiber running red. You will have to fight another hero as fierce as Achilles. And Juno will never stop troubling you. But do not give up. Be braver still."

When the spirit left her, Aeneas spoke. "Lady, I am ready for whatever comes. But I ask you one thing. They say the door to the land of the dead is near here. My father Anchises died on our journey. I carried him on my shoulders out of burning Troy. In a dream, he asked me to come and see him. Please, show me the way."

"Son of Anchises," said the Sibyl, "it is easy to go down into the land of the dead. The door is open day and night. But to come back up into the daylight again, that is the hard part. Only a few have ever done it. But if you truly wish to go, listen. Somewhere in the forest grows a tree with a branch made of gold. It is sacred to Proserpine, the queen of the dead. No one may enter her kingdom without it. Find it, and if the gods want you to go, it will break off easily in your hand. If not, no sword in the world can cut it."

So Aeneas went into the deep, dark forest. How would he ever find one golden branch among so many trees? Then two doves flew down and landed on the grass in front of him. They were the birds of his mother, Venus. They flew a little way, then waited for him, then flew on again, leading him deeper into the forest. At last they perched on a tree, and there among the dark green leaves, something glittered. It was the golden bough, its golden leaves rustling in the breeze.

Aeneas reached up, and the branch broke off easily in his hand. He carried it back to the Sibyl.

Before dawn, at the mouth of a great dark cave by a gloomy lake, Aeneas made offerings to the gods of the underworld. The ground rumbled, dogs howled in the darkness, and the Sibyl cried, "The goddess is coming! Now, Aeneas, draw your sword and follow me. Now you need all your courage!" And she rushed into the cave, and Aeneas followed close behind.

They walked through the shadowy land of the dead, like people walking through a wood on a cloudy night when only a little moonlight comes through. At the gate sat Sorrow and Fear and Sickness and Hunger and Old Age, and all the other troubles of the world. In the middle grew a huge elm tree, and on every leaf clung a false dream. There were monsters too, centaurs, half man and half horse, and the giant Briareus with his hundred hands. Aeneas rushed at them with his sword, but the Sibyl told him they were only shadows, with no bodies at all.

Then they came to the dark river Styx. On it, an old boatman named Charon ferried the spirits across in his boat. He had a long white beard and fiery eyes. A huge crowd of spirits stood on the bank, as many as the leaves that fall in autumn, all stretching out their hands to be taken across.

"Why does he take some and not others?" asked Aeneas.

"Those he leaves behind were never buried," said the Sibyl. "They must wander here for a hundred years before they may cross."

Among them Aeneas saw his friend Palinurus, his pilot, who had fallen into the sea. Aeneas was very sad for him. "You are not buried, my friend," said the Sibyl to Palinurus. "But be comforted. The people near where you came ashore will build you a tomb, and the place will be named after you forever." And that made Palinurus feel better.

When Charon saw Aeneas coming, with a sword in his hand, he shouted, "Stop! This is the land of shadows. No living person may ride in my boat!"

The Sibyl held up the golden bough. When Charon saw it, his anger melted away. He brought his boat to the bank, and Aeneas climbed in. The boat groaned and creaked under the weight of a living man, and water leaked in through the cracks, but they got safely across.

On the other side, in a cave, lay Cerberus, the guard dog of the underworld, with three heads and snakes around his necks. All three heads began to snarl and bark. But the Sibyl threw him a honey cake full of sleepy herbs. Cerberus gobbled it up with all three mouths, and in a moment he was stretched out across the cave, fast asleep.

As they walked on through the Fields of Mourning, Aeneas saw someone he knew. It was Dido, the queen of Carthage, walking among the shadowy trees.

"Dido!" he cried, with tears running down his face. "Is it true, then? Did you die because of me? I swear I did not want to leave you. The gods made me go. I never knew how much I would hurt you. Please, don't go. Speak to me!"

But Dido would not even look at him. She kept her eyes on the ground, as still and silent as a stone. Then she turned away into the shady woods, where her first husband, Sychaeus, was waiting for her, and he loved her as much as she loved him.

Next they came to the place where heroes who died in battle lived. Many Trojans crowded around Aeneas, glad to see him, and asked him how he came to be there. But the Greek soldiers who saw his shining armor ran away in fear.

Then the path divided into two. "The road on the left," said the Sibyl, "leads to Tartarus, where the wicked are punished." Aeneas looked, and saw a great fortress with three walls, and a river of fire rushing all around it. He heard groans, and the clanking of chains. "No good person may go in there," said the Sibyl. "But the road on the right leads to the palace of Pluto, the king of the dead, and to the Elysian Fields. That is our way."

At the door of the palace, Aeneas laid the golden bough as a gift for the queen of the underworld. And then they came to the Elysian Fields, the home of the good and the happy.

It was a beautiful place, with green meadows and woods, with its own sun and its own stars. The air was clear and bright. Some people ran races and wrestled on the grass. Others danced and sang. Their horses grazed in the fields, and there was music everywhere.

And there, in a green valley, sat old Anchises. When he saw Aeneas coming, he stretched out both his hands, and tears ran down his cheeks.

"You've come, my son!" he cried. "I knew you would. I knew your love for me would get you here."

"Father," said Aeneas, "let me hold you!" He threw his arms around his father, three times. But three times he clasped only air, for his father was a spirit, as light as the wind and as thin as a dream.

Then Aeneas saw a river, with crowds of spirits gathered along its banks, as many as bees buzzing around the flowers on a summer day.

"These are spirits who will one day be born again into the world," said Anchises. "First they drink the water of that river, the river Lethe, and forget everything that happened before. Come, my son. I will show you the people who will be born from your family."

He led Aeneas up a little hill, and showed him the faces as they passed. "That young man leaning on a spear is your son Silvius, who will be born to you in your old age, and will be a king. And there is Romulus, the son of Mars, who will build Rome, and its rule will reach to the ends of the earth. That old man with the olive crown and the white beard will give Rome its first laws. And there is Camillus, and there are all the heroes who will come after him."

"Other people," said Anchises, "will be better at carving marble statues, or making speeches, or studying the stars. But this will be the work of Rome: to bring peace to the world, to be gentle with the humble, and to stand up to the proud."

So Anchises showed his son all the glory to come, and told him about the wars he would have to fight, and how to face them.

The dreams of the underworld leave by two gates, a gate of horn and a gate of ivory. Anchises led Aeneas and the Sibyl out through the ivory gate. And Aeneas hurried back to his ships, full of courage for all that lay ahead.
