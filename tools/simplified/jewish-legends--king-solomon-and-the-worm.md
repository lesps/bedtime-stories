title: King Solomon and the Wonderful Worm
adaptedFrom: friedlander-jewish--king-solomon-and-the-worm

namesNote: Friedlander spells the king of the demons Ashmodai; he is also called Ashmedai or Asmodeus. Benaiah was the captain of Solomon's guard.
---

King Solomon, the wisest of all kings, wanted to build a great Temple to God. And he remembered that the altar of God must not be built with tools of iron. For iron makes swords, and swords bring war and death, but the Temple was to be a place of peace and life. So Solomon wanted all the stones for the Temple to be cut without any iron tools at all.

But how could anyone cut great blocks of stone without iron? Even Solomon did not know. So he called together his council of wise men.

One old counsellor stood up. "Long live the King!" he said. "Have you not heard of the Shamir? It is a tiny worm, no bigger than a grain of barley. But it can split the hardest stone just by touching it. God made it on the very first Sabbath eve, at twilight, among the ten wonders of creation."

"Wonderful!" cried Solomon. "Now tell me, where can I find this worm?"

"That I do not know," said the counsellor. "No one has ever found where the Shamir lives. But you are the master of all the spirits and demons. Ask them, and you will find it."

So Solomon looked at the ring on his right hand. The holy Name of God was carved on it. As soon as he spoke the Name, a demon appeared and bowed before him. "What do you wish, King Solomon?"

"Tell me where to find the worm Shamir," said Solomon.

"Mighty King," said the demon, trembling, "I don't know. Only Ashmodai, the king of the demons, knows that secret. He lives far, far away, in a palace on top of a very high mountain. In the mountain he has dug a deep well. Every morning he drinks from it, then he covers the well with an enormous rock and seals it with his ring, and flies off to the ends of the earth. When the sun goes down, he comes home, checks his seal, drinks from the well, and seals it up again."

Then Solomon called for his brave captain, Benaiah. "Go and catch Ashmodai, the king of the demons, and bring him to me," he said. "Take this golden chain, with the holy Name of God written on its links, and this ring of mine, which has the Name on it too. And take this bundle of wool, and these skins full of strong wine." And he told Benaiah exactly what to do.

Benaiah rode for many days across the desert, until he came to a lonely mountain, silent and grim. Bravely he climbed it. Halfway up, he dug a hole into the side of Ashmodai's well, and let all the water run out, and stopped up the hole with the wool. Then he dug another hole above, and poured all the wine into the well. Then he hid behind a rock and waited.

Soon after sunset, Ashmodai came home. He looked at his seal, and it had not been touched. He rolled away the rock and went down to drink. But what was this strange smell? He came back up, puzzled. But he was so thirsty that he went down again, and drank the wine. The more he drank, the more he wanted. Soon his head grew heavy, his knees gave way, and he fell fast asleep.

Then Benaiah crept out, threw the golden chain around Ashmodai's neck, and sealed it with Solomon's ring.

When Ashmodai woke up, he found the chain around his neck. He roared so terribly that the mountain shook, and sparks flew from his eyes. He struggled and struggled, but he could not get up.

"Was it you who did this to me?" he cried.

"The Name of the Lord is upon you," said Benaiah.

Then Ashmodai became quiet and calm, for he knew he was beaten. "I will do as you say," he said.

So they set off together for Jerusalem, and on the way Ashmodai did some very strange things. In one village he brushed against a palm tree and knocked it right over. In another place he bumped into a house and pushed it down. And once, when they met a blind man who had lost his way, Ashmodai hurried to put him on the right road. Benaiah was surprised at the strange way he behaved.

When they passed a magician who was boasting to a crowd that he could see the future and find hidden things, Ashmodai laughed again. "Wouldn't you laugh too," he said, "at a man who says he can find secrets, when there is treasure buried right under his feet, and he doesn't know it? We demons see people and things as they really are."

At last they came to Jerusalem, and Benaiah brought Ashmodai before King Solomon. When Ashmodai saw the king, he began to tremble all over. He was leaning on a long staff, and he threw it down at the king's feet.

"What are you doing?" said Solomon.

"Great King," said Ashmodai, "when you die, for all your glory, you will have no more room in the earth than the length of that staff. And still you are not happy to rule over people. You must rule over the spirits and demons too!"

"Don't be angry," said Solomon gently. "You will not find me a hard master. I only want one small thing from you. I am going to build a great Temple to the Maker of heaven and earth, and I need the worm Shamir. Where can I find it?"

"I don't have the Shamir," said Ashmodai. "Since the days of Moses, the Prince of the Sea has looked after it. He gave it to the woodcock to keep. The woodcock has sworn always to carry the Shamir with him. He lives in a big nest on top of a very high mountain peak, and he uses the Shamir to split the rocks, so that seeds can grow in the cracks for him to eat. Whenever he leaves his nest, he carries the Shamir under his wing."

"Then you shall stay with me until the Temple is built," said Solomon.

Once again Solomon called his captain Benaiah. "Find the woodcock's nest," he said, "and bring me the Shamir. Take with you a glass cover, a little wool, and a small box made of lead."

Benaiah set off over hills and valleys, rivers and deserts, until at last he found the woodcock's nest. The woodcock was away, and his chicks were in the nest. Benaiah covered the nest with the glass cover. Then he hid and waited.

When the woodcock came home, he could not get into his nest. He could see his chicks through the glass, but he could not reach them. He flapped his wings and screeched and tried and tried to break the glass, and the chicks screeched too. But the glass was too strong.

So at last the woodcock took out his precious treasure. He brought the Shamir out from under his wing and put it on the glass, and at once the glass split into pieces. At that very moment, Benaiah gave a great shout. The woodcock was so frightened that he dropped the Shamir. And as soon as it fell to the ground, Benaiah seized it. He wrapped it carefully in the wool, and shut it safely in the little lead box.

Then Benaiah hurried home to Jerusalem, and gave the wonderful worm to King Solomon. And with the help of the Shamir, Solomon built the Temple.

After that, the Shamir disappeared, and no one knows where it is today.
