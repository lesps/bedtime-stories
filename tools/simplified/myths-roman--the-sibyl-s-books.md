As Aeneas and the Sibyl climbed back up from the land of the dead, toward the daylight, Aeneas turned to her.

"Whether you are a goddess or a mortal woman," he said, "I will always honor you. When I reach the world above, I will build you a temple and bring you gifts."

"I am no goddess," said the Sibyl. "I do not need a temple. I am a mortal woman, though I could have lived forever. Let me tell you my story.

"When I was young, the god Apollo loved me, and he promised to give me anything I wished for. So I picked up a handful of sand, and held it out, and said, 'Let me live for as many birthdays as there are grains of sand in my hand.'

"Apollo granted my wish. But I had made a terrible mistake. I asked to live for a long, long time, but I forgot to ask to stay young. And because I would not be his bride, Apollo let me grow old.

"So I have grown older and older. I have lived seven hundred years already, and I still have three hundred more springs and three hundred more harvests to see before I have used up all the grains of sand. My body grows smaller and smaller every year. One day I will shrink away until no one can see me at all. But my voice will stay, and people will still listen to what I say."

The Sibyl lived in a cave, and she wrote the future on leaves from the trees. She would write people's names and what would happen to them on the leaves, and lay them out neatly in her cave, so that people could come and read them. But if someone opened the door and the wind rushed in and blew the leaves about, the Sibyl would not put them back in order. Then the message was lost forever.

Many years later, long after Aeneas, when Rome was ruled by kings, a strange old woman came to see one of the kings, named Tarquin. She was carrying nine books.

"Would you like to buy my books?" she asked, and she named a price. It was a very high price.

"Certainly not," said the king. "That's far too much."

The old woman went away. She burned three of the books. Then she came back with the six that were left.

"Would you like to buy my books?" she asked. And she asked for exactly the same price as before, for only six books.

"Certainly not!" said the king. "That's even worse!"

The old woman went away again. She burned three more books. Then she came back with the last three.

"Would you like to buy my books?" she asked. And she asked for the very same price she had wanted for all nine.

Now the king began to wonder. What could be in these books, that she would burn them rather than sell them cheap? He bought the last three books, for the price of nine.

When they opened the books, they found that they were full of prophecies about the future of Rome. The Romans kept them in a stone chest in the great temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill. Only a few special priests were allowed to read them. And whenever Rome was in great danger, the priests would open the books and look for advice.

But no one ever knew what the six burned books had said.
