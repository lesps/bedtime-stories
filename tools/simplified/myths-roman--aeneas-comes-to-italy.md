The Trojans sailed north along the coast of Italy. At night they passed the island of Circe, the enchantress, and heard lions roaring and wolves howling in her palace. They were men she had turned into animals. But Neptune sent a wind to carry the Trojans safely past.

As the sun came up, they saw a great forest on the shore, full of birds, and through it flowed a wide river, yellow with sand, out into the sea. It was the river Tiber. The Trojans steered their ships into the river and landed. They had come at last to the land Creusa's ghost had told them about.

That land was called Latium, and its king was old Latinus. He had only one child, a daughter named Lavinia. Many princes wanted to marry her, and the most handsome of them was Turnus, prince of the Rutulians. Lavinia's mother, Queen Amata, wanted her to marry Turnus. But strange signs had warned the king against it. A great swarm of bees landed in the sacred laurel tree in the palace, and the prophets said, "A stranger is coming to be Lavinia's husband." Once, as Lavinia stood by the altar, a flame leaped onto her hair and her jeweled crown and filled the palace with smoke, and the prophets said, "She will be famous and great, but a terrible war is coming." And the voice of the king's father, the god Faunus, said, "Do not marry your daughter to a man of Latium. A husband will come for her from across the sea, and he will make our name great from one end of the sky to the other."

On their first day, Aeneas and his son Ascanius and their friends sat down under a tree to eat. They had no plates, so they put their food on flat round loaves of bread. When they had eaten all the food, they were still hungry, so they ate the bread plates too.

"Look!" laughed Ascanius. "We're even eating our tables!"

Aeneas jumped up and hugged him. "That's it!" he cried. "Do you remember the Harpy's prophecy? She said we would not build our city until we were so hungry that we ate our own tables. And now we have! This is the place. We are home!"

And Jupiter thundered three times in the clear sky.

The next day Aeneas sent a hundred men to King Latinus, carrying olive branches as a sign of peace, and gifts. The king welcomed them in his great palace, with its hundred pillars and its statues of old kings carved from cedar wood.

"Welcome, men of Troy," said King Latinus. "What do you want here? Did a storm blow you off course?"

"No, great king," said Ilioneus, who spoke for the Trojans. "We came on purpose. The gods sent us. Our ancestor Dardanus came from this land long ago. We ask only for a little land by the sea to build our homes, and water and air, which are free for everyone."

King Latinus sat thinking for a long time. Then he said, "You shall have your land. And tell your king this. I have a daughter. The gods have told me that she must marry a stranger who will come from far away. I think your king may be that man."

He gave each Trojan a fine horse, and for Aeneas he sent a chariot pulled by two horses that breathed fire from their noses.

But up in the sky, Juno saw it all, and she was furious. "Those Trojans!" she raged. "I could not burn them in Troy. I could not drown them at sea. And now they have reached the Tiber! Well, I cannot stop the Fates. Aeneas will marry Lavinia. But I can make him pay for it in war."

So Juno went down into the dark places under the earth and called up Allecto, the most terrible of the Furies, whose hair was made of snakes. Even her own sisters were afraid of her.

"Stir up a war," Juno told her. "Break this peace."

First, Allecto went to Queen Amata. She took a snake from her hair and dropped it into the queen's dress, and it breathed its poison into her heart. Amata went half mad with anger. "My daughter will marry Turnus, not some wandering stranger!" she cried. And she ran off into the woods with Lavinia, to hide her away.

Then Allecto flew to Turnus. She came to him in his sleep, disguised as an old priestess. "Are you going to let these strangers steal your bride and your kingdom?" she whispered.

Turnus only laughed. "Go home, old woman," he said. "Leave war to the warriors."

Allecto was furious. She threw off her disguise, and Turnus saw her hissing snakes. She threw a flaming torch into his heart, and he woke up in a cold sweat, burning with rage and shouting for his armor.

Last of all, Allecto went to the forest, where young Ascanius was out hunting with his dogs. Now, nearby lived a girl named Silvia, and she had a pet stag. She had raised it since it was a baby. She decorated its antlers with flowers, and combed its fur, and washed it in the stream. By day it wandered in the woods, and every evening it came home.

Allecto made Ascanius's dogs pick up the stag's scent. The dogs chased it, and Ascanius, thinking it was a wild deer, shot it with an arrow. The poor wounded stag ran home, crying, to Silvia.

Silvia called out for help. Her brothers and all the country people came running with clubs and sticks. The Trojans came running to help Ascanius. Soon clubs became swords, and a real battle broke out. A brave young man named Almo, Silvia's eldest brother, was among those who fell, and so was a good old farmer named Galaesus, who had tried to make peace.

That was what Juno wanted. "Enough," she told Allecto. "Now there has been blood. I will see to the rest."

The country people carried their dead to the city and cried out to King Latinus for war. Turnus shouted loudest of all. But the old king stood firm, like a rock in the sea. "This is wrong," he said. "You will be sorry." But no one would listen, and at last he shut himself away in his palace.

Now, in Latium there was a custom. In the city stood a temple with two great gates, the gates of war. In peace, they were kept shut. When war began, the king would open them. The people begged King Latinus to open the gates, but he would not. So Juno herself came down from the sky, and pushed the gates, and burst them open.

All across Italy, people began to get ready for war. They sharpened their axes and polished their shields. They stopped plowing their fields and took down their fathers' old swords. And the great chiefs gathered with their armies. There was Mezentius, a cruel king who did not respect the gods, and his good son Lausus. There was Aventinus, a son of Hercules, wearing a lion skin. There was Messapus, the son of Neptune, and many more.

Biggest of all was Turnus himself, taller than all the rest by a whole head, with a fire-breathing Chimaera on his helmet.

And last came Camilla, a warrior princess, riding at the head of her horsemen in shining bronze armor. She had never cared for spinning or weaving. She loved to fight, and she could run faster than the wind. She could run across a field of wheat without bending a single stalk, and race across the sea without getting her feet wet. Everyone came out to stare at her as she rode past.

And so the war for Italy began.
