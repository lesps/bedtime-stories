The Greeks had fought outside the walls of Troy for ten long years. Even the great hero Achilles was gone now.

When Achilles was a baby, his mother, the sea goddess Thetis, had dipped him in a magic river so that no weapon could ever hurt him. But she had held him by one heel, and the water never touched that spot. One day, Paris shot an arrow, and it struck Achilles in the heel, the only place where he could be hurt. Even today, when someone has one weak spot, we call it their "Achilles heel."

Without Achilles, the Greeks did not know how they would ever win. Troy's walls were too high and too strong.

Then clever Odysseus had an idea. "If we can't break down the walls," he said, "we'll trick the Trojans into letting us in."

The Greeks built an enormous horse out of wood. It was taller than a house, and it was hollow inside. Then Odysseus and some of the bravest soldiers climbed inside it and closed the secret door.

The rest of the Greeks burned their camp, got into their ships and sailed away. But they did not go home. They hid behind an island, just out of sight.

In the morning, the Trojans looked out from their walls. The Greek camp was empty. The ships were gone. All that was left was the giant wooden horse, standing on the beach.

"The Greeks have given up!" they shouted. "We've won!" They threw open the gates and ran out onto the beach to look at the horse.

"What is it?" they asked each other. Some people said it was a present for the goddess Athena, so that the Greeks would have a safe journey home.

"Let's take it into the city!" cried some.

"No! Burn it!" cried others.

A priest named Laocoon ran up. "Are you mad?" he shouted. "Don't trust the Greeks, even when they bring presents!" And he threw his spear at the horse. It struck the side with a hollow boom.

But just then, two enormous sea serpents came swimming out of the waves. They wrapped themselves around Laocoon and his two sons. The Trojans gasped. They thought the gods were angry with Laocoon for throwing his spear at the horse.

"The horse is sacred!" they said. "We must bring it into the city."

Then a Greek man was found hiding on the beach. "My people left me behind," he lied. "That horse is a gift for Athena. They made it too big to fit through your gates, because if it ever goes inside Troy, the city will never be defeated."

That was all the Trojans needed to hear. They tied ropes to the horse and pulled it into the city, singing and dancing. They had to break down part of their own wall to get it through.

King Priam's daughter, Cassandra, could see the future. But she had been given a strange curse: no one would ever believe what she said. "Stop!" she cried. "There are soldiers inside!" Nobody listened.

That night, the Trojans had a great party to celebrate the end of the war. They feasted and sang, and then they went to bed tired and happy.

When the city was dark and quiet, the secret door in the horse's belly opened. Odysseus and the Greek soldiers climbed down a rope, as quietly as cats. They crept to the city gates and opened them wide. Outside, the rest of the Greek army had sailed back in the darkness, and they were waiting.

The Greeks poured into the city. By morning, Troy had fallen. Menelaus took Helen home to Sparta, and after ten long years, the Trojan War was over at last.

Even today, people say "Beware of Greeks bearing gifts." It means: be careful of presents from someone who might be trying to trick you.
