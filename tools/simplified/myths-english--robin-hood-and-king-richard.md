One autumn, all of Nottinghamshire was in a great bustle, because King Richard the Lionheart was travelling through England, and he was coming to Nottingham to stay with the Sheriff. People hung silk banners across the streets, and carpenters built a throne in the town hall for a great feast.

On the day the King arrived, the streets were packed with people. A big, burly friar at the front of the crowd grumbled at a soldier who pushed him. "Hush, Tuck," whispered a man beside him. "You promised to keep quiet." For scattered through the crowd were Robin Hood and his merry men, who had come to see the King.

Then trumpets sounded, and the procession came: heralds with silver trumpets, a hundred knights in shining armor, lords in silk and gold, and soldiers. In the middle rode the Sheriff, and beside him rode a man a head taller, with hair and beard like threads of gold and eyes as blue as the summer sky. It was King Richard, and the crowd roared.

Above all the noise, a great voice bellowed, "Heaven bless our gracious King Richard!" The King laughed. "Sheriff," he said, "you have the loudest priests in England! I wish I had an army of men like him."

But the Sheriff went pale and nearly fell off his horse. For he knew that loud friar was Friar Tuck, and behind him stood Robin Hood, Little John, Will Scarlet and Allan a Dale. "Are you ill, Sheriff?" asked the King. "Only a sudden pain, Your Majesty," said the Sheriff. He was too ashamed to admit that Robin Hood was so little afraid of him that he walked right into Nottingham.

That night at the great feast, the King said, "I've heard a lot about an outlaw called Robin Hood. Tell me about him, Sheriff."

The Sheriff looked gloomy. "They are the boldest lawbreakers in the land," was all he would say. But others at the table told stories of Robin's adventures, and the King laughed until he cried.

"He's as bold and merry a rascal as I ever heard of!" said King Richard. "I'd give a hundred pounds to meet him."

"Then, Your Majesty," said one of his knights, Sir Hubert, "let us dress up as friars, and carry a purse with a hundred pounds in it, and ride through Sherwood Forest. I'm sure we'll meet Robin Hood, and have dinner with him too."

"I like that plan!" said the King.

The next morning, the King and six of his knights put on the black robes of friars and rode into the forest on mules. "Oh, dear," said the King, "we forgot to bring anything to drink. I'd give fifty pounds for a drink right now."

At once a tall man with a yellow beard and merry blue eyes stepped out of the bushes and took hold of the King's bridle. "Holy brother," he said, "we keep an inn near here. For fifty pounds we'll give you a drink and the finest feast you ever ate." He whistled, and sixty men in Lincoln green came out of the trees.

"Who are you, you rogue?" said the King.

"My name is Robin Hood. Show me your purse, reverend brother."

"Take it," said the King sternly, "but don't lay a hand on me."

"My, what proud words!" laughed Robin. "Are you the King of England?" He gave the purse to Will Scarlet, who kept fifty pounds and gave the other fifty back. "Won't you put back your hood, brother? I'd like to see your face."

"No," said the King. "We have promised not to show our faces for a whole day."

"Then keep them covered," said Robin. And he led the friars deep into the forest, to the great greenwood tree.

"You have a fine band of men, Robin," said the King, looking around. "King Richard himself would be glad of such a bodyguard."

"There isn't one of us," said Robin proudly, "who wouldn't give his life for King Richard. We love him, because he is brave, like us."

Then Robin raised his cup. "Here's to good King Richard!" he cried. And everyone drank, even the King himself.

"Now," said the King, "I've heard you are wonderful archers. Show us your skill."

So they hung a garland of flowers on a post, far down the clearing. "Each of you shoot three arrows," said Robin. "And anyone who misses the garland gets a box on the ear from Will Scarlet."

One by one they shot. Wat the Tinker missed by two fingers, and Will Scarlet gave him such a smack that he went heels over head onto the grass, and the King laughed until tears ran down his face. Last of all Robin shot. His first arrow split a piece off the post. His second landed right beside it. But his third arrow had a bad feather, and it wobbled and landed just outside the garland.

How the outlaws roared! They had never seen their master miss before. "That arrow had a bad feather!" cried Robin. "Give me another!"

"No, uncle," said Will Scarlet. "You missed, fair and square. Come here and take your punishment."

"No," said Robin. "I'm the king here, and none of my men may hit me. But I'll take my punishment from this holy friar." And he turned to the disguised King. "Brother, will you do it?"

"Gladly," said King Richard, "since you took fifty pounds from me."

"If you knock me down," said Robin, "I'll give you your fifty pounds back. But if you don't, I'll take everything you have."

"Agreed," said the King. He rolled up his sleeve, and showed an arm that made the outlaws stare. Then he swung, and his blow fell like a thunderbolt. Down went Robin flat on the grass! He sat up and looked around as if he had dropped from the clouds, and gently felt his ear. "Will," he said, "give that fellow his fifty pounds back. I don't want anything more to do with him or his fist!"

Just then Little John came running into the clearing with sixty more outlaws and Sir Richard of the Lea, a knight whom Robin had once helped. "Hurry, Robin!" cried Sir Richard. "King Richard has left Nottingham to come and find you! Come and hide in my castle. But who are these strangers?"

Then Sir Richard looked at the tall friar, and his face went pale. He leaped off his horse and fell on his knees. The tall friar threw back his hood, and everyone saw his golden hair and beard. It was King Richard! All the outlaws fell to their knees.

"Sir Richard," said the King sternly, "how dare you offer to hide these outlaws in your castle?"

"I would rather face Your Majesty's anger," said Sir Richard, "than let any harm come to Robin Hood. I owe him everything."

Then one of the other friars knelt beside him and threw back his hood. It was Sir Richard's son, Sir Henry, who had once saved the King's life in battle. "I stand with my father," he said. "I would give Robin Hood shelter too."

The King looked from one to the other. At last his frown faded, and a smile twitched at his lips. "Get up, all of you," he said. "No harm will come to anyone today. Robin, can you still hear me with that ear?"

"My ears will hear Your Majesty's voice until I die," said Robin. "And I think that blow has paid for all my sins."

"Don't joke about your sins, Robin," said the King. "But I forgive you and your whole band. Only you can't go on living in the forest as outlaws. You said you would serve me, so you shall come to London with me, and Little John, and Will Scarlet, and Allan a Dale. And the rest of your men shall be my royal rangers, taking care of my deer in Sherwood, instead of shooting them. Now, let's have a feast!"

So they had a great feast on white cloths spread on the green grass, and Allan a Dale sang for the King. The King said he had never eaten such a fine dinner in his life. That night King Richard slept in Sherwood Forest on a bed of green leaves. And the next day, when the King rode into Nottingham with Robin Hood and his merry men behind him, the Sheriff didn't know where to look.

Then Robin Hood and Little John, Will Scarlet and Allan a Dale said goodbye to all the rest of the band, promising to come back and visit often, and rode away to London with the King.
