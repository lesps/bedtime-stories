---
title: Cuchulain and the Beheading Game
basedOn: Bricriu's Feast (Fled Bricrenn), an Irish tale preserved in the Book of the Dun Cow, written down around the year 1100
workId: irish-bricriu
order: 10

In Ulster there lived a man called Bricriu, who was nicknamed Poison-Tongue, because he loved nothing better than making trouble. One day he built a splendid new house and invited the king of Ulster and all his heroes to a great feast there.

Now at feasts in those days, the bravest hero always got the best piece of meat, called the champion's portion. And before the feast, Bricriu went secretly to each of the three greatest heroes of Ulster in turn. First he went to Laegaire the Victorious. "Why shouldn't you have the champion's portion?" he whispered. "You're the bravest." Then he went to Conall Cernach and whispered the same thing. Then he went to young Cuchulain and whispered the same thing again.

So when the meat was carved, all three heroes jumped up at once, each claiming the champion's portion. They drew their swords, and there would have been a terrible fight if the king had not stopped them. And from then on, they argued and argued about which of them was the bravest. They were set many tests, and every time Cuchulain did best. But the other two would never agree that he had won.

Then, one evening, when the heroes of Ulster were sitting in the king's hall at Emain Macha, a stranger walked in. He was a huge, ugly giant, wrapped in an old black cowhide. His yellow eyes were each as big as a cauldron. In his left hand he carried a great block of wood, and in his right hand an enormous axe.

He went and stood by the fire. "I have come to look for something I have never found anywhere," he said. "Not in Ireland, nor in Scotland, nor in the whole world. A man who will keep his word."

"What do you mean?" they asked.

"Here is my bargain," said the giant. "One of you may cut off my head tonight, with this axe. And then tomorrow night, I will come back, and cut off his."

The heroes looked at one another. Then a warrior called Fat-Neck stood up. "I'll do it," he said. So the giant laid his head on the block, and Fat-Neck swung the axe, and off came the giant's head.

But the giant simply stood up, picked up his head and his axe and his block, and walked out of the hall. Everyone stared after him in horror.

The next night, the giant came back, with his head on his shoulders again, just as before. But Fat-Neck was nowhere to be found.

"Isn't there anyone in Ulster who keeps his word?" said the giant scornfully. "Where are those three heroes who are always arguing about who is the bravest?"

So Laegaire the Victorious said he would do it. He cut off the giant's head. And the giant picked it up and walked out, just as before. But the next night, when the giant came back, Laegaire had disappeared.

Then Conall Cernach tried. He cut off the giant's head, and the giant picked it up and walked out. And the next night, Conall was gone too.

"The heroes of Ulster are all talk," said the giant. "Their courage is gone. They want the champion's portion, but there is not one of them who dares to claim it. Where is that poor little lad, Cuchulain? Is his word any better?"

"I don't want your bargain," said Cuchulain.

"No wonder," said the giant. "You're afraid to die."

At that, Cuchulain jumped up, seized the axe, and struck off the giant's head with one great blow. And the giant stood up, picked up his head, and walked out.

The next night, all the heroes of Ulster were in the hall, watching Cuchulain. He sat waiting for the giant, and he was very sad. He knew he must die. But he was not going to break his word.

"You will hear the giant come soon," said the king, gently. And then, in came the giant, with his head back on his shoulders.

"Where is Cuchulain?" he said.

"Here I am," said Cuchulain.

"You're very quiet tonight, poor fellow," said the giant. "You're afraid to die. But at least you have kept your word."

Cuchulain went to the block and laid his head on it.

"Stretch out your neck," said the giant. "It isn't long enough."

"Don't torment me," said Cuchulain. "Kill me quickly. I didn't keep you waiting last night."

He stretched out his neck as far as it would go. The giant lifted his huge axe until it touched the roof beams. It creaked like a tree in a storm. And down it came.

But it came down with the blunt side, and it did not even scratch Cuchulain's neck.

"Get up, Cuchulain," said the giant. "Of all the heroes of Ulster and of all Ireland, there is not one to match you for courage, or for keeping your word. From today, you are the champion of all the heroes of Ireland, and the champion's portion is yours. And if anyone argues with you about it, his own life will be in danger."

Then the giant vanished. For he was not really a giant at all. He was Curoi, a great lord and enchanter, who had promised to settle the quarrel between the three heroes. And from that day on, nobody argued any more about who was the bravest hero in Ulster.
