---
title: Eglė, Queen of Serpents
basedOn: the Lithuanian folk tale, first printed in 1837 and told in many versions, among them the one Jonas Basanavičius published in 1904

Long ago, in Lithuania, an old man and his wife had twelve sons and three daughters. The youngest daughter was called Eglė.

One summer evening, the three sisters went to bathe in the lake. When they had finished swimming, they climbed out to get dressed. But when Eglė went to pick up her shirt, there was a grass snake coiled up on it.

"Go away!" cried Eglė. "Get off my shirt!"

But the snake spoke to her in a man's voice. "I will give you back your shirt," it said, "if you promise to marry me."

Eglė didn't know what to do. She couldn't go home without her shirt. So at last she said, "All right. I promise." And the snake slid away into the reeds.

Three days later, a great crowd of snakes came hissing and slithering into the farmyard. They had come to fetch the bride.

Eglė's parents were horrified. They didn't want to give their daughter to the snakes. So they tried to trick them. First they dressed up a white goose and gave it to the snakes instead. The snakes set off with it. But on the way, a cuckoo called out from a tree:

"Cuckoo, cuckoo! They have tricked you! That's a goose, not Eglė!"

So the snakes came back, angry. This time the parents gave them a sheep, and then a cow. But each time, the cuckoo called out and told the snakes they had been tricked. At last the snakes came back in a fury. "Give us the real bride," they hissed, "or we will bring drought and hunger on your land!"

So, weeping, the parents gave them Eglė.

The snakes took her down to the seashore. And there, waiting for her, was not a snake at all, but a handsome young man. "I am Žilvinas," he said. "I was the snake who sat on your shirt." He led her out to an island, and down under the sea, to his palace. It was a beautiful palace, and Žilvinas was the king of the snakes, and so Eglė became their queen.

Žilvinas was so kind and loving that Eglė was happy. Nine years went by, and they had four children: three sons, called Ąžuolas, Uosis and Beržas, which mean Oak, Ash and Birch, and a little daughter called Drebulė, which means Aspen. Eglė almost forgot her old home.

But one day her eldest son asked her, "Mother, where are your father and mother? Can we go and see them?" And Eglė began to long for home.

"Please let us go and visit my family," she begged Žilvinas.

Žilvinas did not want her to go. "First," he said, "you must spin this bundle of silk." But however much she spun, the silk never got any smaller. So Eglė went to an old wise woman for advice. "Throw it in the fire," said the wise woman. Eglė did, and a toad came crawling out from under the house, for it was the toad's magic that had kept the silk from ever running out. Now the spell was broken, and Eglė had done what Žilvinas asked.

"Next," said Žilvinas, "you must wear out these iron shoes." Eglė walked and walked, but the shoes did not wear out. So the wise woman told her to take them to the blacksmith and have them heated in his fire, and after that they wore out quickly.

"Last of all," said Žilvinas, "you must bake a cake." But he had hidden all the bowls and pots. So the wise woman told her to spread leaven on a sieve, so that it would hold water, and that way Eglė mixed and baked her cake.

So at last Žilvinas let them go, for nine days. He walked with them to the shore, and taught them how to call him when they wanted to come home. "Stand at the edge of the sea," he said, "and call out: Žilvinas, dear Žilvinas, if you are alive, let the sea foam with milk; if you are dead, let the sea foam with blood. But don't tell anyone else these words."

Eglė's family were overjoyed to see her. But her twelve brothers did not want her to go back to the sea. They made up their minds to get rid of Žilvinas, and to do that, they needed to know the words that would call him.

So they took Eglė's eldest son into the forest, and tried to make him tell. But he would not say a word. Then they tried the second son, and the third, but they would not tell either. Last of all, they took little Drebulė. And she was so frightened that she told them the words.

Then the brothers went down to the shore with their scythes, and called out the words. And when Žilvinas came up out of the sea, they killed him. Then they went home, and said nothing to their sister.

When the nine days were over, Eglė went down to the shore with her children, and called out: "Žilvinas, dear Žilvinas, if you are alive, let the sea foam with milk; if you are dead, let the sea foam with blood."

And the sea foamed red.

Then Eglė knew that Žilvinas was dead, and who had done it. In her grief, she turned to her children. "My sons," she said, "you were brave and true. Stand here for ever as strong trees." And her eldest son became an oak, and the second an ash, and the third a birch. "And you, my daughter," she said to Drebulė, "who gave away your father's secret, you shall be an aspen, and tremble at every breath of wind." And Drebulė became a trembling aspen.

"And I," said Eglė, "shall be a spruce." For that is what her name means. And she became a tall, dark spruce tree, standing by the sea.

And that is why, to this day, the aspen's leaves tremble at the slightest breeze.
