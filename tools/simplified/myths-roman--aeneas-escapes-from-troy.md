For ten long years the Greeks had been trying to capture the city of Troy. They camped outside its tall walls and fought battle after battle. But still the city did not fall.

Then the goddess Minerva gave a Greek named Epeius a clever idea. He built an enormous horse out of wood. Inside its hollow belly hid some of the bravest Greek warriors. Then the rest of the Greeks packed up their tents, climbed into their ships and sailed away. But they did not go home. They hid behind an island just out of sight.

In the morning, the people of Troy could not believe their eyes. The Greeks were gone! They flung open the gates and ran out to see the empty camp. And there stood the giant wooden horse.

"It's a gift for the goddess Minerva," some said. "Let's pull it into the city!"

But a priest named Laocoön came running. "Are you mad?" he cried. "Do you really think the Greeks have gone? Either there are soldiers inside this thing, or it's some other trick. I don't trust the Greeks, even when they bring gifts!" And he threw his spear at the horse, and it struck its side with a hollow boom.

Just then some shepherds came, dragging a Greek prisoner with his hands tied behind his back. His name was Sinon, and he was a very good liar.

"The Greeks wanted to kill me," Sinon said, "so I ran away. They have gone home. They built this horse as a gift to Minerva, and they made it huge on purpose, so that you could not fit it through your gates. Because if you bring it into Troy, the goddess will protect your city forever."

Then something terrible happened. Two giant sea serpents came swimming across the water. They slid up onto the beach and went straight for Laocoön and his two sons, and wrapped them in their coils, and that was the end of them. Then the serpents slithered away and hid under the statue of Minerva.

The people of Troy were terrified. "Laocoön threw a spear at Minerva's horse," they said, "and the goddess punished him. Sinon is telling the truth!"

So they made a gap in their own walls and pulled the wooden horse into the city with ropes. Children sang and touched the ropes for luck. Four times the horse got stuck, and four times they heard the clank of armor inside. But nobody paid attention.

That night, while Troy slept, the Greek ships sailed back in the moonlight. Sinon crept to the horse and opened a secret door, and the Greek warriors climbed out. They opened the city gates, and the whole Greek army poured in.

Among the Trojans there was a prince named Aeneas. His mother was Venus, the goddess of love. That night, as he slept, he dreamed that he saw the ghost of Hector, the greatest Trojan hero, who had died in the war.

"Run, Aeneas!" said the ghost. "The enemy is inside the walls. Troy has fallen. Take the gods of Troy with you, and sail far away across the sea, and build a new city."

Aeneas woke up. He heard shouting and the clash of swords, and he climbed onto his roof. All over the city, fires were burning.

Aeneas grabbed his sword and ran out to fight. He and a few friends fought bravely through the burning streets. He saw the Greeks break down the doors of King Priam's palace. He saw that the old king could not be saved. Then he remembered his own family, alone at home: his old father, Anchises, his wife, Creusa, and his little son, Ascanius.

Just then his mother, Venus, appeared to him, shining in the darkness. "My son," she said, "why are you fighting? Troy is lost. The gods themselves have decided it. Go home to your family. I will keep you safe."

So Aeneas ran home through the flames. But his old father would not leave.

"Go, you who are young and strong," said Anchises. "I am too old to start again in a strange land. Leave me here."

Aeneas and Creusa and little Ascanius all begged him with tears in their eyes, but Anchises would not change his mind.

Then a wonderful thing happened. A soft flame of light appeared on top of little Ascanius's head. It flickered in his hair, but it did not burn him. His parents tried to put it out, but old Anchises lifted his hands to the sky in joy.

"Great Jupiter," he cried, "if this is a sign from you, show us another!"

At once there was a crash of thunder, and a shooting star streaked across the sky, trailing light behind it, and disappeared over the mountains.

"I am ready," said Anchises. "Lead the way, my son. I will go wherever you go."

"Then climb on my shoulders, Father," said Aeneas. "You won't be heavy for me. Whatever happens, we will be safe together, or not at all." He put a lion skin over his shoulders, and the old man climbed onto his back, holding the little statues of the gods of their home. Little Ascanius held his father's hand and trotted along beside him, taking two steps for every one of his father's. And Creusa followed behind.

Aeneas had never been afraid in battle. But now, carrying his family through the burning city, he jumped at every sound. They had almost reached the city gate when they heard soldiers coming, and Aeneas hurried away down a different path. When at last they were safely outside the walls, he turned around. Creusa was not there.

Aeneas left his father and son with his men and ran all the way back into the burning city to find her. He searched everywhere, calling her name. Then, suddenly, her ghost appeared in front of him, taller than she had been in life.

"Don't be so sad, my dear husband," she said gently. "This was the will of the gods. I am not going with you. You have a long journey ahead, over many seas. But at the end of it you will come to a land in the west, where a river called the Tiber flows, and there you will find happiness and a new kingdom. Now goodbye, and look after our son."

Aeneas tried three times to put his arms around her, and three times she slipped through them like a breath of air, like a dream. Then she was gone.

When Aeneas returned to the others, he found that a great crowd of Trojans had gathered there, men and women, all ready to follow him wherever he went. The morning star was rising over the mountains. Aeneas lifted his father onto his shoulders once more, and they set off toward the hills, to begin their long journey.
