Up on Mount Olympus, Venus went to Jupiter, the king of the gods, with tears in her eyes. "Father," she said, "what have my son Aeneas and the Trojans done wrong? Everywhere they go, the whole world is against them."

Jupiter smiled and kissed her. "Don't be afraid, my daughter," he said. "Nothing has changed. Aeneas will reach Italy. And one day his children's children will build a great city called Rome, and it will rule the world." Then he sent Mercury down to the city of Carthage, to make its people kind to the Trojans.

The next morning, Aeneas and his friend Achates went exploring. In the middle of a wood they met a young huntress, with a bow on her shoulder and her hair blowing in the wind. It was really Venus, Aeneas's mother, in disguise. But he did not know her.

"You must be lost," she said. "This is Africa, and the city nearby is Carthage. Its queen is called Dido. Let me tell you her story.

"Dido came from the city of Tyre, far across the sea. Her husband, Sychaeus, was the richest man in the land, and Dido loved him dearly. But her brother, the king of Tyre, was greedy for his gold, and he had Sychaeus killed in secret. Then the ghost of Sychaeus came to Dido in a dream. He told her what had happened, and where his treasure was hidden, and told her to run away.

"So Dido gathered all the people who hated her wicked brother, and they took the treasure and sailed away in ships. When they arrived here, they asked the people of this land to sell them some ground for a new city. But they did not ask for much. They asked for only as much land as could be covered by the hide of one bull.

"The people laughed and agreed. What could you build on a piece of land the size of a cowskin? But clever Dido cut the hide into strips, so thin they were almost like threads, and tied them end to end into one enormous long string. Then she laid it on the ground in a great circle, around a whole hill. And on that hill she built a fortress, and around it grew the city of Carthage."

As the huntress turned to go, a rosy light shone from her, a sweet smell came from her hair, and her robe flowed down to her feet. Aeneas knew her at last.

"Mother!" he cried. "Why do you always come in disguise? Why can't I ever hold your hand and talk to you face to face?"

But she was gone. Venus wrapped Aeneas and Achates in a magic mist, so that no one could see them, and they walked into Carthage.

The city was full of busy people. Some were building walls. Some were dragging stones. Some were digging a harbor. Others were building a theater. They worked like bees in summer. "How lucky they are," said Aeneas. "Their city is already rising."

In the middle of the city was a great temple of Juno, and on its walls were pictures. Aeneas stared. They were pictures of the Trojan War! There was Troy, and the Greek ships, and fierce Achilles in his chariot, and the Trojan heroes he knew. Tears ran down his cheeks. "Look, Achates," he said. "Even here, people know our story and feel sorry for us."

Then Queen Dido arrived, beautiful as the goddess Diana, with a crowd of young men around her. She sat on her throne and listened to her people. And as Aeneas watched from inside the mist, he saw some of his own lost sailors come in. Their ships had survived the storm after all! They told the queen of their troubles, and she answered them kindly.

"Don't be afraid," said Dido. "Everyone knows of Troy and its brave men. You may stay here and rest. And I wish your king Aeneas had come with you."

At that very moment the mist melted away, and there stood Aeneas, shining and handsome, for his mother had made him look like a god.

"Here I am," he said. "I am Aeneas of Troy. Thank you, Queen, for your kindness. I will never forget it, as long as the rivers run into the sea."

Dido was amazed. "I know what it is to be in trouble," she said. "I have learned to help people who are in trouble too." She invited Aeneas to her palace, and sent his men twenty oxen and a hundred sheep and lambs for a feast. That night there was a great banquet in the palace, with purple cushions and silver dishes and golden cups.

Now Venus was worried. What if the people of Carthage turned against her son? So she called her other son, Cupid, the god of love.

"Take off your wings," she told him, "and take the shape of little Ascanius, Aeneas's son. When Queen Dido hugs you, breathe love into her heart, so that she will love Aeneas and be kind to him."

So Cupid took little Ascanius's place at the feast. Dido held him on her lap and hugged him, not knowing that he was a god. And slowly she forgot her old husband, Sychaeus, and began to love Aeneas instead.

After dinner, Dido asked Aeneas to tell them all his adventures. So he told them about the wooden horse and the fall of Troy, and his long, long journey. Dido listened all night, and when it was over, she loved him more than ever.

The next day, Dido talked to her sister, Anna. "This stranger," she said, "how brave he is, and how much he has suffered! Since my husband died, I never thought I would love anyone again. But I think I love Aeneas."

"Then don't be sad all your life," said Anna. "Let him stay. The Trojans will make Carthage strong."

So Aeneas stayed. Dido showed him around her city, and every evening she asked him to tell his story again. Then Juno, who wanted to keep the Trojans out of Italy, made a plan with Venus. The next day there was a great hunt. Dido rode out on a fine horse, and Aeneas rode beside her, and little Ascanius galloped about on his pony, hoping to meet a wild boar or a lion. Then Juno sent a great storm, with thunder and hail. Everyone ran for shelter, and Dido and Aeneas found themselves alone in the same cave. There they promised to love each other. After that, Dido called Aeneas her husband.

Then Rumor began to fly. Rumor is a strange, fast creature, covered in feathers, and under every feather there is an eye and an ear and a tongue. She flew all over Africa, telling everyone that the queen and the Trojan had forgotten all about their duties because of love.

The news reached a king named Iarbas, who had wanted to marry Dido himself. He was furious, and he complained to Jupiter. And Jupiter sent Mercury flying down to Aeneas with a message.

Mercury found Aeneas helping to build the walls of Carthage, wearing a fine purple cloak that Dido had made him. "What are you doing?" said Mercury. "Have you forgotten your kingdom in Italy? If you don't care about it yourself, think of your son Ascanius. Italy is his, and Rome. Go!" And he vanished.

Aeneas was frightened and full of doubt. He knew he must obey the gods. But how could he tell Dido? He told his men to get the ships ready in secret.

But Dido found out. "Were you going to sneak away without even saying goodbye?" she cried. "Don't you love me?"

"I will never forget you, Dido, as long as I live," said Aeneas. "But I did not choose this. The gods are sending me to Italy. It is not my choice."

Dido did not believe him. She was angry, and then she was so sad that she fainted, and her maids carried her to her room. She sent her sister Anna to beg him to wait, just a little longer. But Aeneas stood as firm as an oak tree in the wind. He was sad too, but he would not change his mind.

That night, Mercury came to Aeneas in a dream. "Why are you sleeping? Go now, before the morning!" So Aeneas woke his men, cut the ropes with his sword, and the Trojan ships sailed away in the dark.

In the morning, Dido looked out from her tower and saw the ships far out at sea. Her heart broke. She had a great fire built in her courtyard, and on it she put everything that Aeneas had left behind, to burn it all. And there, in her grief, the queen of Carthage died.

Far out at sea, Aeneas looked back and saw a great glow of fire rising over Carthage. He did not know what it was. But he was afraid that something terrible had happened, and his heart was heavy as he sailed away.
