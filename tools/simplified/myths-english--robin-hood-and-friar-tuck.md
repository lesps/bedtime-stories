Early the next morning, Robin Hood set off to find the Curtal Friar of Fountain Dale, who would marry Allan a Dale to his true love, Ellen. He took Little John, Will Scarlet, David of Doncaster and Arthur a Bland with him. Under his green jacket he wore a coat of steel chain mail, and on his head a steel cap, and at his side hung a fine sword.

They walked all morning, over streams and along shady forest paths, until they came to a wide, slow river covered with lily pads. "Just around that bend there's a shallow ford," said Will Scarlet, "and on the other side is the little hut where the Friar lives."

"Wait here, lads," said Robin. "I want to have this adventure by myself. But if you hear my horn, come quickly." And off he went alone.

Just around the bend, Robin heard voices. Two men seemed to be talking below the river bank. But the strange thing was that both voices sounded exactly alike. Robin lay down on the grass and peeped over the edge.

Down below, in the cool shade of a willow tree, sat one big, strong man, and nobody else. His head was as round as a ball, with curly black hair, and a shaved bald patch on top. He wore a friar's robe and a string of prayer beads. His cheeks were as red as apples, and his little grey eyes twinkled with fun. Between his knees he held a huge meat pie, and he was munching away at it, with a big bottle of wine beside him.

"This," thought Robin, "is the merriest feast and the merriest fellow in all merry England."

When the Friar had finished eating, he wiped his greasy hands on the ferns, picked up his bottle, and began to talk to himself as if he were two people.

"Dear lad, you are the sweetest fellow in the world! Won't you have a drink? After you, lad, after you! Well, if you insist..." And he passed the bottle from one hand to the other, and drank enough for two.

Robin had to put his hand over his mouth to stop himself laughing out loud.

"Now, sweet lad, won't you sing me a song?" the Friar went on. "Oh, no, I croak like a frog today! Nonsense, your voice is as sweet as a bird's. Let's sing one together. You sing the girl's part, and I'll sing the boy's." And he began to sing a love song, with the boy's part in a deep, gruff voice and the girl's part in a high, squeaky one.

That was too much for Robin. He burst out laughing and joined in the chorus at the top of his voice. Together they bellowed, "So it's hark! hark! hark! to the joyous lark!" so loudly they could have been heard a mile away.

But the moment the song ended, the Friar jumped up, put on a steel cap, and pulled out a huge sword. "What spy is this? Come out, and I'll chop you up like meat for a pudding!"

"Put that away, friend," said Robin, jumping down beside him, still laughing. "People who sing so nicely together shouldn't fight. My throat is dry after all that singing. Is there any wine left in that bottle?"

The Friar grumbled, but he handed it over. Robin drank and drank, glug, glug, glug. The Friar snatched the bottle back, peered into it sadly, and finished off the last drop himself.

"Do you know the Curtal Friar of Fountain Dale?" asked Robin. "Is he on this side of the river or the other?"

"Find out for yourself," said the Friar.

"I'd like to cross the river and look for him," said Robin. "But I don't want to get my fine clothes wet. You have strong shoulders. Will you carry me across?"

The Friar's face went red with rage. "What! Me, the holy Tuck, carry a puny little peacock like you?" Then he stopped, and his eyes twinkled. "But why not?" he said sweetly. "The good Saint Christopher carried travellers across rivers. Come, I'll carry you. And let me carry your sword too, so it doesn't get wet."

So Robin gave the Friar his sword and climbed onto his back, and the Friar splashed across the river and set him down on the other side.

"Thank you, holy father," said Robin. "Now please give me my sword, for I'm in a hurry."

The Friar winked. "Oh, but I have business on the other side of the river," he said. "And I'm afraid if I get wet again I'll get aches in my joints. So now you must carry me back. You see, I have two swords, and you have none."

Robin bit his lip. "You cunning friar," he said. "You've caught me fair and square. Give me my sword, and I promise I'll carry you back."

So Robin carried the heavy Friar back across. He stumbled over the stones and stepped into holes, and the sweat ran down his face, while the Friar kicked him with his heels and called him names. But secretly, as he went, Robin felt for the buckle of the Friar's sword belt and loosened it. When they reached the bank and the Friar jumped down, Robin pulled, and the Friar's sword came away in his hands.

"Now," panted Robin, "I have two swords and you have none. Carry me back across, or I'll prick you full of holes!"

"I didn't think you were so clever," said the Friar grimly. "Give me my sword, and I promise to carry you." So Robin gave it back, and the Friar took him on his back and waded into the river, without a word.

But when they reached the middle, where the water was deepest, the Friar gave a sudden heave of his shoulders and threw Robin right over his head, like a sack of grain. Splash! Down went Robin into the river.

"There," said the Friar calmly, wading back to the shore. "That should cool you off."

Robin came up spluttering, with water running out of his ears. Then he was furious. He splashed to the bank and drew his sword, and the Friar drew his, and Robin saw that the Friar wore a coat of chain mail under his robe too. And they fought, up and down and back and forth, with a great clashing of swords, for an hour or more. But neither could beat the other, and neither was hurt.

At last Robin cried, "Stop, friend! Let me blow three times on my horn."

"I think you have some trick in mind," said the Friar. "But I'm not afraid. Blow your horn, if I can blow my whistle three times too."

So Robin blew his horn, and around the bend came running Little John, Will Scarlet, David and Arthur, with arrows ready on their bows.

"So that's your trick!" cried the Friar, and he blew a shrill blast on his silver whistle. Out of the bushes burst four huge shaggy hounds. "Get him, Sweet Lips! Get him, Bell Throat! Get him, Beauty! Get him, Fangs!"

Robin dropped his sword and scrambled up a tree just in time, and the dogs sat around it looking up at him, like dogs looking at a cat on a roof. Then the Friar sent the dogs at the other outlaws. They shot their arrows, but the old ballad says that each dog jumped aside, caught the arrow in its mouth and bit it in two!

It would have gone badly for them, but Will Scarlet stepped forward. "Fangs! Beauty! Down! What does this mean?"

At once the dogs stopped and came wagging up to him and licked his hands, because they knew him well.

"What!" cried the Friar. "Is that young Master William Gamwell, in such company?"

"Yes, Tuck," said Will. "But I'm Will Scarlet now, and this is my uncle, Robin Hood."

"Robin Hood!" said the Friar, and he held out his great hand. "I've often heard of you, but I never thought I'd fight you. I'm sorry. No wonder I found such a strong man against me!"

"But we're wasting time," said Robin. "I still have to find the Curtal Friar."

Will Scarlet laughed and pointed. "Uncle, he's standing right beside you!"

"What? You're the man I've been searching for all day, and got such a ducking for?"

"Some people call me the Curtal Friar of Fountain Dale," said the Friar, with a twinkle. "And some call me simply Friar Tuck."

"I like that name best," said Robin. "But why didn't you tell me who you were?"

"You never asked," said Friar Tuck.

So they all set off back to Sherwood, with the big dogs at their heels. And on the way, Robin told Friar Tuck all about Allan a Dale and his true love, Ellen.
