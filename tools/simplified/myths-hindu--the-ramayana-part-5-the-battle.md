Rama's army had crossed the sea to Lanka, and the great battle had begun. Ravana hid behind the walls of his city. But he had one more warrior to send out: his giant brother Kumbhakarna, who slept for months at a time.

When Kumbhakarna woke from his long sleep, Ravana said to him, "Only you can save us now." "Go out and drive Rama's army away!"

Kumbhakarna was as tall as a mountain. He strode out through the gates of Lanka and roared, and the mountains shook, and the sea roared back. The monkeys were so frightened that they began to run away.

But the monkey prince Angada stopped them. "Where are you running to?" he shouted. "Turn round and fight!" So the monkeys came back, and threw stones and trees and rocks at the giant. But the rocks broke into pieces against his iron body, and the monkeys fled again.

Brave Hanuman did not run. He threw a huge rock at Kumbhakarna, but the giant knocked it away with his spear and struck Hanuman down. Then the monkey chiefs came at him, one after another, and he struck them all down too. Prince Angada came, and King Sugriva came, and Kumbhakarna threw them both to the ground, and they lay there stunned.

At last the giant came to Lakshmana. "You are a brave warrior," he said. "But I want to fight a greater one. Let me go and fight your brother Rama."

"Go, then," said Lakshmana. "But Rama will be the end of you."

And so it was. Kumbhakarna fought Rama, and Rama's flaming arrows struck him again and again, until at last the giant fell. His great body tumbled into the sea, and the waves rose up as if in a storm.

Then Ravana's son Indrajit, a great magician, fought three battles against Rama's army. And at last Lakshmana beat him, and Indrajit was killed.

When Ravana heard the women of Lanka weeping for his son, he swore to have his revenge. He called out all his warriors, with their spears and swords and clubs, their elephants and chariots and horses, their drums and trumpets. And Ravana rode out of the gates of Lanka in his chariot. He never came back through those gates again.

The sun grew dim, and darkness fell. Vultures swooped over the battlefield, and the sea heaved, and the earth shook. And the battle began. Many of Ravana's chiefs were beaten by King Sugriva and Prince Angada. But Ravana himself swept through the army of the monkeys, until he saw Lakshmana.

Then Ravana's brother Vibhishana, who was fighting on Rama's side, killed Ravana's horses. Ravana leaped down from his chariot, and hurled a huge spear at Vibhishana. But Lakshmana cut the spear in two before it could hit him.

"You have saved that traitor," said Ravana to Lakshmana. "Now save yourself! You killed my son, and now I will have my revenge." And he threw a terrible spear at Lakshmana, and Lakshmana fell to the ground and lay still.

Rama saw his brother fall, and he rushed at Ravana in fury. And Ravana fled from him.

Rama knelt by his brother. "Lakshmana, have you fallen?" he wept. "You followed me from Ayodhya into the deepest forest, and stood by me in every battle. There is no love like a brother's love. If you are gone, how can I go on? What shall I say to your mother?"

But the wise Susena comforted him, and Hanuman flew to the far-off mountains and brought back herbs that could heal. And Rama looked after his brother, until Lakshmana opened his eyes and stood up, strong again.

Now Ravana came back in a new chariot, shining like the sun. The gods were watching the battle, and Indra, the king of the gods, saw that Rama was fighting on foot. "Quick, Matali," said Indra to his charioteer. "Take my chariot to Rama, for Ravana's days are over. The gods help those who are brave and true."

So Matali drove Indra's chariot down from the sky, pulled by horses as bright as sunbeams. He gave Rama heavenly weapons, and armour made by the builder of the gods. "I will drive for you," he said. "Now go and defeat Ravana!"

Then Rama and Ravana fought, Rama in Indra's chariot and Ravana in his own. Their arrows were so thick that they hid the sky. They fought on and on, wounded and tired, but neither would give in. The wind stopped blowing in fear, and the sun grew pale.

At last Rama took up a weapon that the sage Agastya had given him, a weapon of the god Brahma, which flew like lightning and burned with heavenly fire. He shot it from his bow, and it pierced Ravana's heart. And Ravana fell, and moved no more.

The Rakshasas cried out in terror, and the monkeys shouted for joy. Flowers fell like rain from the sky, and heavenly music sounded. The sea danced, the sun shone brightly, and a soft, sweet-smelling breeze blew over the land. And a voice from the sky said, "Rama, champion of the good, your work is done!"

Ravana's queen, Mandodari, wept for her husband. "No ordinary man could have beaten you," she said. "Rama must be the god Vishnu himself. I prayed that Sita would go back to her husband. But you would not let her go, and so you have lost everything."

Rama was kind, even to his fallen enemy. He ordered that Ravana should have a king's funeral, with sweet-smelling wood and garlands and rich robes. Vibhishana lit the fire. And Rama unstrung his bow, and laid down his weapons, for the war was over.
