When Odysseus left the island of the enchantress Circe, she gave him some good advice.

"First you will pass the Sirens," she said. "They sing songs so beautiful that sailors who hear them jump into the sea to swim to them, and are never seen again. Put wax in your men's ears so they cannot hear. If you want to hear the song yourself, have your men tie you to the mast, and tell them not to untie you, no matter what you say."

So as they sailed near the Sirens' island, Odysseus softened some wax and pressed it into his men's ears. Then they tied him tightly to the mast.

Across the water came the Sirens' song. It was the most beautiful music Odysseus had ever heard. "Untie me!" he shouted. "Let me go to them!" But his men could not hear him, and they kept rowing, and they tied the ropes even tighter. At last the song faded away behind them, and they were safe.

Next came a narrow strait of water with a monster on each side.

On one side, in a cave high up on a cliff, lived Scylla. She had six long necks, and on each neck was a head with sharp teeth. She reached down and snatched sailors off passing ships.

On the other side lived Charybdis, a giant whirlpool. Three times a day, she sucked down all the water, and anything floating on it, and then spat it all out again.

Odysseus steered away from the roaring whirlpool. But while everyone was watching Charybdis, Scylla darted her six heads down from her cave and snatched six of his men. It was the saddest thing Odysseus had ever seen. But the rest of the ship got through.

Circe had warned him about one more thing. "You will come to the island of the Sun," she had said. "There the Sun keeps his cattle. Whatever happens, do not eat them."

The men were tired and begged to rest on the island, and Odysseus let them, but he made them promise not to touch the cattle. Then the wind stopped blowing, and they could not leave. For a whole month they were stuck there. Their food ran out. They caught fish and birds, but they were always hungry.

One day, while Odysseus was asleep, his men gave in. They took some of the Sun's cattle, cooked them and ate them.

When they finally sailed away, the gods sent a terrible storm. Lightning split the mast. The ship broke to pieces, and all of Odysseus's men were lost. Only Odysseus survived, holding on to a piece of wood, floating on the sea.

The waves carried him to the island of a sea nymph named Calypso. She was kind to him and took care of him. She wanted him to stay with her forever. "Stay," she said, "and I will make you live forever, like a god."

But Odysseus only wanted to go home to Penelope and Telemachus. Every day he sat on the beach and looked out to sea toward home.

At last Zeus sent his messenger, Hermes, to Calypso. "Zeus says you must let Odysseus go," said Hermes.

Calypso was sad, but she obeyed. She helped Odysseus build a raft, filled it with food and water, and sent a good wind to carry him.

He sailed for many days. But just when he could see land, a great storm smashed his raft to bits. A kind sea nymph flew down to him, looking like a seabird. She gave him a magic veil. "Wrap this around you and swim," she said. "It will keep you from drowning."

So Odysseus swam and swam, through the wild waves, toward an unknown shore.
