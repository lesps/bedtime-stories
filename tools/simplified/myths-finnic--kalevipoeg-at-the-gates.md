Kalevipoeg, the son of Kalev, was now the king of Estonia. One day, he was feasting with his friends Alevipoeg and Sulevipoeg, when news came that enemies had invaded the country.

That night, the three friends dug a deep pit in a secret place, and buried the king's treasure in it, so that it would be safe. Kalevipoeg put it into the care of the god Taara, and sang magic songs over it. And they say that nobody has ever found it, because the man who can dig it up has not yet been born.

In the morning, Kalevipoeg took his spear and his sword, and blew his horn. The sound echoed through every town and forest in Estonia, and the people came from all over the land to follow their king. Then there were great battles, which lasted many days. Kalevipoeg drove the enemies away. But many brave men were lost, and among them were both of his friends. Sulevipoeg fell in battle. And one day Alevipoeg bent down to drink from a lake, and slipped, and fell into the water, and was drowned. They say that on sunny days you can still see his great iron helmet and his sword shining at the bottom of the lake.

Kalevipoeg was so sad that he gave up his kingdom to his friend Olev, and went away to live alone in a little hut by a river in the pine forest, where he caught fish and crayfish to eat.

But people would not leave him in peace. So one day he wandered away through the forests. And three days later he came to the shore of Lake Peipus, and to the brook called Käpä.

Now, long before, Kalevipoeg had lost his great sword in that very brook. A wicked sorcerer had stolen it while he slept, and dropped it into the water. And Kalevipoeg had left it there, with a command: that if the man who had brought it there ever came that way again, the sword should cut off his feet. He meant the sorcerer. But he had not said so clearly. And the sword was confused, because long before, a smith in Finland had put a curse on it.

Kalevipoeg had forgotten all about this. He came singing to the brook, and waded in. And the great sword rose up out of the water, and struck at his legs. Kalevipoeg cried out and dragged himself to the shore. And there, on the bank of the brook, the great hero died.

His soul flew up like a happy bird to the halls of Taara in the sky. There he sat among the heroes by the fire, resting his cheek on his hand, and listening to the singers sing about his great deeds.

But the father of the gods knew that a hero as great as Kalevipoeg could not sit still in heaven for ever. Once, Kalevipoeg had fought the wicked lord of the underworld, Sarvik, and bound him in chains. So the gods met together for many days and nights, and at last they decided what he should do. He should guard the gates of the underworld, so that Sarvik could never break free.

So Kalevipoeg's soul flew down from the sky like a bird, and went back into his body. But even all the gods could not make his legs whole again. So they set him on a white horse, and sent him to the gates of the underworld.

When Kalevipoeg came to the rocky gate, a voice called out from heaven: "Strike the rock with your fist!" He struck it, and the rock split open, and his right hand was caught fast in the crack.

And there he sits to this day, on his white horse at the gates of the underworld, guarding the chains of the demons, while he is held fast himself. The demons keep piling burning wood around their chains to soften them, but every morning when the cock crows, their chains grow thick and strong again. And sometimes Kalevipoeg tries to pull his hand free from the rock, until the earth shakes and the sea foams. But he cannot get loose.

But one day, they say, a great fire will blaze up on both sides of the rock and melt it. And then Kalevipoeg will pull out his hand, and come back to the earth, and bring a new time of happiness to the people of Estonia.
