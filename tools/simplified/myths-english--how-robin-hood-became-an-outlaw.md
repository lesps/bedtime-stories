Long ago in Merry England, when King Henry the Second ruled the land, there lived in Sherwood Forest, near the town of Nottingham, a famous outlaw named Robin Hood. No archer ever lived who could shoot an arrow as well as he could, and no band was ever as merry as the seven score men who lived with him in the greenwood. They took from the rich who had cheated the poor, and they gave to the poor who needed help, and the country people loved them. This is how it all began.

When Robin was eighteen years old, the Sheriff of Nottingham announced an archery contest, with a barrel of ale for the prize. "I'll go too," said Robin. So he took his good yew bow and a bundle of arrows and set off from Locksley Town, through Sherwood Forest, toward Nottingham. It was a fine May morning, and he whistled as he walked, thinking of Maid Marian and her bright eyes.

Under a great oak tree he came upon fifteen of the King's foresters, eating a huge pie and drinking ale. "Hello, little lad!" called one of them, with his mouth full. "Where are you going with your penny bow and your farthing arrows?"

Robin was cross, because no young man likes being laughed at. "My bow and arrows are as good as yours," he said. "And I am going to the shooting match at Nottingham."

The foresters roared with laughter. "Listen to the baby!" they said.

Robin grew angrier. "See that herd of deer at the end of the clearing?" he said. "I bet you twenty marks that I can hit one from here."

"Done!" said the forester who had spoken first.

So Robin strung his bow, drew the arrow back to his ear, and let it fly. It sped down the clearing like a hawk, and the finest deer in the herd fell. "Well?" said Robin. "I think I have won the bet."

But the foresters were furious. "Get away from here," said the first one, "or I'll beat you black and blue!"

Robin said nothing. He turned and walked away into the forest. But the forester who had lost the bet had drunk too much ale, and he was so angry that he snatched up his bow and shot an arrow after Robin. It whizzed past Robin's head, three inches away. Robin spun round and shot back, and his arrow did not miss. The forester fell, and he never got up again.

Before the others could catch him, Robin had vanished into the deep greenwood. But his heart was sick. "I wish you had never spoken to me," he cried, "and I wish I had never passed your way! I struck in a hurry, and I will be sorry for a long, long time."

Now Robin was an outlaw, because he had killed a man and shot the King's deer. A reward of two hundred pounds was offered to whoever caught him. And the Sheriff of Nottingham swore he would catch him himself, because he wanted the money, and because the forester had been his relative.

So Robin hid in Sherwood Forest. And during that year, many others came to join him, men who had been driven from their homes. Some had shot a deer in winter when their families were starving. Some had had their farms taken from them by greedy lords and rich abbots. They chose Robin to be their leader, and they made a promise. They would take back from the cruel lords and greedy rich men what had been taken from the poor, and they would help the poor whenever they were in need. And they promised never to hurt a child or a woman.

One fine morning, Robin said, "We've had no fun for two weeks. I'm off to look for adventure. Stay here, but listen for my horn. If I blow it three times, come quickly."

He walked until he came to a stream with a narrow log bridge across it. As he stepped onto one end of the bridge, a tall stranger stepped onto the other end.

"Stand back," said Robin, "and let the better man cross first."

"Stand back yourself," said the stranger, "for I am the better man."

"We'll see about that," said Robin, and he fitted an arrow to his bow.

"You talk like a coward," said the stranger, "standing there with your bow, when I have nothing but a stick."

"No one has ever called me a coward," said Robin. He put down his bow and cut himself a good oak stick, six feet long. And as he trimmed it, he looked at the stranger. He was a head and a neck taller than Robin, and twice as broad. In fact he was seven feet tall!

"Now," said Robin, "we'll fight on the bridge until one of us falls into the stream."

"That suits me," said the stranger, twirling his staff until it whistled.

Never was there such a fight! Whack! Crack! Thwack! They hit and dodged and hit again, until at last the stranger gave Robin such a blow that he tumbled head over heels into the stream with a great splash.

"And where are you now, my lad?" roared the stranger, laughing.

"Floating down the stream," said Robin, laughing too. He climbed out, dripping. "Give me your hand. You are a brave fellow and a fine fighter. My head is humming like a hive of bees!" And he blew three blasts on his horn.

At once his merry men came running through the trees, dressed in Lincoln green. "Master!" cried Will Stutely. "You're soaked to the skin!"

"That fellow knocked me into the water," said Robin.

"Then he shall have a ducking too!" said Will. And twenty of them jumped on the stranger. He knocked several of them down before they could hold him.

"Stop!" cried Robin, laughing until his sides ached. "He is a good, brave man, and no harm shall come to him. Listen, friend. Will you stay and join my band? You shall have three suits of Lincoln green a year, and a share of everything we have."

"Only if you can shoot better than me," said the stranger.

So they set up a target, and the stranger shot an arrow right into the very middle. Everyone clapped. Then Robin shot, and his arrow hit the stranger's arrow and split it in two!

"That is a shot indeed!" cried the stranger. "I will join you gladly. Men call me John Little."

"John Little?" said Will Stutely, who loved a joke. "But you are so very small! We shall call you Little John instead."

The big man frowned. But Robin said, "The name suits you. Little John you shall be." So they all went back to their home deep in the forest, where they lived in huts of bark and branches under a great oak tree, and they had a feast. And afterwards, they gave the giant a christening. They held him tight, and a bald man pretending to be a priest poured a whole jug of ale over his head, while everyone laughed. "Your name," said the pretend priest, "is Little John."

And so Robin Hood became an outlaw, and gathered his merry men, and found his best friend, Little John.
