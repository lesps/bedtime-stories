In the last days of King Arthur, there was a war between Arthur and Sir Lancelot, and King Arthur sailed across the sea to fight it. While he was away, he left his nephew Sir Mordred to rule England.

But Mordred was a traitor. He sent out letters saying that King Arthur had been killed in battle, and he had himself crowned king. He even wanted to take Queen Guinevere for his own wife. The queen hated him, but she was clever. She told him she needed to go to London first, to buy things for the wedding. And as soon as she got to London, she shut herself up in the Tower of London with her soldiers, and however hard Mordred tried, he could not get her out.

The Bishop of Canterbury told Mordred to stop his wicked plans, and Mordred was so angry that the bishop had to run away and hide.

Then news came that King Arthur was hurrying home across the sea. Mordred told the people that King Arthur would only bring them war, and that life would be easy and happy with him as king. And many people believed him and followed him. So when King Arthur's ships reached Dover, Mordred and his army were waiting for them. There was a great battle on the beach, and in the end Mordred and his men were driven back, and ran away.

After the battle, Arthur found his nephew Sir Gawain lying in a boat, badly hurt. "Of all the men in the world, I loved you best," said the king, "you and Sir Lancelot."

"Uncle," said Gawain, "my time has come, and it is my own fault. If Sir Lancelot were still with you, this war would never have happened, and I am the reason he isn't. Please bring me pen and paper, so that I can write to him."

So King Arthur held Gawain up while he wrote a letter to Lancelot. "Come quickly," Gawain wrote, "for King Arthur needs you. My brother Mordred has taken his crown." And when the letter was finished, Gawain died. King Arthur buried him in the chapel of Dover Castle.

Mordred gathered a great army, and the two armies met on a wide plain near Salisbury. But the night before the battle, Sir Gawain appeared to King Arthur in a dream. "Don't fight tomorrow," he said, "or you will be killed. Make peace with Mordred for a month. By then, Sir Lancelot will have come to help you."

So the next day, King Arthur sent messengers to Mordred, and Mordred agreed to make peace. It was arranged that King Arthur and Mordred would meet in the open ground between the two armies, each with fourteen men, to seal the peace.

"But if you see anybody draw a sword," Arthur told his army, "come at once, because I don't trust Mordred." And Mordred said exactly the same thing to his own army.

So King Arthur and Mordred met, and they agreed on the peace, and they drank wine together, and all was well. But while they were drinking, an adder crept out of a bush and bit one of the knights on the foot. Without thinking, the knight drew his sword to kill the snake.

When the two armies saw the flash of that sword, they thought it meant treachery. They blew their trumpets and horns and shouted, and charged at each other. And so the last battle began, the saddest battle that was ever fought. It went on all day long. And at the end of the day, King Arthur looked around, and of all his knights, only two were still alive: Sir Lucan and his brother, Sir Bedivere.

"Alas, that I should live to see this day," said the king. "But where is that traitor Mordred, who has caused all this?"

Then he saw Mordred, leaning on his sword. "Give me my spear," said Arthur.

"Let him go, my lord," begged Sir Lucan. "Remember your dream! You have won the battle. If you stop now, the danger will pass."

"Whatever happens to me," said the king, "he shall not escape." And he ran at Mordred with his spear, and struck him. And Mordred, as he fell, struck the king a terrible blow on the head with his sword.

Sir Lucan and Sir Bedivere carried the king to a little chapel nearby. But Sir Lucan had been badly hurt in the battle himself, and as he tried to lift the king, he fell down at Arthur's feet and died.

"He has died trying to help me," said the king sadly, "and he needed help more than I did. Now, Sir Bedivere, stop crying, and listen. Take my sword, Excalibur, down to the lake over there, and throw it into the water. Then come back and tell me what you saw."

"I will, my lord," said Bedivere. But when he looked at the beautiful sword, with its handle covered in gold and jewels, he thought, "It would be a terrible waste to throw this away." So he hid Excalibur under a tree, and went back to the king.

"What did you see?" asked Arthur.

"Nothing but the wind and the waves," said Bedivere.

"You are not telling me the truth," said the king. "Go back and do as I told you."

But again Bedivere could not bear to throw the sword away. He hid it again, and came back, and said he had seen nothing but the dark water.

"That is twice you have deceived me!" cried the king. "Go now, and do as I say!"

Then Bedivere was ashamed. He took the sword from its hiding place, and went down to the water's edge, and threw it far out over the lake with all his strength. And as it flew through the air, an arm and a hand rose up out of the water, and caught Excalibur by the handle, and waved it three times, and then sank down with it under the water.

Bedivere went back and told the king what he had seen. "Help me down to the water," said Arthur, "for I have stayed here too long." So Bedivere carried him on his back down to the lake. And there, by the shore, was a little boat full of beautiful ladies in black hoods, with a queen among them. And they all wept when they saw King Arthur.

"Put me in the boat," said the king. Bedivere laid him gently in it, and the ladies cried, and the boat moved away from the shore.

"Oh, my lord Arthur," cried Bedivere, "what will become of me now, left here all alone?"

"Be brave," said the king, "and do the best you can. I am going to the valley of Avalon, to be healed of my wound. And if you never hear of me again, pray for me."

And Bedivere watched the boat until it was out of sight.

When Queen Guinevere heard about the battle, she went to live in a convent and became a nun, and she spent the rest of her life in prayer, sorrowing for King Arthur. Sir Lancelot came over the sea with his army when he got Gawain's letter, but he came too late. He went to see Guinevere one last time, and then he became a hermit, together with Sir Bedivere and the Bishop of Canterbury, and lived a quiet life of prayer.

When Lancelot died, some years later, his old friend Sir Ector stood beside him and said, "Lancelot, you were the bravest and most courteous knight who ever held a sword, and the most faithful friend who ever rode a horse."
