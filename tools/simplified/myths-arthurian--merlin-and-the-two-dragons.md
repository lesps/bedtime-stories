Long ago, before the time of King Arthur, a man called Vortigern made himself king of Britain by trickery. To help him fight his enemies, he invited a fierce people called the Saxons to come over the sea. But the Saxons turned against him, and took much of his land, and now Vortigern was afraid.

He asked his magicians what to do. "Build yourself a great strong tower," they said, "where you will be safe."

So Vortigern went looking for the right place, and he chose a mountain called Erir. He gathered workmen from all over the country, and they began to lay the foundations of the tower. But a strange thing happened. Whatever the workmen built in the day, the earth swallowed up in the night, so that the next morning there was nothing left to see.

Vortigern asked his magicians why this was. They told him that he must find a boy who had never had a father, and offer him up as a sacrifice. Only then, they said, would the tower stand.

So Vortigern sent messengers all over the land to look for such a boy. At last they came to a town that was later called Kaermerdin. Some boys were playing in front of the town gate, and the messengers, who were tired from their journey, sat down to watch.

Towards evening, two of the boys began to quarrel. Their names were Merlin and Dabutius.

"How dare you quarrel with me, you fool?" said Dabutius. "We are not equals! My father and my mother were both from royal families. But nobody knows who you are. You never had a father!"

When the messengers heard that, they looked hard at Merlin, and asked the people standing nearby who he was. They said nobody knew who his father was. But his mother was the daughter of a king in South Wales, and she lived among the nuns at the church of Saint Peter in the town.

The messengers hurried off, and brought Merlin and his mother to King Vortigern. The king welcomed Merlin's mother kindly, because she was a princess, and asked her who Merlin's father was.

"My lord," she said, "I do not know of any man who was his father. All I know is this. When I was a young girl, a beautiful young man used to appear to me in my room, and talk to me, and then suddenly vanish. Many times he came, and sometimes I could hear him but not see him. And after that, I had my baby. I know no other father for him than that young man."

The king was amazed, and he sent for a wise man called Maugantius, to ask him if such a thing could be true.

"I have read in the books of the wise," said Maugantius, "that between the moon and the earth there live spirits, who are partly like men and partly like angels. They can take the shape of men whenever they please. Perhaps one of them came to this lady, and he was the boy's father."

Merlin had been listening to all of this. Now he went up to the king. "Why have my mother and I been brought here?" he asked.

"My magicians told me to find a boy who had no father," said Vortigern, "and to sacrifice him, to make my tower stand."

"Then tell your magicians to come here," said Merlin, "and I will prove that they are wrong."

The king was surprised, but he sent for the magicians, and they sat down in front of Merlin.

"You don't know what is stopping the tower from standing," Merlin said to them. "So you said that I must die, as if that would make it stand. But tell me this: what is under the foundations? For something is down there that will not let the tower stand."

The magicians were frightened, and said nothing.

"My lord," said Merlin to the king, "tell your workmen to dig down into the ground. You will find a pool of water underneath, and that is what makes the foundations sink."

The workmen dug, and there, deep under the ground, was a pool of water.

Then Merlin turned to the magicians again. "Now tell me, you deceivers, what is under the pool?" But they did not say a word.

"My lord," said Merlin to the king, "tell your men to drain the pool. At the bottom of it you will find two hollow stones, and in them, two dragons, fast asleep."

The king believed him now, and he had the pool drained. And it was just as Merlin had said. Everyone was amazed at his wisdom, and thought it must be a gift from heaven.

Vortigern sat down on the bank of the drained pool to watch. And out of the hollow stones came the two dragons. One was white, and the other was red. They rushed at each other and began a terrible fight, breathing out fire. At first the white dragon was stronger, and it chased the red dragon to the far end of the pool. But the red dragon was so upset at being chased that it turned and fought back, and drove the white dragon away.

When the fight was over, the king asked Merlin what it meant. And Merlin burst into tears, and began to prophesy.

"Sad times are coming for the red dragon," he said, "for it will soon be driven out. The white dragon stands for the Saxons, whom you invited here, and the red dragon stands for the Britons, and the white dragon will take their land. There will be war, and the churches will be ruined.

"But in the end, those who have been beaten will rise up against the strangers. For a boar from Cornwall will come to help them, and he will trample their enemies under his feet. The islands of the ocean will be his, and he will rule the forests of France. Even Rome will be afraid of his courage, and nobody will know how he comes to his end. People will talk about him for ever, and stories of his great deeds will be told and told again."

Merlin went on to say much more, in riddles that are hard to understand. But people have always believed that the boar from Cornwall was King Arthur, who was still to come.
