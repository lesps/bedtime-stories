Demeter was the goddess who made everything grow. She made the wheat grow tall and the apples grow sweet. She took care of every field and garden in the whole world.

Demeter had a daughter named Persephone, and she loved her more than anything. She hardly ever let Persephone go out alone.

But one summer the crops were growing too slowly, and Demeter had to hurry off to help them. She climbed into her flying chariot, pulled by two winged dragons.

"May I go and play with the sea nymphs while you're gone?" asked Persephone.

"Yes," said her mother. "But stay with them. Don't go wandering in the fields by yourself."

The sea nymphs had green hair and lived under the waves. They brought Persephone pretty shells, and she sat on the sand and played with them. But the nymphs could not come up onto dry land, or they would dry out like seaweed in the sun.

"Wait here," said Persephone. "I'll pick some flowers and make you each a crown."

She ran into the fields. The first flowers she found had already faded, so she went a little farther, and then a little farther. She found violets as big as her hand, and roses of every color. Then she saw a bush she had never seen before. It had a hundred flowers on it, all bright and shiny, and all a little different.

"That bush wasn't there a moment ago," thought Persephone. "How strange." But it was so beautiful that she wanted to take it home for her mother's garden. She grabbed it and pulled.

The ground began to crack. She pulled again, and a hole opened up. It grew wider and wider and deeper and deeper. From down in the hole came a rumbling, like horses' hooves and rolling wheels.

Up out of the hole burst a golden chariot, pulled by four black horses with smoke snorting from their noses. In the chariot sat a man with a dark, stern face and a crown of jewels.

"Don't be afraid," he said, trying to smile. "Would you like a ride in my chariot?"

"Mother!" cried Persephone. "Mother, come and save me!"

But Demeter was far away. The man jumped down, lifted Persephone into the chariot, and the horses galloped off.

"Why are you so frightened?" he said. "I am Hades, the king of everything under the ground. All the gold and diamonds in the earth are mine. My palace is made of gold, with diamond lamps. You may have anything you like there."

"I don't want a golden palace," sobbed Persephone. "I want to go home!"

"My palace is gloomy and lonely," said Hades. "It needs a happy little girl to run up and down the stairs and make it cheerful."

"I will never smile again," said Persephone, "until you take me home to my mother."

As they raced along, Persephone looked out and saw her mother far away in a wheat field. She screamed as loudly as she could. Then the chariot rushed down a dark road between the rocks, and the sunlight disappeared.

At the gate of his kingdom lay Hades' dog, Cerberus. He was a huge, ugly dog with three heads. "He won't bite you," said Hades, "unless you try to leave without my permission."

They crossed a bridge over a dark, slow river. "This is the River of Forgetting," said Hades. "If you take one sip, you will forget your mother, and you will be happy here."

"No!" cried Persephone. "I would rather be sad and remember my mother than be happy and forget her."

Hades told his cook to make every kind of treat. But Persephone would not eat a single bite. There was a rule in Hades' kingdom: anyone who eats food there can never leave.

Far away, Demeter had heard one faraway scream. She flew home on her dragons, but the house was empty. She ran to the beach.

"Where is Persephone?" she cried.

"She went to pick flowers," said the sea nymphs, "and she never came back."

So Demeter lit a magic torch that would burn until her daughter came home. For nine days and nine nights she searched. She knocked on every door. She asked the tree spirits and the fauns in the woods. Nobody had seen Persephone.

On the tenth day she found a gloomy cave where Hecate, the saddest goddess in the world, sat among dead leaves.

"Nine days ago I heard a girl scream," said Hecate. "And I heard wheels rumbling."

The two of them went to ask Apollo, the bright god of the sun. He sees everything that happens under the sky.

"Oh yes, I saw her," said Apollo, strumming his harp. "Hades took her to his palace under the ground. Don't worry. It's a very grand palace."

"Will you help me bring her back?" begged Demeter.

"I'm sorry, I'm far too busy," said Apollo. "But I will write a lovely song about your sadness."

"You have a harp where your heart should be," said Demeter, and she went on alone.

She was so tired and sad that she looked like an old woman. One day she came to the palace of a king and queen whose baby boy was sick. Demeter offered to be his nurse. She loved him like her own child, and he grew healthy and strong.

Every night, Demeter laid the baby in the warm, glowing coals of the fire. The fire did not hurt him at all. Little by little, it was making him live forever, like a god. But one night the queen peeked in and saw her baby lying in the fire. She screamed and snatched him out.

"Oh, foolish mother," sighed Demeter. "He would have lived forever. Now he will grow up to be a fine man, but only a man." And she left the palace and went on searching.

Now Demeter was angrier and sadder than ever. "Nothing will grow," she said, "not one blade of grass, until my daughter comes home."

So nothing grew. The fields turned brown. The trees had no apples. People and animals grew hungry all over the world. They begged Demeter to let the plants grow again.

"Not until my daughter walks on the earth again," she said.

At last Hermes, the quick messenger of the gods, flew down with his winged cap and sandals. He leaped right over Cerberus and ran into Hades' palace.

Persephone had been there for six months. She still had not eaten anything. But she was a cheerful girl, and she could not stay sad all the time. She explored the thousand rooms of the palace. She even began to feel a little sorry for lonely King Hades.

"I wish you could like me a little," Hades often said.

"You should have tried to make me like you before you carried me off," said Persephone. But one day she whispered, "I like you a little."

"Is there anything you would eat?" Hades asked her.

"Only a slice of my mother's bread," said Persephone, "or some fruit from her garden."

Hades sent a servant to find fruit. But because the plants had stopped growing, all he could find was one old, dried-up pomegranate.

"Take it away," said Persephone. "I won't touch it."

But when the servant was gone, she picked it up just to smell it. It had been so long since she had smelled fruit. And before she knew it, she had bitten into it. Six little seeds were in her mouth.

Just then the door opened, and in came Hades with Hermes.

"Persephone," said Hades, "I have done a wrong thing by keeping you here. Go home to your mother." And he looked very sad.

Persephone felt sorry for him. She almost told him about the seeds, but Hermes whispered, "Hurry, before he changes his mind!"

They rushed past the barking three-headed dog and up into the sunshine. Everywhere Persephone stepped, flowers popped up. Grass turned green behind her. Wheat sprouted in the fields. The birds sang for joy.

Demeter was sitting sadly on her doorstep. Suddenly her magic torch flickered and went out. She looked up and saw the brown fields turning green.

"Who dares to make the earth green," she said, "before my daughter comes home?"

"Open your arms, Mother," cried a voice, "and you will see!"

Persephone ran into her mother's arms. They hugged and cried and laughed all at once.

Then Demeter asked, "My child, did you eat anything in Hades' palace?"

"Only six pomegranate seeds," said Persephone, "this very morning."

Demeter's face fell. "Then for each seed, you must go back to Hades for one month every year. Six months with me, and six months with him."

"Don't be too cross with Hades," said Persephone, kissing her. "He was wrong to take me. But he is lonely, and he was kind in his way. I can stay with him for six months, as long as I can be with you for the other six."

And so it was. The old Greeks said that every year, when Persephone goes down under the ground, her mother is sad, and the plants stop growing, and it is winter. And every spring, when Persephone comes home, the flowers spring up again to welcome her.
