namesNote: Morrison calls him Manannan Mac y Leirr, his Manx name. The three running legs on the flag of the Isle of Man are the same sign.

Long ago, the Isle of Man was ruled by a great wizard called Manannan.

One day, Saint Patrick came riding over the sea from Ireland on his snow-white horse. As he came near the island, Manannan put out his magic, and the air grew so thick with mist that Patrick could see nothing at all: not the sun, nor the sky, nor the sea, nor the land. He rode into the mist, but however hard he tried, he could not find his way out. And behind him in the water, a great sea monster was waiting to swallow him up.

Patrick didn't know which way was east and which way was west. He was just about to turn back, when he heard a curlew calling:

"Come you, come you, come you!"

"The curlew will be feeding among the rocks," said Patrick to himself. "She is calling to her chicks."

Then he heard a goat bleating:

"Beware, beware, beware!"

"Where a goat bleats to warn her kid not to fall," said Patrick, "there must be a steep hill."

Last of all, he heard a cockerel crowing:

"Come to us, come, come!"

"Well, bless my soul," said Patrick, "I do believe I'm just behind Peel Hill!"

And with that, his horse took one great leap onto the island, and climbed straight up the steep rock. And sure enough, there he was, at the top of Peel Hill. Standing there, he called out:

"My blessing on the curlew! From now on, nobody will ever find her nest. My blessing on the goat! Nobody will ever see her when her kids are born. My blessing on the cockerel! He shall crow at dawn, at this same hour, for ever after."

As for the sea monster, Patrick turned it into solid rock. And there it lies still, with its great fin on its back.

Where his horse's hooves struck the top of the hill, a spring of pure water came bubbling up, and Patrick and his horse both drank from it. It is called Saint Patrick's Holy Well to this day. And if you go down to the ledges of the rock, you can still see the hoofprints his horse made as it climbed up.

Then Patrick looked around, and the mist was lifting. And he saw a great army of warriors standing round Manannan's fairy hill, with the first rays of the rising sun shining on their spears. But Patrick knew they were not real. They were only phantoms, made by Manannan's magic. And he told them to be gone.

And then, behold! Manannan and all his phantom army turned into three-legged men, and went whirling away like wheels, faster than the wind, all the way to the cliffs of Spanish Head. They whirled over the top so lightly that the seagulls on the ledges below never even woke up. And on they went, over the rough grey sea, until they came to Manannan's enchanted island, fifteen miles out from the Calf of Man. And there Manannan sank his whole island down to the bottom of the sea, and he and his company were never seen again.

Saint Patrick stood on Peel Hill and blessed the island. And it has been blessed ever since. Then he leapt down onto the little island below, which has been called Saint Patrick's Isle ever since.

Manannan's going had stirred up a terrible storm. And from the rocks, Patrick saw a ship being blown straight towards them, with her sails torn away. Patrick raised his hand, and the storm was calmed. The ship came upright again, and everyone on board was saved. They looked up in wonder at the rider in shining armour on the snow-white horse, standing bright against the black rocks.

And ever since that day, when the Manx fishermen sail past the Horse Rock, they take off their caps and say a little prayer:

Saint Patrick, who blessed our island, bless us and our boat, going out well, coming in better, with living and dead in the boat.
