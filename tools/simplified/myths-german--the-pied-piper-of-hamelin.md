Hamelin is a town in Germany, beside the deep, wide river Weser. It is a very pleasant place. But hundreds of years ago, the people of Hamelin had a terrible problem.

Rats! There were rats everywhere. They fought the dogs and chased the cats. They ate the cheeses and licked the soup from the cook's own spoon. They made nests in people's best Sunday hats. And they squeaked and squealed so loudly that nobody could hear themselves talk.

At last the people marched to the town hall. "Our Mayor is a fool!" they cried. "And the town council is no better! We buy you fine robes, and you can't even get rid of the rats! Think of something, or out you go!"

The Mayor and the council were very frightened. They sat and thought for a whole hour. "Oh, if only I had a trap!" groaned the fat Mayor, scratching his head.

Just then there was a gentle tap at the door.

"Come in!" cried the Mayor. And in came the strangest man anyone had ever seen. He was tall and thin, with sharp blue eyes and loose, light hair. He wore a long coat, half yellow and half red, and a red and yellow scarf, and at the end of the scarf hung a pipe, a kind of flute. His fingers kept wandering to the pipe, as if they could not wait to play it.

"Please, your honors," said the stranger, "I have a secret charm. With it I can make every creature under the sun follow me, anything that creeps or swims or flies or runs. People call me the Pied Piper. Last June I freed the great Khan of Tartary from a swarm of gnats, and I rid a prince in Asia of his vampire bats. If I rid your town of rats, will you pay me a thousand gold coins?"

"A thousand? We'll pay you fifty thousand!" cried the Mayor and the council.

So the Piper stepped into the street, smiling a little smile. He put his pipe to his lips and blew three shrill notes. And at once there came a sound like an army muttering. The muttering grew into a grumbling, and the grumbling grew into a mighty rumbling. And out of all the houses the rats came tumbling!

Great rats, small rats, thin rats, fat rats, brown rats, black rats, grey rats, tan rats, grandfathers and babies, uncles and cousins, all with their tails in the air and their whiskers twitching, they followed the Piper. From street to street he played, and step by step they danced after him, until he came to the river Weser. And into the river they all went, and the river carried them away.

All except one big, fat rat, who swam across and went home to Rat-land to tell the others what had happened. "When I heard that music," he said, "it sounded like apples being crunched, and pickle jars being opened, and cupboards full of jam being left open. And a voice seemed to say, 'Oh, rats, be happy! The whole world is one big pantry! Munch away, crunch away, breakfast, lunch and dinner!' And just as I saw a great barrel of sugar shining in front of me, I found myself in the river."

How the people of Hamelin cheered! They rang the church bells until the steeple rocked. "Block up all the rat holes!" cried the Mayor. "Let's never see a rat again!"

Just then the Piper appeared in the market square. "First, if you please," he said, "my thousand gold coins."

A thousand gold coins! The Mayor turned pale. So did the council. They had wanted to spend that money on fine wines for their dinners.

"Well, now," said the Mayor with a wink, "the job is done. We saw the rats go into the river, and they can't come back. Of course we'll give you something for your trouble. But a thousand gold coins? That was just a joke. Here, take fifty!"

The Piper's face fell. "No joking!" he said. "I can't wait around. I must go to Baghdad, where the Caliph's cook is waiting to thank me for clearing his kitchen of scorpions. And people who make me angry may find that I can play a different tune."

"Are you threatening us?" shouted the Mayor. "Go on, then! Blow your pipe until you burst!"

So the Piper stepped into the street once more, and put his long pipe to his lips. And before he had blown three notes, the sweetest notes anyone had ever heard, there was a rustling and a bustling. Little feet were pattering. Wooden shoes were clattering. Little hands were clapping, and little voices chattering. And out of all the houses came the children running, all the little boys and girls, with rosy cheeks and curly hair and sparkling eyes. Tripping and skipping, they ran after the wonderful music, shouting and laughing.

The Mayor and the council stood as still as blocks of wood. They could not move, and they could not call out. They could only watch as the children danced away behind the Piper.

He led them out of the town and up to a mountain called Koppelberg Hill. "He can't get over that mountain," said the people. "He will have to stop, and our children will come home." But when the Piper reached the mountain, a door opened in its side, as if a cave had suddenly appeared. The Piper went in, and the children followed. And when the very last one was inside, the door in the mountain closed.

Did I say the very last one? Not quite. One little boy was lame, and he could not dance fast enough to keep up. For the rest of his life he was sad. "It's so dull in our town now that my friends have gone," he used to say. "The Piper promised to take us to a wonderful land, where the sparrows were brighter than peacocks, and the dogs could outrun the deer, and the bees had no stings, and the horses had eagles' wings. And my lame foot was going to be made well. But just as I got to the mountain, the music stopped, and I was left outside, all alone."

The Mayor sent messengers north, south, east and west, to offer the Piper all the gold and silver he wanted, if only he would bring the children back. But the Piper and the children were gone forever.

The people of Hamelin never forgot. They named the street where the children had danced the Pied Piper's Street, and nobody was ever allowed to play music there. They wrote the story on a stone pillar, and painted it on the church window. And far away in Transylvania there are people who say that their great-great-grandparents came out of a cave under the ground long ago, from Hamelin town. But how or why, they do not know.

So remember: if someone helps you, whether they get rid of your rats or your mice, and you have made them a promise, keep it!
