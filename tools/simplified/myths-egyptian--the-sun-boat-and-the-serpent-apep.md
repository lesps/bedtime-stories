---
title: The Sun Boat and the Serpent Apep
adaptedFrom: spence-egypt--the-journey-of-the-sun-through-the-night
order: 7
namesNote: Spence calls the sun at night Af Ra, and the underworld the Duat; Khepera is the sun at dawn, in the form of a scarab beetle.
---

Every evening, when the sun goes down in the west, the sun god Ra does not go to sleep. He sails on in his boat, into the Duat, the dark world under the earth. And all night long he travels through it, from one end to the other.

As soon as he sinks below the edge of the world, Ra changes his shape. Now he has the head of a ram, and he is called Af Ra, the sun of the night.

At the entrance to the Duat, the ape gods sing songs of praise as his boat comes in. Serpents breathe out fire, and by the light of their flames, Ra's pilots steer the boat through the darkness. All the doors swing open before him.

In the Duat live the dead. And when Ra passes by, the dead wake up, and breathe fresh air, and come alive again for one short hour. Ra gives them food and drink, and light. Then he sails on, and they must wait in the dark until he comes again the next night.

The Duat has twelve parts, one for each hour of the night, and Ra must pass through all of them. In each part there are dangers. In one, two serpents attack him, and he must fight them off. In another, the kingdom of the god Seker, there is no river at all, so the boat is no use. There, Ra speaks words of great power, and the gods of that place lead him through secret tunnels under the ground, until he comes to a stream of boiling water.

Halfway through the night, Ra turns his boat towards the east, towards the mountain where the sun comes up.

But the most dangerous part is still to come. In the seventh part of the Duat, his way is blocked by the wicked serpent Apep. But the goddess Isis and the other gods who travel with Ra are ready for him. They strike Apep with their daggers, and drive him back, and the sun boat sails safely past.

In the eighth part, a crowd of gods pull the boat along with ropes. In the ninth part, the boat sails all by itself. In the tenth and eleventh parts, it glides across a string of lakes, with a shining disk of light at the front of the boat to show the way.

At last Ra comes to the twelfth part, the last hour of the night, where the dark waters of Nu are, and where Nut, the goddess of the morning sky, lives. There, in front of the boat, lies another enormous serpent. Twelve gods take hold of the tow rope, and go in at the serpent's tail, and pull Ra's boat right through the serpent's long body, and out of its mouth.

And when Ra comes out, he is not Af Ra any more. He has turned into Khepera, the young sun of the morning.

Then twelve goddesses pull Khepera up into the sky, and bring him to Shu, the god of the air. And Shu lifts him up through an opening at the edge of the world. And the people of the earth see a shining disk of light rising in the east. It is morning, and the sun has come back.

And all the gods who travel with him cheer, and sing songs of praise. And the next evening, the whole journey begins again.
