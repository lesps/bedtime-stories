Odysseus was a king and a very clever man. After a long war far from home, he set sail with his men to go back to his little island kingdom. But the journey home took ten long years, because they had one adventure after another.

One day, after a terrible storm, their ship came to a green island. Nobody knew its name. The men were tired and very hungry. For two days they ate nothing but clams and oysters from the beach.

On the third morning, Odysseus climbed a cliff to look around. Far away, in the middle of the island, he saw a white marble palace among tall trees. Smoke was curling up from its chimney.

He started to walk toward it. But a little bird came flying at his face. It had purple wings, yellow legs, and a tuft of golden feathers on its head like a tiny crown.

"Peep, peep, pe-weep!" cried the bird, sadly. Every time Odysseus stepped toward the palace, the bird flapped its wings in his face, as if to push him back.

"I think you are trying to warn me," said Odysseus. So he turned back. On the way, he caught a big deer, and he carried it to his men for dinner.

The next morning the men were hungry again. Odysseus said, "We must ask for food at that palace. But we have met giants and monsters on this journey. So let half of us go, and half of us stay with the ship."

His friend Eurylochus led the first group of twenty-two men. As they walked through the woods, they smelled the smoke from the palace kitchen.

"Roast pig!" cried one man. "My mouth is watering!"

Then the little purple bird came flying. "Peep, peep, pe-weep!" it cried.

"That bird knows something we don't," said Eurylochus. "Let's turn back."

But the others only laughed. "That bird would make a nice snack," said one. And they hurried on.

They came to a clear spring and stopped to drink. When they looked into the water, their faces looked so silly that they all burst out laughing. Then lions, tigers and wolves came running out of the trees. The men were terrified. But the wild animals only wagged their tails and rubbed against them like friendly dogs. Eurylochus did not like it. He thought he saw something fierce hiding in their eyes.

Inside the palace, a fountain splashed in a great hall. The water kept making shapes as it fell. Behind a door, someone was singing a beautiful song, and they could hear a loom clicking as someone wove cloth.

"Let's go in!" said the men.

"Wait," said Eurylochus. But they would not wait. So Eurylochus hid behind a pillar, and watched the others go through the door.

A beautiful woman stood up from her loom to greet them. Her name was Circe. Four young women were with her. One had sea-green hair. One wore a dress of oak bark. One sprinkled drops of dew from her fingers.

"Welcome, my friends," said Circe. "I know all about you. Look." On her loom was a picture woven in colored thread. It showed all of their adventures, from the very start of their journey.

"You must be hungry," said Circe. "Come and eat."

She led them to a grand room and sat each man on a golden throne. Servants brought in hot roast meat, rich stews and big jugs of wine. The men ate and ate and drank and drank. Some of the food tasted a little strange, but they did not care. They gobbled it up like pigs at a trough.

When they could not eat another bite, Circe laughed. She stood in the middle of the room and lifted her magic wand.

"You have eaten like pigs," she said. "Now you shall look like pigs too!"

She waved her wand. In a flash, every man turned into a pig. They tried to shout, but they could only grunt and squeal. Circe's servants chased them out to the pigsty and threw them some acorns.

Eurylochus waited behind the pillar for a long time. Then he heard squealing, and he saw the fountain make the shape of a pig. He ran all the way back to the ship.

"Where are the others?" asked Odysseus.

Eurylochus cried as he told the story. "That woman is a witch," he said. "I don't know what she has done with them."

Odysseus picked up his sword and his bow. "I am their king," he said. "It is my job to help them. Wait for me until tomorrow."

As he walked into the woods, the purple bird came again. "Peep, peep, pe-weep!"

"I'm sorry, little bird," said Odysseus. "I must go."

Then a young man appeared on the path. He wore a cap with little wings on it, and he had wings on his sandals too. He was Hermes, the messenger of the gods. Some people called him Quicksilver, because he was so quick.

"Where are you going, Odysseus?" asked Hermes. "This island is under a spell. Circe lives in that palace. She turns people into the animal they are most like. That purple bird was once a king who was too proud of his fine purple robes. The lions and wolves were once cruel men."

"And my men?" asked Odysseus.

Hermes laughed. "You know how greedy they were. She turned them into pigs!"

"Can I help them?"

"With my help, you can," said Hermes. He bent down and picked a little plant with a snow-white flower. "Hold this flower close and keep smelling it. When Circe gives you her wine, take a deep breath of the flower first. Then her magic cannot hurt you."

Odysseus thanked him. When he turned around to ask one more question, Hermes was already gone.

At the palace, the lions and wolves came to fawn on Odysseus. But he waved his spear at them and told them to get out of his way. He knew what they really were.

Circe met him in the hall. "Welcome, brave stranger," she said with a smile. "Your friends are already here, enjoying themselves. You look like a king. Come, have a drink from my royal cup."

A servant brought a golden cup of sparkling wine. Odysseus held the white flower to his nose and took a long, deep breath. Then he drank the wine, every drop, and looked Circe right in the eye.

Circe struck him with her wand. "Now turn into the animal you are most like!" she cried.

But nothing happened. Odysseus stood there, looking more like a king than ever. He threw the cup across the floor. He drew his sword.

"Your tricks are over, Circe," he said.

Circe fell to her knees. "Spare me!" she cried. "Hermes told me you would come. You are the wisest man in the world. No magic can beat you."

"Then promise to turn my men back into men," said Odysseus.

"I promise," said Circe.

She took him out to the pigsty. Twenty-two big pigs came running to Odysseus, squealing loudly. "Yes, those are my men," he said. "I can tell by the way they act."

Circe waved her wand and said some magic words. The pigs' snouts grew shorter. Their ears grew smaller. They stood up on their back legs. Little by little, they turned back into men.

Then one of Circe's maidens threw a handful of acorns on the ground. The men dropped to their knees and gobbled them up! Then they remembered themselves, and stood up looking very silly.

"Thank you, Odysseus!" they said, though their voices still sounded a little grunty.

"Don't thank me," said Odysseus. "Whether you stay men or turn back into pigs is up to you."

"Peep, peep, pe-weep!" sang a voice from a tree. It was the little purple bird, who had tried so hard to help.

"Circe," said Odysseus, "turn that good little bird back into a king." Circe waved her wand, and down from the tree jumped a king in a purple robe and a golden crown, bowing his thanks.

But Odysseus did not ask Circe to change the lions, tigers and wolves. "They were cruel men," he said. "It is better that they look like what they really are."
