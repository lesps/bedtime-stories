Long ago, Prince Negru Vodă rode along the river Argeș with ten master builders. Nine of them were great masters. But the tenth, Manole, was the greatest of them all.

The Prince was looking for a place to build a monastery, a church more beautiful than any in the world. Beside the river they met a shepherd boy playing his pipe. "Shepherd," said the Prince, "have you seen an old wall somewhere around here, a wall that was started long ago and never finished?"

"I have, my lord," said the shepherd. "It is down there among the reeds."

So they went down to the old wall, and the Prince said to the builders, "Here you will build me a monastery taller and more beautiful than any other. If you do, I will make you rich. But if you fail, I will have you walled up in its foundations."

The builders set to work. All day they laid stones, and the walls rose up. But in the night, the walls fell down. The next day they built again, and the next night the walls fell down again. And so it went on, day after day. Whatever they built in the day fell down in the night.

The Prince grew angry, and the builders grew afraid.

Then one night, Manole had a dream. A voice spoke to him: "Whatever you build will fall down, unless you wall up a living person inside the wall. Tomorrow at dawn, the first wife or sister who comes bringing food to the builders must be built into the wall."

Manole told the other builders his dream. And they all swore an oath: whichever wife or sister came first, they would build her into the wall, and they would not warn their own wives.

At dawn the next morning, Manole climbed up on the scaffolding and looked out across the fields. And who should he see coming, carrying food and drink for him, but his own wife, Ana.

Manole's heart broke. He fell to his knees and prayed, "Dear God, send a great rain, to stop her from coming!" And God sent a great rain, and the streams turned into rivers. But Ana kept on coming.

Again Manole prayed, "Dear God, send a great wind, to blow her back home!" And God sent a great wind, so strong that it bent the fir trees down to the ground. But still Ana kept on coming. And at last she arrived.

The other builders were glad, because their own wives were safe. But Manole took Ana in his arms, and led her to the wall, and said, "Stand here, my dear. Let's play a game. We'll pretend to build you into the wall."

Ana laughed, and stood still. And the builders began to lay stones around her. The wall rose up to her ankles, and to her knees, and Ana stopped laughing. "Manole, Manole," she said, "the joke has gone on long enough. The wall is pressing on me!" But Manole said nothing, and the builders worked on, and the wall rose higher and higher, until it closed over her. And still from inside the wall came her voice, softer and softer: "Manole, Manole..."

And from that day, the walls stood. The monastery rose up, tall and more beautiful than any other in the world.

When it was finished, the Prince came to see it. The builders were standing up on the roof. "Tell me," said the Prince, "could you build me another monastery, even more beautiful than this one?"

"Of course we could!" boasted the builders. "We could build one much more beautiful!"

When the Prince heard that, he ordered the scaffolding to be taken away, so that the builders were left up on the roof, and could never build anything more beautiful for anyone else.

The builders made themselves wings out of thin wooden shingles, and they jumped from the roof to fly away. But they fell, and where each of them fell, he turned into stone.

Last of all, Manole tried to fly. But just as he jumped, he heard Ana's voice from inside the wall, calling, "Manole, Manole..." And he fell to the ground.

Where Manole fell, a spring of clear water bubbled up out of the earth. And people say that its water tastes salty, from all the tears he cried.
