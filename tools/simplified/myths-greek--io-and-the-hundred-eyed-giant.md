One day Hera, the queen of the gods, noticed that the sky had suddenly gone dark over one river. "That's strange," she thought. "My husband Zeus must be hiding something under that cloud."

She swept the cloud away. There was Zeus, standing by the river. Beside him stood a beautiful white cow.

The cow was really a young woman named Io, the daughter of the river god. Zeus was fond of her, and when he heard Hera coming, he had quickly turned her into a cow.

"What a lovely cow," said Hera sweetly. "Whose is it?"

"Oh, it just appeared out of the earth," said Zeus.

"Then you won't mind giving it to me," said Hera.

Zeus could not say no without making Hera even more suspicious. So he gave her the cow.

Hera sent the cow to be guarded by a giant named Argus. Argus had a hundred eyes all over his head. When he slept, he closed only two eyes at a time. The other ninety-eight eyes stayed wide open, watching.

Poor Io had to eat grass all day. At night Argus tied her up with a rope. When she tried to speak, all that came out was "Moo!" which frightened even her.

One day her father and sisters came by. They patted the pretty cow and gave her grass. Io licked her father's hand. She wanted so much to tell him who she was. Then she had an idea. With her hoof, she scratched her name in the sand: I-O.

"My daughter!" cried her father. He hugged her neck and cried. But Argus came and drove her away, and sat on a hill where he could see in every direction.

Zeus felt sorry for Io. He called Hermes, the messenger god. "Go and free her," he said.

Hermes took off his winged sandals and his winged cap. He dressed up as a shepherd and walked along playing a set of pipes made from reeds. Argus had never heard such music before.

"Young man," said Argus, "come and sit with me. Play me another song."

So Hermes sat down and played, and told stories, and played some more. Some of Argus's eyes began to close. But some stayed open.

"Where did you get those pipes?" Argus asked sleepily.

"I'll tell you," said Hermes. "There was once a nymph named Syrinx who loved to hunt in the woods. One day the wild god Pan saw her and chased after her. She ran to the edge of a river and called to her sisters, the water nymphs, for help. Just as Pan reached out to catch her, he found he was holding a bunch of tall reeds instead. Syrinx had become the reeds. Pan sighed, and his breath blew across the reeds and made a soft, sad sound. So he cut the reeds, tied them together, and made the first set of pipes. And he named them after her."

Before Hermes reached the end of the story, every one of Argus's hundred eyes had closed. Hermes struck him down, and Argus never woke up.

Hera was sad that her faithful watchman was gone. She took his hundred eyes and set them in the tail feathers of her favorite bird, the peacock. You can still see them there today.

But Hera was not finished with Io. She sent a buzzing gadfly to sting her. Io ran and ran to get away from it. She swam across a sea, which is still called the Ionian Sea after her. She crossed a narrow strait, which was named the Bosporus, which means "the cow's crossing."

At last she reached the land of Egypt. There Zeus promised Hera that he would leave Io alone. So Hera let her change back. Io's hair fell away. Her horns shrank. Her hooves turned back into hands and feet. She stood up, and she was a young woman again.

At first she was almost afraid to speak, in case she mooed. But when she spoke, her own voice came out, and she laughed with joy.
