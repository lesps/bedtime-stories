One Christmas, King Arthur held a great feast at Camelot that lasted for fifteen days. On New Year's Day, all the knights and ladies sat down in the great hall to dinner. Queen Guinevere sat on the high platform, and beside her sat Sir Gawain, King Arthur's nephew.

The first dish had only just been served when the doors burst open, and in rode a knight on a horse. He was enormous, almost a giant. And he was green. His coat was green, his cloak was green, his stockings were green, and even his skin and his hair and his beard were green. His horse was green too, with its mane and tail plaited with gold and hung with little bells. In one hand the knight held a branch of holly, and in the other a huge axe, with a blade of green and gold.

Nobody said a word. Everyone stared.

"Who is the king here?" the Green Knight asked. "I have come to offer you a Christmas game. If any knight here is brave enough, let him take this axe and strike me one blow. I will not fight back. But in a year and a day, I shall give him a blow in return."

Still nobody spoke. The Green Knight laughed. "Is this the famous Round Table?" he said. "Are you all afraid of a few words?"

King Arthur's face went red with anger, and he jumped up and took hold of the axe himself. But Sir Gawain stood up. "My lord," he said, "let this game be mine." And the king gave him the axe.

The Green Knight got down from his horse, and bent his head, and moved his long green hair aside to show his neck. Gawain lifted the axe and struck, and the Green Knight's head fell off and rolled across the floor.

But the Green Knight did not fall. He walked over and picked up his own head by the hair, and climbed back onto his horse, holding it up in his hand. And the head opened its eyes, and spoke.

"Gawain," it said, "remember your promise. In a year's time, on New Year's morning, come and find me at the Green Chapel, and take the blow that you owe me." Then the Green Knight turned his horse and galloped out of the hall, and nobody knew where he went.

King Arthur hung the green axe up on the wall, as a reminder of the wonder they had seen.

The year went by. Winter turned to spring, and spring to summer, and summer to autumn. And at the beginning of winter, Gawain put on his armour and set out to find the Green Chapel. On his red shield was a golden star with five points, which stood for all the things a good knight should be: generous, friendly, pure, polite and kind. He rode his good horse Gringolet, and everyone at court was sad to see him go, for they thought they would never see him again.

Gawain rode north through Wales and over the wild hills, asking everyone he met if they knew the way to the Green Chapel. But nobody had ever heard of it. He fought with wolves and wild men and giants, and he slept out in his armour in the freezing sleet and snow.

On Christmas Eve, cold and tired, he prayed for somewhere to stay. And just then he saw a fine castle through the trees, with a moat all around it. The lord of the castle welcomed him warmly. He was a big man with a red face and a broad beard. And his wife was the most beautiful lady Gawain had ever seen. With her was an old, old lady, who was treated with great respect.

Gawain spent Christmas at the castle, and when he told the lord that he was looking for the Green Chapel, the lord laughed. "It's only two miles from here," he said. "Stay with us until New Year's Day, and I'll have someone show you the way."

Then the lord made a strange bargain with him. "I'm going hunting for the next three days," he said. "You stay here and rest. And at the end of each day, I'll give you whatever I catch in the forest, and you give me whatever you get in the castle." Gawain laughed, and agreed.

On the first day, the lord went hunting deer. And while he was away, his beautiful wife came into Gawain's room, and sat and talked with him, and before she left, she gave him a kiss. That evening, the lord came home and gave Gawain all the deer he had caught. And Gawain gave the lord a kiss, as their bargain said.

On the second day, the lord hunted a fierce wild boar. And the lady came again, and talked with Gawain, and this time she gave him two kisses. That evening, the lord gave Gawain the boar, and Gawain gave the lord two kisses.

On the third day, the lord hunted a fox. And the lady came again, and gave Gawain three kisses. Then she tried to give him a gold ring, but he would not take it. So she took off her belt, which was made of green silk. "Take this, then," she said. "It's a magic belt. Whoever wears it can never be killed." Gawain thought of the Green Knight's axe, and he took the belt. And the lady asked him to keep it a secret from her husband.

That evening, the lord came home and gave Gawain the fox's skin. And Gawain gave the lord three kisses. But he did not give him the green belt. He kept that hidden.

On New Year's morning, Gawain put on his armour, and tied the green belt around himself, and set out with a guide to find the Green Chapel. The snow was falling, and the wind was cold.

"Sir," said the guide, "please don't go on. The Green Knight is bigger and stronger than any four of King Arthur's knights, and he kills everyone who comes near his chapel. Go home another way, and I promise I'll never tell anyone."

"Thank you," said Gawain, "but I gave my word, and I will keep it." So the guide left him, and he rode on alone.

The Green Chapel was not a chapel at all. It was a grassy mound beside a stream, with a dark cave in it. And from somewhere nearby came a terrible grinding noise, like someone sharpening an axe on a stone. Then out came the Green Knight, as green as ever, with a huge new axe in his hand.

"Welcome, Gawain," he said. "You have kept your promise. Now take off your helmet, and take your blow."

Gawain took off his helmet and bent his head. The Green Knight swung the axe high. But as it came down, Gawain flinched, just a little.

The Green Knight stopped. "You can't be Gawain," he said, "who is so famous for his courage. When you struck at me, I didn't flinch."

"I flinched once," said Gawain, "but I won't do it again. Strike!"

The Green Knight swung again, and again he stopped the axe before it touched Gawain's neck. Gawain did not move.

Then the Green Knight swung the axe a third time. And this time it came down on Gawain's neck, but very lightly, and only nicked the skin. A few drops of blood fell on the snow.

At once Gawain leapt up, and drew his sword. "I've taken my blow," he cried, "and I didn't flinch! That was our bargain. If you strike again, I'll fight back!"

But the Green Knight just leaned on his axe and smiled. "Don't be so fierce, Gawain," he said. "Nobody here has treated you badly. I am the lord of the castle where you stayed. My name is Bertilak. The first swing was for the first day, when you gave me back my wife's kiss. The second swing was for the second day, when you gave me back her two kisses. And the third swing, the one that nicked you, was for the third day, when you kept the green belt and didn't tell me. It was my belt, and my wife gave it to you because I sent her to test you. You are the best knight in the world, Gawain. You only failed a little, because you wanted to stay alive, and nobody can blame you much for that."

Then he told Gawain that the old lady at the castle was Morgan le Fay, the enchantress, who was King Arthur's sister. It was her magic that had turned him green, and sent him to Camelot to test the knights of the Round Table.

But Gawain was ashamed. He untied the green belt and held it out. "I was a coward," he said, "and I broke my word."

"Keep it," said the Green Knight kindly. "Keep it to remember this adventure."

So Gawain kept the green belt, not because it was beautiful or magic, but to remind himself that even the best knight can make mistakes. And he rode home to Camelot.

Everyone at court was overjoyed to see him alive. Gawain told them the whole story, and showed them the scar on his neck and the green belt, and he told them how ashamed he was. But King Arthur and his knights would not hear of it. They laughed, and they all agreed that from then on, every knight of the Round Table would wear a green sash, in honour of Sir Gawain.
