This is a much later story about Fionn, from the days when people told tales about him by the fire and made him into a giant. In these stories they call him Fin McCool.

Once upon a time, Fin McCool and the other giants of Ireland were busy building a road of stones across the sea to Scotland. You can still see the beginning of it on the north coast of Ireland. It is called the Giant's Causeway.

But one day Fin suddenly put down his tools, pulled up a fir tree, broke off its roots and branches to make a walking stick, and set off for home. "I'm worried about my wife, Oonagh," he told the others. "I must go and see how she is."

That wasn't the real reason, though. The real reason was a giant called Cucullin. He was the strongest giant there had ever been. When he got angry and stamped his foot, the whole countryside shook. Once he had squashed a thunderbolt flat with his fist, and he kept it in his pocket, shaped like a pancake, to show to anybody who wanted to fight him. He had beaten every giant in Ireland except Fin. And he had sworn he would never rest until he had beaten Fin too.

Fin did not want to fight a giant who could squash thunderbolts. And he had heard that Cucullin was on his way to the Causeway to look for him.

Fin and Oonagh lived right on the top of a hill called Knockmany. It was very windy up there, and there was no water. "Why do you live in such a windy place?" the neighbours asked him.

"I like a good view," said Fin. But really he lived there so that he could see Cucullin coming.

"You're home early," said Oonagh, giving him a kiss so loud it made the lake at the bottom of the hill ripple.

"I missed you," said Fin.

But after a few days Oonagh could see that something was worrying him. "It's this Cucullin," Fin admitted. Then he put his thumb in his mouth. Whenever Fin sucked his thumb, he could see things that were far away, or things that were going to happen.

"He's coming," said Fin. "I can see him below Dungannon. He'll be here tomorrow at two o'clock. If I run away, I'll be disgraced. But if I fight him, he'll flatten me like his pancake!"

"Don't worry," said Oonagh. "Leave it to me, and maybe I'll get you out of this better than you could get yourself out of it with your thumb." And Fin felt better, because Oonagh was very clever, and she was friends with the fairies. Some people said she was a fairy herself.

Oonagh had a sister called Granua, who lived on the next hill, Cullamore. The valley between them was only three or four miles wide, so on summer evenings the two sisters could chat across it from one hilltop to the other.

"Granua!" Oonagh called. "Can you see anything coming?"

"Goodness me," Granua called back, "I can see the biggest giant there ever was, coming up from Dungannon!"

"That's Cucullin," said Oonagh. "He's coming to beat Fin. What shall we do?"

"I'll invite him up to my house for supper," said Granua. "That will give you time to think of a plan."

So Granua made a great smoke on her hilltop and whistled three times, which was how people long ago told travellers they were welcome to come and share a meal. And Cucullin went up to Granua's house for the night.

Meanwhile, Oonagh got busy. She borrowed twenty-one iron griddles from the neighbours. A griddle is a flat iron pan for baking bread on. She baked each griddle right inside a round loaf of bread, so you couldn't see it. Then she baked one more loaf, with no griddle in it, and she put it to one side, where she would know it. She also made a pot of soft white curds, like cottage cheese. And she told Fin exactly what to do.

Now, Oonagh knew something about Cucullin. All his tremendous strength was in the middle finger of his right hand. Without that finger, he would be no stronger than an ordinary man.

The next day, at two o'clock, they saw Cucullin coming across the valley. "Quick," said Oonagh. "Get into the cradle and pretend to be our baby. Lie still and don't say a word until I tell you."

Fin did not like this one bit. But he climbed into the big cradle, and Oonagh tucked him in.

In came Cucullin. "Is this where the great Fin McCool lives?" he asked.

"It is," said Oonagh. "Sit down, won't you?"

"Is he at home?"

"No," said Oonagh. "Somebody told him that a big lump of a giant called Cucullin was down at the Causeway looking for him, and he went off in a fury to find him. I hope for that poor giant's sake that Fin doesn't catch him!"

"I am Cucullin," said the giant. "And I've been looking for Fin for a whole year."

Oonagh laughed as if he was the smallest, silliest thing she had ever seen. "Have you ever seen Fin?" she asked.

"No. He always keeps out of my way."

"I thought so. Well, if you take my advice, you poor little thing, you'll hope you never do. Now, while you're here, would you do me a favour? The wind is blowing right in at the door. When Fin is home, he always turns the house round to keep the wind out. Would you do it for me?"

Cucullin was a bit surprised. But he went outside, pulled his middle finger until it cracked three times, put his arms round the house, and turned it right round. In the cradle, Fin broke out in a cold sweat.

"Thank you kindly," said Oonagh. "And there's one more thing. We have no water up here. Fin was going to pull apart the rocks at the bottom of the hill to find a spring. Would you do that for me too?"

Cucullin cracked his finger nine times, bent down, and tore open the solid rock, making a crack four hundred feet deep and a quarter of a mile long. Even Oonagh was startled. But she didn't show it.

"Come in and have something to eat," she said. "Fin wouldn't want me to send anyone away hungry, even an enemy." And she put in front of him some bacon and cabbage, and butter, and six round loaves of bread, with the griddles inside them.

Cucullin was very hungry. He took a huge bite of a loaf, and—CRUNCH!

"Ow!" he roared. "Two of my teeth have come out! What kind of bread is this?"

"That's Fin's bread," said Oonagh calmly. "It's the only kind he eats. Nobody else can eat it, except Fin and the baby. I thought a strong fellow like you could manage it. Try another one. Maybe it's softer."

Cucullin took a huge bite of the second loaf. CRUNCH! "OW!" he yelled, twice as loud. "There go two more! Take your bread away, or I won't have a tooth left in my head!"

"Hush," said Oonagh, "you'll wake the baby. There now, you've woken him."

From the cradle came a great bellow. "Mammy, I'm hungry! Give me something to eat!"

Oonagh went over and gave him the loaf with no griddle in it, and Fin gobbled it up in no time. Cucullin stared. "If the baby can eat that bread," he thought, "what must his father be like? I'm lucky I never met him!"

"I'd like to see that baby," he said.

"Get up, my darling," said Oonagh, "and show this little man what Fin McCool's son can do."

Fin climbed out of the cradle, dressed up to look like a little boy, and took Cucullin outside. "Are you strong?" he asked. "Can you squeeze water out of this white stone?" And he gave Cucullin a white stone.

Cucullin squeezed and squeezed. He squeezed until his face went black. But not one drop of water came out.

"You call yourself a giant?" said Fin. "Give it here. Watch what Fin's little boy can do." And he took the stone, but quickly swapped it for a lump of the soft white curds. He squeezed, and out came the whey, as clear as water, dripping through his fingers.

"Now I'm going back to my cradle," said Fin. "I won't waste my time on someone who can't eat my daddy's bread or squeeze water out of a stone. You'd better go away before my daddy gets home, or he'll make porridge out of you."

Cucullin's knees were knocking. He went in to say goodbye to Oonagh. "Tell Fin I give up," he said. "I'm no match for him. I'll keep away from him for the rest of my life. But before I go, could I feel that baby's teeth? I'd like to know what kind of teeth can eat that bread."

"Of course," said Oonagh. "But they're right at the back of his mouth, so you'll have to put your finger in a long way."

So Cucullin put the middle finger of his right hand into Fin's mouth, right to the back. And Fin bit it off!

All Cucullin's strength went out of him at once. He gave a great groan and fell down on the floor, as weak as any ordinary man. Then Fin jumped out of the cradle. And that was the end of the great Cucullin, who had frightened all the giants of Ireland for so long. He never troubled Fin or anybody else again.

So Fin beat the strongest giant in the world, not by his own strength, but by the cleverness of his wife, Oonagh.
