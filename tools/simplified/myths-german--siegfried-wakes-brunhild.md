title: Siegfried Wakes Brunhild

Bragi's little ship sailed far over the sea, past mountains of ice that melted when Bragi played his harp. At last it came into a quiet bay. Beside the bay stood a castle with sea-green towers, and all around its walls was a moat full of glowing red light.

Everything was still. Not a breath of wind stirred. The leaves on the trees hung without moving. The green flag on the tower hung limp. No bird sang, and no insect buzzed.

"Play your harp, Bragi," said Siegfried, "and wake them all up."

So Bragi played and sang, the most beautiful music in the world, about everything lovely on the land and in the sea and in the sky. But nothing stirred. The sun went down, and still everything slept.

"Music cannot wake a sleep like this one," said Bragi. And he told Siegfried the story.

"In Odin's home there is a great hall called Valhalla, where Odin holds a feast every day for the heroes who have fallen in battle. His warrior maidens, the Valkyries, bring the heroes there. One of the Valkyries was called Brunhild. She was the most beautiful of them all. But she did not always do as Odin told her. Sometimes she saved warriors that Odin had chosen, or helped her friends to win.

"So Odin was angry, and he sent her away to live like an ordinary person on the earth. The kind old king of this land, Isenland, found her wandering alone, and he took her as his daughter. When he died, Brunhild became queen. But that made Odin angrier still. He pricked her with the thorn of sleep, and he said, 'She will sleep until someone comes who is brave enough to ride through fire to wake her.' And the whole land fell asleep with her."

"My songs cannot wake her," said Bragi. "A brave hero must ride through the flames. That is why I have brought you here. Now I must sail on, to bring my music to other lands."

Siegfried's heart jumped for joy. Here at last was a good deed worth doing! He said goodbye to Bragi and stepped ashore, and Greyfell followed him. Bragi's ship sailed away, and Siegfried stood alone on the beach.

The full moon rose out of the sea. And then a crowd of tiny fairies came down to dance on the sand. Siegfried hid among the tall reeds to watch. As the moon rose higher, they began to sing, songs about summer days and cool shade and quiet sleep. Siegfried's eyes grew heavy, and he began to fall asleep too.

But just then a little brown elf came up out of the sea.

"What news from the world over the water?" asked the fairy queen.

"The prince is on his way," said the elf. "If he is brave enough, he will wake the princess and all the sleepy people of the castle."

"He will have to be the bravest of men," said the queen. "The moat is full of fire, and no one who is afraid will dare to cross it."

"I will dare!" cried Siegfried, jumping up. And all the fairies flitted away and left him alone.

In the morning he looked at the red flames again, and he wasn't sure what to do. Should he wait for a sign from Odin? Then there was a flash of light, and Greyfell came galloping across the sand, with a thousand sunbeams sparkling from his mane. He had never looked so bright. Siegfried felt brave again, as if Odin himself had spoken to him. He jumped onto Greyfell's back, and they galloped to the edge of the moat.

The moat was full of angry, hissing flames, twisting and curling like fiery snakes. But Siegfried did not stop. He gave the word, and Greyfell leaped straight into the fire. And the flames ran away from the sunbeams shining from Greyfell's mane! Siegfried rode right through the moat, without a single burn, and in through the open gate.

Inside, everyone in the castle was asleep. The gatekeeper was snoring in his little house, with his rusty keys at his feet. In the stables, the horses slept in their stalls, and the grooms slept beside them. The birds were asleep in their nests, and the watchdogs were stretched out in front of the doors. In the garden, the fountain had stopped, and the bees had fallen asleep inside the apple blossoms. In the kitchen, the cook was dozing by the fire, the butler was snoring in the pantry, the dairymaid was napping among the milk pans, and even the flies were asleep on the sugar. In the great dining hall, a thousand knights slept at the table, and their chief slept with his cup still at his lips.

Siegfried hurried on from room to room, because he knew nobody would wake until the princess did. In the grandest hall of all, he found her. Brunhild lay asleep on a couch under a golden canopy, with her ladies sleeping around her. She had slept for many, many years, but she had not grown a day older.

Siegfried stood beside her. Gently he kissed her forehead, and softly he said her name.

"Brunhild!"

The spell was broken! Brunhild sat up, as beautiful as a queen. Her ladies woke up around her. And all over the castle, from the cellar to the bell tower, everyone woke up at once. The watchman on the tower yawned and said he had never been asleep. The gatekeeper grabbed his keys and hurried to lock the gate. The horses neighed. The dogs barked. The birds were embarrassed that the sun had caught them napping, and flew off to find breakfast. The knights in the dining hall picked up their knives and went on with their feast. And their chief dropped his cup and rubbed his eyes, wondering how he could have fallen asleep in the middle of dinner.

Siegfried looked out of a window. The fire in the moat was gone, and the moat was full of clear, sparkling water. A warm wind blew from the sea, bringing the faraway sound of Bragi's harp. The flowers opened to the sun, and the birds sang.

And Brunhild stood smiling at Siegfried's side, and welcomed him to Isenland and her green-towered castle.
