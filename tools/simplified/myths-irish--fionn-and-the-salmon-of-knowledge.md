Long ago in Ireland there was a band of great warriors and hunters called the Fianna, and their leader was Uail, the son of Baiscne. But Uail had enemies: the sons of Morna. They wanted to lead the Fianna themselves, and at last they killed him.

Uail had a baby son. His mother, beautiful long-haired Muirne, was so afraid that the sons of Morna would kill the baby too that she could not keep him with her. So she gave him to two wise women to bring up in secret. One of them was his own aunt, Bovmall. They took him deep into the woods of Slieve Bloom and hid him there in a little hut.

At first the boy was called Deimne. Later he was called Fionn, which means "the fair one", because of his fair hair. You say it "Fewn", to rhyme with "tune".

Fionn grew up in the woods with the birds for playmates. He knew every little path, every bird's song, and every sound the wind could make. He climbed every tree his nurses told him not to climb, and then bigger ones.

And his nurses trained him. To teach him to run, one of them would chase him round and round a tree with a prickly thorn switch, and then it was his turn to chase her. Fionn learned to run very fast indeed! To teach him to jump, they set him chasing hares across a bumpy field. Whichever way the hare jumped, Fionn had to jump that way too, until he could hop better than any hare. To teach him to swim, they threw him into a deep, cold pool. He splashed and spluttered, and they pulled him out, and threw him in again, until he could slip through the water like an otter. At last he could swim underwater so quietly that he could catch a wild duck by the leg. "Qu—" said the duck, and down it went before it could finish its "ack".

When Fionn was six, his mother came to see him, secretly, at night. She took him in her arms and kissed him, and sang him a sleepy song. But when he woke up in the morning she was gone, for she had to hurry back before the sons of Morna found out.

The sons of Morna did come looking for him. But his nurses had warned him in time, and he was far away, travelling with a band of young poets. For a while after that he lived with Bovmall's husband, Fiacuil, a wild man who had become a robber when Fionn's father was killed. Fiacuil lived hidden in a great cold marsh, and he taught Fionn all its secrets: where it was safe to swim, and where the long weeds under the water would catch you and pull you down.

Then Fionn went back to the woods and grew up tall and strong. He could run down a deer and carry it home. At last his nurses said, "You must leave us now. The sons of Morna are watching for you again." So Fionn went out into the world on his own.

First he came to a lake where some boys were swimming. He joined in, and he could swim far better than any of them. "He is fair and well shaped," said one of the boys, and that is how he got the name Fionn. But the boys grew jealous of him, and he went on his way.

He worked as a hunter for a king, and no deer ever escaped from him. Then he worked for the King of Kerry, who, though Fionn did not know it at first, had married his mother. One day Fionn played chess with the king and beat him seven games in a row.

"Who are you?" cried the king, staring at him.

"I'm the son of a farmer," said Fionn.

"You are not," said the king. "You are the son of Uail. You can't stay here. I don't want you killed while you are under my roof." So Fionn went on his travels again.

More than anything, Fionn wanted to be wise. So he went to learn from a poet called Finegas, who lived on the bank of the River Boyne. He called himself Deimne, so that the sons of Morna would not find him.

Fionn asked Finegas question after question, and Finegas answered every one.

"Why do you live beside a river?" asked Fionn.

"Because poems come to you beside running water," said Finegas.

"And why this river, out of all the rivers in Ireland?"

Finegas smiled. "I will tell you that," he said. "A wise man once told me that I would catch the Salmon of Knowledge in the Boyne. And whoever eats the Salmon of Knowledge will know everything."

"How does the salmon get wise?" asked Fionn.

"Over a secret pool, in a secret place, grows a magic hazel tree. The Nuts of Knowledge fall from it into the water, and the salmon eats them."

"Wouldn't it be easier just to find the tree and eat the nuts?"

"You can only find the tree if you already know where it is," laughed Finegas. "And you can only know that by eating the nuts. And you can only get the nuts by eating the salmon."

"Then we will have to wait for the salmon," said Fionn.

So Fionn stayed with Finegas, and fetched water and lit the fire and kept the hut, and learned all about poetry. Finegas had been fishing for the salmon for seven years.

Then one day Finegas came to Fionn with a basket on his arm. He looked happy and sad at the same time. "Look in the basket," he said.

There was a beautiful salmon. "It is The Salmon," said Finegas.

"Oh, master, I'm so glad for you!" cried Fionn.

Finegas was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "I'm going away for a little while. Roast the salmon for me while I'm gone. But don't eat any of it."

"I won't eat the littlest piece," said Fionn.

So Fionn roasted the salmon over the fire. As it cooked, a big blister rose up on its skin. Fionn didn't like the look of it, so he pressed it down with his thumb. It burned his thumb! So he popped his thumb into his mouth to cool it.

When Finegas came back, the salmon was ready, on a wooden plate among cool green leaves.

"You didn't eat any of it?" asked Finegas.

"Didn't I promise?" said Fionn. "But I did burn my thumb on it, and put my thumb in my mouth. If the salmon tastes as good as my thumb did, it will taste very good!"

Finegas looked at him. "What did you say your name was?" he asked.

"Deimne."

"Your name is not Deimne," said Finegas gently. "It is Fionn."

"That's true," said Fionn, surprised. "But how do you know?"

"I didn't tell you the whole truth," said Finegas. "It was foretold that I would catch the salmon. But it was not foretold that I would eat it. It was meant for someone called Fionn. It was meant for you."

"Then you shall have half," said Fionn.

"I will not eat one tiny scrap of it," said Finegas. "Eat it all, and I'll watch."

So Fionn ate the Salmon of Knowledge. And afterwards, whenever he wanted to know something hidden, he only had to put his thumb in his mouth, under his wisdom tooth, and the knowledge would come to him.

"You will eat a Salmon of Knowledge yourself one day," Fionn told Finegas. "I promise, and I know it."

Then Fionn said goodbye to his kind teacher, and set off for Tara, where the High King of Ireland lived.

It was Samhain, at the very end of autumn, and all the great people of Ireland had come to Tara for the king's feast. When everyone sat down in the great hall, the High King, Conn of the Hundred Battles, noticed one young man still standing, because there was no seat for him.

"Young man," said the king, "I want to welcome you. What is your name?"

"I am Fionn, the son of Uail, the son of Baiscne," said Fionn.

Everyone in the hall caught their breath. For right there, beside the king, sat Goll mac Morna, the son of Morna and the leader of the Fianna. But the king only said, "You are the son of a friend, and you shall have a friend's seat," and he gave Fionn a place beside his own son.

Now on the night of Samhain, the doors between our world and the Otherworld stand open. And for nine years, on that night, a creature from the Otherworld called Aillen had come to Tara to burn it. He came playing sweet, soft music on a harp and a pipe, and everyone who heard it fell fast asleep. Then he breathed out a blast of fire, and burned whatever it touched.

The king stood up. "Aillen will come tonight," he said. "Who will defend Tara?"

Nobody said a word. Even the bravest champions stared down at their cups. It was the young stranger who stood up.

"What will I be given if I do it?" asked Fionn.

"Whatever you can rightly ask for," said the king.

"Then I will do it," said Fionn.

Everyone wished him luck, but in their hearts they said goodbye, because they were sure he would never come back.

Out in the dark, on the wide plain outside Tara, Fionn waited alone. Then he heard footsteps. It was Fiacuil, the wild man from the marsh.

"I've come to help you," whispered Fiacuil. "Nobody can stay awake when Aillen plays. But take this." And he handed Fionn a terrible magic spear, with its head all wrapped up. "This spear is so fierce and hot and smelly that nobody could fall asleep near it. When you hear the music, unwrap it and hold your face close to it." Then Fiacuil hurried away, afraid of hearing the music himself.

Fionn waited. The night grew darker and darker, and the wind died away. Then, out of the darkness, came the sound of music: low and sweet, sweeter than any music in the world. Fionn's eyes began to close. But he quickly unwrapped the spear and pressed his forehead close to its sizzling point, and he stayed wide awake.

The music stopped. Aillen thought everyone was asleep. He breathed out a great blast of blue fire, like lightning. But Fionn spread out his cloak, and caught the fire in it, and the fire slid off and sank deep into the ground.

Aillen was astonished. He blew again, and again Fionn caught the fire. Then Aillen was afraid. He turned and ran back towards his home inside the hill. Fionn ran after him, faster and faster, and just at the door of the hill, he threw the spear. And Aillen never came to Tara again.

At sunrise, Fionn walked back into Tara. Everyone was amazed to see him alive, and the city safe.

"What do you ask for?" said the king.

"What is rightly mine," said Fionn. "To be leader of the Fianna, like my father."

The king turned to Goll mac Morna. "You must choose, Goll," he said. "Leave Ireland, or put your hand in this young man's hand and follow him."

Goll could do hard things gracefully. "Here is my hand," he said, and he twinkled his eyes at Fionn.

So Fionn became the leader of the Fianna, and the greatest of all its captains. Many years later, his men were resting after a hunt and arguing about what was the finest music in the world.

"The cuckoo calling from the highest tree," said his son, Oisin.

"The ring of a spear on a shield," said his grandson, Oscar.

Others said the belling of a stag across water, or the song of a lark, or the laugh of a happy girl.

"And what do you think, chief?" they asked Fionn.

"The music of what happens," said Fionn. "That is the finest music in the world."
