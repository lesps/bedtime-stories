When Aeneas sailed away from Carthage, a storm blew up. "We'll never reach Italy in this weather," said Palinurus, the ship's pilot. "But Sicily is near. Let's go there."

"Gladly," said Aeneas. "My father Anchises is buried in Sicily."

The king of that part of Sicily was a kind old man named Acestes, whose mother had been a Trojan. He came down to the shore wearing a lion skin, with a spear in his hand, and welcomed them with food and drink.

The next day, Aeneas called everyone together. "It is exactly one year since we buried my father here," he said. "Let us honor him with a festival, and hold games in his memory."

So Aeneas put a wreath of myrtle on his head and went to his father's tomb. He poured out wine and milk and scattered flowers. And as he spoke to his father's spirit, a great snake slid out from under the tomb, with seven coils and scales that shone blue and gold like a rainbow. It glided gently between the altars, tasted the offerings, and slid back under the tomb. Everyone took it as a good sign.

Nine days later, the games began. The prizes were laid out for all to see: crowns and palm branches, swords and purple cloaks, and gold and silver. A trumpet blew, and people came from all over Sicily to watch.

First came the boat race. Four ships took part, each with a captain standing at the back and the rowers sitting ready with their oars, wearing garlands on their heads. Far out in the sea was a rock, and Aeneas had set a leafy branch on it. The ships had to row out, go around the rock and come back.

The trumpet sounded, and off they went! The sea foamed white with the oars, and the crowd on the shore cheered.

Gyas, in a ship called the Chimaera, took the lead. Behind him came Cloanthus in the Scylla, then Mnestheus in the Sea-Horse and Sergestus in the Centaur, side by side.

As Gyas came near the rock, he shouted to his steersman, an old man named Menoetes. "Keep close to the rock! Cut in close!" But Menoetes was afraid of hidden rocks under the water, and he kept steering out to sea.

"Closer, Menoetes!" yelled Gyas. Then he looked back and saw Cloanthus slipping past him on the inside, between his ship and the rock.

Gyas was so angry that he grabbed old Menoetes and threw him overboard, splash! into the sea. Then he took the steering oar himself. Poor Menoetes, soaked and spluttering, climbed up onto the rock and sat there, coughing up salt water. The crowd laughed and laughed.

Now Mnestheus and Sergestus raced for the rock. "Row, my friends!" cried Mnestheus to his men. "I don't ask to be first. But let's not come last!"

Sergestus tried to cut in too close, and crunch! His ship ran onto the rock, his oars snapped, and he was stuck fast. While his men pushed at the rock with poles, Mnestheus sped past him. Then he passed Gyas, whose ship had no steersman now. Only Cloanthus was ahead.

The crowd roared. Mnestheus's men pulled with all their strength, and the Sea-Horse began to catch up with the Scylla. They were almost level. Then Cloanthus stretched out his hands to the sea and prayed. "Gods of the sea, help me win, and I will give you a white bull!"

The sea nymphs heard him. And the god of harbors gave the Scylla a mighty push, and it shot forward, faster than the wind, faster than an arrow, and was first to reach the shore.

Cloanthus won a cloak embroidered with gold. Mnestheus got a suit of armor so heavy that two servants could hardly carry it. Gyas got two bronze cauldrons and silver cups. And last of all, Sergestus came creeping home, his ship rowing on one side only, like a snake that has been run over by a wheel. Even he got a prize, for bringing his ship and his men back safely.

Next came the foot race. Many young men lined up, among them two best friends, Nisus and Euryalus. Off they ran! Nisus took the lead, far ahead of everyone. Behind him came a runner named Salius, and then Euryalus.

But just before the finish, Nisus slipped in a puddle and fell flat in the mud. As he lay there, he saw his friend coming. So he stuck out his leg, and tripped Salius, who went rolling over and over on the ground. And Euryalus ran past them both and won!

Salius was furious. "That's cheating!" he shouted. But the crowd loved Euryalus, and Aeneas said, "The prizes go in the order everyone finished." But he felt sorry for Salius, and gave him a lion skin with golden claws.

Then Nisus held up his muddy arms. "If you give prizes to people who fall over," he said, "what about me? I would have won if I hadn't slipped!" Aeneas laughed, and gave him a fine shield.

Then came the boxing. A huge, boastful young man named Dares strode out. He was so big and strong that nobody dared to box with him. He was about to take the prize when King Acestes nudged an old man named Entellus.

"Entellus," he said, "you were the greatest boxer in all Sicily once. Are you going to let him win without a fight?"

"I'm old now," said Entellus. "My strength is gone." But he stood up and took off his cloak, and showed his huge arms. Then the two of them began.

Dares was young and quick, darting this way and that. Entellus was slow and heavy, and stood still, watching. Then Entellus swung a mighty punch, missed, and fell over with a crash like a falling tree. The crowd gasped. But Entellus got up, angry now, and chased Dares all over the field, punching left and right like a hailstorm. Aeneas stopped the fight before Dares was badly hurt. "Enough," he said. "The gods are on his side today."

Then came the archery. A dove was tied by a string to the top of a ship's mast, and the archers took turns. The first arrow hit the mast. The second arrow cut the string, and the dove flew free. The third archer shot the dove as it flew away. Old King Acestes was last, and he had nothing left to aim at. So he shot his arrow high into the sky, just to show how strong his bow was.

And the arrow burst into flames! It streaked across the sky like a shooting star, leaving a trail of fire, until it burned away to nothing. Everyone stared in wonder. Aeneas hugged the old king and gave him the very best prize of all, a bowl that had belonged to Anchises.

Last of all, the boys of Troy rode out on their ponies, led by young Ascanius. They wore wreaths and golden collars, and they galloped in and out in patterns, pretending to fight a battle, twisting and turning like the paths of a maze, or like dolphins playing in the waves. The Trojans cheered to see how much the boys looked like their brave fathers.

But while everyone was watching the games, the goddess Juno was making trouble. The Trojan women sat apart on the beach, looking out at the sea. They were so tired of sailing. "Seven years we've been wandering," they sighed. "Will we never have a home?"

Juno sent her messenger Iris down to them, disguised as one of the women. "Let's burn the ships!" said Iris. "Then we'll have to stay here." She threw a burning stick at the ships, and then flew away up her rainbow. And the women, in a kind of madness, grabbed burning sticks from the altars and set the ships on fire.

The men came running. Ascanius galloped up and pulled off his helmet. "What are you doing?" he cried. "These are our ships, our hopes! Look, it's me, Ascanius!" The women came to their senses and ran away, ashamed. But the fire was spreading. Aeneas tore his clothes and prayed to Jupiter for help, and at once a great rainstorm poured down from the sky and put out the flames. Only four ships were burned.

That night, the spirit of Anchises came to Aeneas in a dream. "My son," he said, "leave the old and the tired here, with King Acestes. Take only the bravest to Italy. And before you fight for your new home, come down to the land of the dead and visit me. The Sibyl will show you the way."

So Aeneas marked out a new town in Sicily with a plow, for all who wanted to stay, and King Acestes became its ruler. Then, after nine days of feasting and many tearful goodbyes, the rest of the Trojans set sail.

Venus begged Neptune to keep them safe, and he promised that only one man would be lost at sea. That night, as the ships sailed over the calm sea, the god of sleep came down to Palinurus, who was steering Aeneas's ship. "Rest a while," he whispered. "I will steer for you." But Palinurus held on tight to the steering oar and kept his eyes on the stars. "I don't trust the sea," he said.

So the god of sleep shook a branch dripping with the water of forgetfulness over his eyes. Palinurus's eyes closed, and the god pushed him overboard into the sea, still holding a piece of the steering oar. He called for help, but no one heard him.

Later Aeneas woke and found the ship drifting, with no one at the helm. He took the steering oar himself and steered through the night, sad for the loss of his faithful friend.
