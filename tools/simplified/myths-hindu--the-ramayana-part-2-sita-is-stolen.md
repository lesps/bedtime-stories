For thirteen years Rama, Sita and Lakshmana lived in the forest. At last they made their home in a little cottage of leaves at Panchavati, by the river Godavari. Rama and Sita were happy there together, and in the long days the brothers told each other stories of the olden times.

But this was a land where the Rakshasas roamed. And one day a Rakshasa woman called Shurpanakha came by the cottage. She was the sister of Ravana, the ten-headed king of the Rakshasas, who ruled the island of Lanka. When she saw Rama, so tall and strong and handsome, she fell in love with him at once.

"Who are you?" she asked. "And why do you live in this dark forest, where the Rakshasas rule?"

Rama told her who he was, and why he had come to the forest. "And who are you?" he asked.

"I am Shurpanakha," she said, "sister of Ravana, the king of Lanka. I want you to be my husband. Send that pale little wife of yours away! I'll get rid of her and your brother, and you and I will roam the mountains together."

Rama smiled. "I have a wife already," he said. "But my brother Lakshmana has none. Why don't you ask him?"

So Shurpanakha turned to Lakshmana. But Lakshmana only laughed at her. "I am just Rama's servant," he said. "Wouldn't you rather be married to the master?"

Then Shurpanakha flew into a rage. "You are making fun of me!" she screamed. And she rushed at Sita to kill her. Sita fell to the ground in terror, but Rama jumped up to protect her. And Lakshmana drew his sword, and wounded Shurpanakha, and she ran away howling into the forest.

She ran to her brothers Khara and Dushana, who lived in the forest, and they came with their army to fight Rama and Lakshmana. But Rama and Lakshmana beat them all. So Shurpanakha went to Lanka, to her brother Ravana, and to Ravana's friend Maricha, and told them everything.

Soon afterwards, Sita was wandering in the woods when she saw a wonderful deer. Its coat shone with gold, its sides were silvery white, and its antlers sparkled like sapphires. "Rama! Lakshmana! Come and look at this beautiful deer!" she called.

"Be careful, Sita," said Lakshmana. "No real deer has jewels on its coat. This is a trick of the Rakshasas, who can take any shape they like."

But Sita could not stop looking at the deer. "Rama," she begged, "please catch it for me! If we catch it alive, it can live with us here, and when we go home to Ayodhya, I will look after it in the palace. And if you can't catch it alive, bring me its golden skin, to remember the forest by."

So Rama told Lakshmana to stay and look after Sita, and he went after the deer. For a long time he followed it through the woods and valleys, but it slipped away from every trap. At last Rama shot it with an arrow. And as it fell, the deer, which was really the Rakshasa Maricha, cried out in Rama's own voice: "Help me, Lakshmana! I am dying!"

Back at the cottage, Sita heard the cry. "That was Rama!" she said. "He is in danger! Go and help him, Lakshmana, quickly!"

"Don't be afraid, Sita," said Lakshmana. "Nobody in the world can beat Rama. That was a trick. And Rama told me to stay here with you."

But Sita was wild with fear for Rama. She said cruel things to Lakshmana. She said he didn't care if his brother died.

Lakshmana trembled, and there were tears in his eyes. "You are like a mother to me," he said, "and I won't answer you back. But your words hurt me like burning arrows. Very well, I will go to Rama. May the spirits of the forest watch over you while I am gone." And he went.

Now Sita was alone. And that was what Ravana had been waiting for.

Ravana came to the cottage dressed as a holy man, in a hermit's robe, with sandals on his feet and a bowl hanging from his staff. The whole forest fell silent. The trees stood still, the breeze stopped blowing, and the animals made no sound. Ravana looked at Sita, and praised her beauty. "Who are you, lovely lady?" he asked. "You should be living in a palace, not in this wild forest."

Sita thought he was a real holy man. She told him who she was, and how Rama had been sent away from home. "Rest here," she said kindly, "until my husband and his brother come back with food."

"I am no holy man," said Ravana. "I am Ravana, the king of the Rakshasas, and the ruler of Lanka. Come with me, Sita. Be my queen, and you shall have five thousand maids to wait on you."

Sita's eyes flashed with anger. "I am the wife of Rama!" she said. "You might as well try to pull a tooth from a hungry lion, or tear a mountain up by its roots, as try to take me from him!"

But Ravana seized Sita, and lifted her into his golden chariot, which was pulled through the air by flying donkeys. Up into the sky it rose, over the hills and the forest. And Sita cried out, "Lakshmana, save me! You were right, and I was wrong. Forgive me! Rama, help me!" And she called to the trees and the flowers, the mountains and the forest spirits: "Tell Rama that Ravana has carried me away!"

The vulture Jatayu heard her cries. He flew up and fought Ravana with his beak and claws to stop him. But Ravana struck him down, and Jatayu fell to the ground, badly hurt.

And Ravana flew on with Sita, far over the ocean, to his island of Lanka.
