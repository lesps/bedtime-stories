All over Germany, people used to tell of the Wild Hunt. On stormy nights, they said, you could hear it go roaring through the sky: the thunder of hooves, the blowing of horns and the barking of hounds. Some said it was the old god Wodan out hunting. Others told this story.

Once there was a count, a great lord, who loved hunting more than anything in the world.

One Sunday morning, when the church bells were ringing and the people were singing in the cathedral, the count blew his hunting horn. "Halloo! Halloo!" he cried. His horse reared up, and all his huntsmen and hounds came rushing after him, through the fields and over the hills.

As they galloped across a crossroads, two strangers came riding up, one on his right and one on his left. The rider on the right had a silver horse, and a kind, gentle face, as bright as spring. The rider on the left had a fiery red horse, and a grim face, and his eyes flashed like lightning.

"Welcome!" cried the count. "Welcome to the hunt! There is no better fun on earth or in heaven!"

"Your horn does not sound right with the church bells," said the gentle rider on the right. "Turn back. Nothing good will come of hunting today."

"Hunt on, hunt on, my lord!" said the rider on the left. "Never mind the bells! Hunting is the sport of princes."

"Well said!" cried the count to the rider on the left. "You are a man after my own heart!"

So on they galloped. Soon a white stag sprang up in front of them, with great branching antlers, and the count blew his horn louder than ever.

The stag hid in a field of ripe corn. A poor farmer came running out. "Mercy, my lord!" he begged. "Please don't ride through my corn! It is all I have!"

The gentle rider on the right warned the count again. But the fierce rider on the left laughed, and urged him on. And the count listened to the rider on the left.

"Out of my way!" he shouted at the farmer. And the whole hunt jumped over the hedge and trampled the corn flat.

The stag ran on, to a meadow where cows were grazing, and it hid among them. The hounds came after it. The herdsman threw himself down in front of the count. "Mercy, my lord! Leave my cows in peace! Some of them belong to poor widows, and they are all they have in the world!"

Again the gentle rider warned the count. Again the fierce rider urged him on. And again the count listened to the fierce one.

"Set the dogs on them!" he cried. And the hounds rushed at the herd, and chased the poor cows and the herdsman away across the meadow, in a great terrible tumble.

The tired stag ran into the dark forest, to the little chapel of a holy hermit, and hid inside. The hunt came crashing after it. The old hermit stepped out of his hut.

"Stop!" he said gently. "This is God's house. Every creature is crying out to heaven about what you have done. This is your last warning."

For the third time the gentle rider warned the count. For the third time the fierce rider urged him on. And for the third time the count listened to the fierce one.

"I don't care!" he shouted. "I will have my fun!" He cracked his whip and blew his horn. "At it, at it!"

And suddenly, everything was gone. The hermit was gone, and his hut. The huntsmen were gone, and their horses, and the hounds. All the noise of the hunt was swallowed up in silence.

The frightened count looked around. He blew his horn, but it made no sound. He shouted, but he could not hear his own voice. He kicked his horse, but it would not move forward or back.

Then it grew dark around him, darker and darker, and there was a roaring like a faraway sea. And a voice like thunder spoke from high above his head.

"You cruel man! You have been cruel to people and to animals. You have heard their cries, and you did not care. So now you shall be hunted yourself, forever and ever!"

Yellow lightning flashed through the trees. Flames of green and blue and red leaped up around him. And out of the ground came a thousand ghostly hounds, howling and snapping at his heels.

The count fled, through forest and field. But through the whole wide world the hounds came barking after him, by day through the deep places under the earth, and by night high up through the sky.

And that, the old story says, is the Wild Hunt, which will go on until the end of the world. So if you hear the wind howling on a stormy night, think of the Wild Huntsman, and remember to be kind to people and to animals.
