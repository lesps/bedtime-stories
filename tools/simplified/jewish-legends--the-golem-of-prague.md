Long ago, in the old city of Prague, there lived a wise and learned rabbi, Rabbi Loew. From his study in the Jewish quarter he could see the river and the narrow streets around the old cemetery, where his grave can still be seen today.

Rabbi Loew was a great scholar, and he did wonderful things. At night people saw little flashes of blue and red light from his window, where he did his experiments, and they whispered that he was a magician who could call up spirits. So people were a little afraid of him, and no one would come to work for him as a servant.

"If they say I am a magician," the rabbi said to himself, "why shouldn't I make myself a servant?"

So he set to work. After a few weeks he had made a creature shaped like a big, strong woman. Then, at midnight, he wrote the holy Name of God on a piece of parchment, rolled it up, and put it in the creature's mouth.

At once the creature jumped up and began to move! It rolled its eyes and waved its arms, and almost walked straight out of the window. In alarm the rabbi snatched the parchment out of its mouth, and the creature fell down, still.

"I must be careful," said Rabbi Loew.

Soon the creature was running errands for the rabbi and doing all kinds of jobs. She could do everything except speak. But the rabbi learned that he must always take the Name out of her mouth before he went to sleep, or she might get into mischief.

One cold Sabbath afternoon, while the rabbi was preaching in the synagogue, some children stood outside his house looking at the creature by the window. When they rolled their eyes, she rolled hers. "Come and play with us!" they shouted. And she jumped straight out of the window.

"We're cold," said one child. "Can you make us a fire?"

The creature had been made to obey, so she gathered sticks and lit a fire in the street, and danced round it with the children. But she kept piling on sticks and old barrels, until the fire spread to a house. The children ran away in fright. Before the fire could be put out, a number of houses had burned down. The creature was nowhere to be found. Only the parchment with the holy Name, which could not burn, was found in the ashes.

The city council was angry, and Rabbi Loew was called before King Rudolf. "Is it not a sin to make a living creature?" said the king.

"It had no life except what the holy Name gave it," said the rabbi.

"I don't understand," said the king. "You will be locked up, and you must make another creature, so that I can see it for myself. If it is as you say, you shall live. If not, you shall die, and all your people will be sent away from this city."

So the rabbi set to work again, and this time he made a man, much bigger than the woman. "As Your Majesty can see," he said, "it is only a thing of wood and glue, with springs in its joints. Now watch." And he put the holy Name in its mouth.

Slowly the creature stood up, and saluted the king. The king was delighted. "Give him to me, rabbi!" he cried.

"I cannot," said Rabbi Loew. "The holy Name must stay with me, or the creature may do great harm again. This time I will be careful, and I will not use him on the Sabbath."

The king saw that this was wise, and he set the rabbi free and let him take the creature home. People stared in wonder when they saw the creature walking down the street beside Rabbi Loew, but the children ran away, crying, "The bogey-man!"

This time the rabbi was very careful. Every Friday, just before the Sabbath began, he took the Name out of the creature's mouth, so that it could not move.

The creature grew more wonderful every day. And one evening, it woke the rabbi from his nap by speaking! "I want to be a soldier, and fight for the king," it said. "I belong to the king. You made me for him."

"Silence!" said the rabbi, and the creature had to obey. But Rabbi Loew was worried. "This monster must not become my master," he thought. "It could do terrible harm to me, and to all my people."

He began to wonder whether the king had been right, and it was wrong to make a man. The creature grew surly and disobedient. But still the rabbi did not break it up, because it was so useful. It did all his cooking and washing and cleaning, faster and better than three servants.

Then one Friday afternoon, as the rabbi was getting ready to go to the synagogue, he heard a great noise in the street. "Come quickly!" people shouted at his door. "Your bogey-man is trying to break into the synagogue!"

Rabbi Loew rushed out. The monster had slipped out of the house, and it was battering at the door of the synagogue.

"What are you doing?" the rabbi demanded.

"I am breaking into the synagogue to destroy the scrolls of the holy Law," said the monster. "Then you will have no power over me. And I will make a great army of bogey-men, to fight for the king and to destroy your people."

"I will stop you first!" cried Rabbi Loew. He sprang forward and snatched the parchment with the Name out of the creature's mouth, so fast that it crashed down at his feet, nothing but a heap of broken springs and bits of wood and glue.

For many years afterwards, the pieces were kept in the attic of the synagogue, and people who came to visit were shown them, and told the story of the rabbi's bogey-man.
