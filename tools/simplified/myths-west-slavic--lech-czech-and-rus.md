title: Lech, Czech and Rus
basedOn: the legend of the three brothers as the Greater Poland Chronicle (about 1295) tells it, with the later Polish story of the white eagle's nest at Gniezno and the Czech story of Čech on the hill of Říp

Long, long ago, there were three brothers called Lech, Czech and Rus. They lived with their families and their people in a land far to the south. But their people grew so many that there was no longer room for them all. So the three brothers decided to set off together and look for new lands where their people could live.

They travelled for a long time, through forests and over rivers and across wide plains. At last they said goodbye to each other. "Let each of us go his own way," they said, "and find a home for his own people."

Czech went to the west. He led his people through the forests until they came to a lonely hill called Říp, standing all by itself in the middle of a wide plain. Czech climbed to the top of the hill and looked all around. He saw green meadows and dark forests, and rivers full of fish. "This is a land flowing with milk and honey," he said. "Here we will stay." And his people settled there, and that land is Bohemia, the land of the Czechs.

Rus went to the east, and his people settled in the wide lands there. And those people were called Russians, after him.

Lech went to the north. He led his people across the plains until they came to a beautiful country of fields and forests and lakes. One evening, as the sun was setting, Lech saw a great old oak tree. And at the top of the oak was a nest, and in the nest was a white eagle.

As Lech watched, the white eagle spread out its great wings. The setting sun shone behind it, and the whole sky turned red. And the white eagle shone against the red sky.

"This is a sign," said Lech. "Here we will build our home. And the white eagle on the red sky shall be our sign for ever."

So Lech built a town there, beside the oak tree, and called it Gniezno, which means "the nest." And his people settled all around, and they became the Poles.

And that is why, to this day, the flag of Poland is white and red, and its sign is a white eagle on a red shield.
