Phaethon was a boy whose father was Apollo, the god of the sun. But Phaethon had never met his father.

One day at school, another boy laughed at him. "You? The son of the Sun? I don't believe it!"

Phaethon ran home to his mother, red-faced. "Is it true?" he asked. "Is the Sun really my father? Give me proof!"

His mother pointed up at the sky. "As the Sun is my witness, it is true," she said. "If you don't believe me, go and ask him yourself. His palace is just beyond the eastern edge of the world, where the sun comes up."

So Phaethon traveled east until he came to the palace of the Sun. It shone with gold and jewels, and it was so bright that he had to shade his eyes. Inside, Apollo sat on a glittering throne. Around him stood the Days and the Months and the Years. Spring wore a crown of flowers. Summer wore a crown of wheat. Autumn's feet were purple from stomping grapes, and Winter had frost in his hair.

"Why have you come, my son?" asked Apollo.

"Father," said Phaethon, "people say I'm not really your son. Please give me a sign, so everyone will know."

Apollo took off his crown of sunbeams so the boy could come closer. Then he hugged him. "You are my son," he said. "And to prove it, I will give you anything you ask. I promise, by the river Styx." That was the most serious promise a god could make. It could never be broken.

"Then let me drive your chariot," said Phaethon. "Just for one day."

Apollo's face fell. "Oh, my son, ask for anything but that. No one can drive the sun chariot but me. Not even Zeus. The horses are wild and strong, and they breathe fire. The road across the sky is steep and dangerous. Please, choose something else."

But Phaethon would not change his mind, and Apollo had made a promise he could not break.

So, sadly, Apollo led his son to the golden chariot. The doors of the dawn swung open, and the stars faded away. The Hours led out the four fiery horses. Apollo rubbed a special cream on Phaethon's face to protect him from the heat, and put the crown of sunbeams on his head.

"Hold the reins tight," he said. "Don't use the whip. Stay on the middle path. Not too high, or you'll burn the sky. Not too low, or you'll burn the earth."

Phaethon jumped into the chariot, and the horses leaped into the sky.

At once the horses knew something was wrong. The chariot felt too light. This driver was not their master. They bolted off the path and galloped wherever they liked.

Phaethon pulled on the reins, but the horses would not listen. He looked down at the earth, so far below, and his knees shook. He wished he had never asked.

The horses ran too high, and the stars felt the heat. Then they ran too low, and the earth began to burn. Forests caught fire. Rivers boiled and dried up. Grassy lands turned into deserts. Mountain snow melted, and the mountaintops smoked.

The Earth cried out to Zeus. "Help me! Everything is burning!"

Zeus climbed his highest tower. He took a bolt of lightning and threw it at the chariot. It knocked Phaethon right out of his seat. He fell through the sky like a shooting star, and splashed down into a river far below. The wild horses broke free and ran home to their stable.

Phaethon's sisters came to the river and cried for their brother. They cried so long that they turned into poplar trees on the riverbank. And their tears, falling into the water, turned into drops of golden amber.

Apollo was so sad that he hid his face, and for one whole day, the world had no sun.
