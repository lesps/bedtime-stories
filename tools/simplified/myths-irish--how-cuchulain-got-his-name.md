The greatest hero of Ulster was called Cuchulain. You say it "koo-HULL-in". But that was not the name he was born with. When he was a little boy, his name was Setanta. This is the story of how he got his new name.

In those days, Conor, the king of Ulster, kept a band of boys at his court, a hundred and fifty of them, the sons of all the chiefs. They lived together and learned to be warriors, and they spent a great deal of time playing hurling. Hurling is a game played with a stick and a ball, and you score by hitting the ball into a hole at the other end of the field.

In Ulster there lived a famous smith called Culain, who made swords and spears. Culain wanted to give a feast for the king. But he was not rich, and his house was not big. "Please bring only a few of your best warriors," he said to the king, "for my house cannot hold a crowd." So the king said he would come with just a small company.

On the evening of the feast, the king went down to the playing field to say goodbye to the boys before he set off. And there he saw something so amazing that he could not tear himself away.

At one end of the field, a hundred and fifty boys were all trying to stop one little boy from getting his ball into their goal. But they couldn't stop him. He drove his ball right past them all and into the hole. Then they changed sides. All hundred and fifty boys hit their balls at once at the little boy's goal, and he stopped every single one of them. Then they wrestled, and he threw them all to the ground, but all of them together could not make him move one foot.

The little boy was Setanta. He was only about six or seven years old.

"That boy is a wonder," said the king. "If only he grows up to be as good a man as he is a boy."

"Don't worry about that," said Fergus, one of the king's warriors. "As his games are better than other boys' games, so his deeds will be greater than other heroes' deeds."

"Call him over," said the king. "He shall come to the feast with us."

But when Setanta came, he said, "Thank you, but I can't come just now."

"Why not?" asked the king, surprised.

"Because the boys haven't finished playing yet."

"I can't wait for you," said the king. "It's getting late."

"Don't wait," said Setanta. "I'll finish this game and then run after you."

"But do you know the way?"

"I'll follow the tracks of your chariot wheels and your horses on the road," said Setanta.

So the king went on to Culain's house without him. Culain welcomed them, and there were fresh rushes on the floor, fires burning, a great tub of ale, and meat roasting on the spits. Everyone sat down in his own place and hung his weapons on the wall above his head.

Before they began to eat, Culain asked the king, "Is anyone else coming tonight, or are all your company here?"

"All are here," said the king. He had quite forgotten about the little boy. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I have a watchdog," said Culain. "He is as fierce and strong as a hundred ordinary dogs. When I let him off his chain at night to guard the house, nobody dares come anywhere near. He obeys no one but me."

"Let him loose, then," said the king. "Everyone is here."

So Culain let the great hound loose. It bounded out over the wall, raced around the whole countryside, and came back panting. Then it lay down in front of the house, with its head on its paws, watching the road.

Meanwhile, the boys finished their game. Setanta set off along the road after the king, hitting his ball ahead of him with his hurley stick and running after it, so the journey seemed short. When he came to Culain's gate, the great hound saw him, and gave a howl that echoed all over the countryside.

Inside the house, the king and his warriors heard it, and they were struck dumb with fear. "Oh, no," said the king. "That's my foster son, Setanta! He promised to follow me. The hound will have killed him!"

Outside, the hound came charging at Setanta with its huge jaws wide open. Setanta had nothing in his hands but his ball and his hurley stick. So with all his strength, he hit the ball straight into the hound's open mouth. And while the hound stopped short, choking, Setanta grabbed it with both hands and swung it against the stone gatepost. And the great hound fell dead.

The king's men were all running out by then. They didn't even wait for the gates to open, but jumped over the walls. Fergus got there first. He found the little boy standing safe, with the great hound dead beside him. Fergus lifted Setanta up onto his shoulder and carried him in to the king, and put him down at the king's knee.

But Culain went out and found his dog, and he came back very sad. "I'm sorry I ever gave this feast," he said. "That dog guarded my house and my cattle and everything I own. Without him, everything I have will be stolen. Little boy, you are welcome here for your family's sake, but not for your own."

"Don't be upset," said Setanta. "I will choose my own punishment. If there is a puppy of that dog's breed anywhere in Ireland, I will bring it up for you until it is big enough to be your watchdog. And until it is grown, I myself will be your hound. I will guard your house and your cattle, and nothing of yours will be stolen without my knowing it."

"That's a fair offer," said everyone. "And from now on, your name won't be Setanta any more. You will be called Cuchulain, the Hound of Culain."

"I like my own name better," said the boy.

"Don't say that," said the king's druid. "One day the name Cuchulain will be on everyone's lips. It will be famous among the bravest heroes in the whole world."

"In that case," said the boy, "I'm happy with it."

So from that day on, he was called Cuchulain. And when he grew up, he guarded the whole of Ulster against its enemies, just as he had promised to guard Culain's house. And then people loved best to call him the Hound of Ulster.
