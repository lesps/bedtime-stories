At last the morning came when Ellen was to be married to old Sir Stephen of Trent. Robin Hood got up early, and so did all his merry men, and last of all, yawning and rubbing his eyes, so did Friar Tuck.

After breakfast, Robin chose twenty of his best men to come with him. Then he went into the bushes and came out dressed as a wandering minstrel, in a bright coat covered with ribbons, with a harp slung over his shoulder. All his men stared, and many of them laughed.

"I know it's a bit gaudy, like a grasshopper," said Robin, looking down at himself. "But it suits me rather well. Little John, carry these two bags for me."

Little John weighed them in his hand. "Master, I hear gold clinking!"

"It's my own money," said Robin. "Come on, lads, let's go."

So they marched out of Sherwood Forest, with Allan a Dale and Friar Tuck in the middle, into the green valley where Ellen lived, past fields of barley and meadows full of sheep. They came to a little church where the wedding was to be held. Across the road from the church was a stone wall covered with sweet-smelling honeysuckle, and the outlaws jumped over it and hid behind it in the shade.

"David of Doncaster," said Robin, "climb up on the wall and hide in the honeysuckle, and tell me when you see someone coming."

So they waited. Allan paced up and down, too nervous to sit still. Friar Tuck fell asleep and snored, with a sound like someone slowly sawing wood.

"David, what do you see?" asked Robin.

"I see white clouds floating, and three black crows flying, but nothing else, master."

After a while Robin asked again. "I see windmills turning, and three tall poplar trees swaying, but nothing else."

And at last David said, "Now an old friar is coming over the hill with a big bunch of keys, and he is going to the church door."

Robin shook Friar Tuck awake. "Get up, holy man! Go and talk to that friar, and get yourself into the church, so you'll be there when we need you."

So Friar Tuck went across to the church, where the old friar was struggling with the rusty lock. "Let me help you, brother," said Tuck, and he opened the door with one turn of the key. "I've come all the way from Fountain Dale. May I sit inside and watch this fine wedding?"

"You are welcome, brother," said the old friar.

Then Robin, in his minstrel's clothes, sat down on a bench beside the church door, while Little John and Will Stutely went inside.

Soon six men came riding up. The first was the Bishop of Hereford, dressed in rich silk, with a heavy gold chain around his neck and jewels sparkling on his velvet cap. Beside him rode the Prior of Emmet, who owned the church. Robin frowned. "That Bishop is far too fancy for a holy man," he thought. "All that silk and gold was paid for by poor people working hard."

"Hello, fellow!" said the Bishop. "Who are you, dressed up in such bright feathers?"

"I'm a harper from the north," said Robin, "and nobody in England plays like me. If you let me play at this wedding, my Lord Bishop, I promise that the bride will love the man she marries for as long as they both live."

"Really?" said the Bishop. "If you can make this girl love the man she marries, I'll give you whatever you ask. Play something now!"

"No," said Robin. "I won't play until the bride and bridegroom come."

"You're a rude fellow," said the Bishop, frowning. "Ah, here comes my cousin, Sir Stephen."

Up the road came a tall, thin knight with grey hair, dressed all in black silk. Beside him rode Ellen's father, a stout farmer called Edward. Behind them came Ellen herself, and six soldiers. When Robin saw Ellen, he understood why the knight wanted to marry her, for she was the most beautiful girl he had ever seen. But she was pale and drooping, like a lily with a broken stem, and she walked into the church with her head bowed.

"Why don't you play?" said the Bishop sternly.

"I'll play, all right," said Robin calmly, "more than you think. But not until the right moment."

"After this wedding," thought the Bishop, "I'll have this saucy fellow whipped."

Now Ellen and Sir Stephen stood before the altar, and the Bishop opened his book. Ellen looked around in despair, like a frightened deer. Then Robin Hood, in all his fluttering ribbons, strode up the aisle and stood between the bride and the bridegroom.

"Let me look at this girl!" he said loudly. "Why, she has lilies in her cheeks instead of roses. This is no proper wedding. Sir Knight, you are so old and she is so young. You are not her true love, and you shall not marry her!"

Everyone stood frozen with surprise. Then Robin put his horn to his lips and blew three blasts that rang from the floor to the roof. At once Little John and Will Stutely jumped to his side with their swords drawn, and a great voice boomed from the organ loft, "Here I am, master, whenever you want me!" It was Friar Tuck.

Ellen's father rushed forward to drag her away, but Little John stood in his way. Sir Stephen reached for his sword, but he had not brought it to his own wedding. His soldiers drew their swords. But just then there was a great noise at the door, and in rushed eighteen outlaws in Lincoln green, with Allan a Dale at their head. Allan knelt and gave Robin his good yew bow.

"Allan a Dale!" shouted Ellen's father. "Is this your doing?"

"No," said Robin. "It's my doing, and I don't care who knows it. My name is Robin Hood."

At that name, everyone went quiet. The Prior and his monks huddled together like frightened sheep, and the Bishop crossed himself.

"I mean you no harm," said Robin. "But here is the man Ellen promised to marry, and she shall marry him."

"No!" said her father. "I say she shall marry Sir Stephen."

But Sir Stephen had been standing silent and proud. "Take your daughter back," he said coldly. "I would not marry her now for all of England. I loved her, old as I am. But I did not know that she loved this young man, and that he loved her. If she would rather have a minstrel than a knight, let her choose." And he walked proudly out of the church with his soldiers.

"I have no business here either," said the Bishop quickly, and he tried to leave. But Robin caught hold of his robe. "Wait, my Lord Bishop. I have something more to say to you."

Then Robin turned to Ellen's father. "Give your blessing to your daughter's marriage, and all will be well. Little John, give me the bags of gold. Look, farmer, here are two hundred gold coins for your daughter's wedding present. Give your blessing and you shall have them. Refuse, and she will be married anyway, and you won't get a penny. Choose."

Ellen's father frowned and thought it over. He was a sensible man, after all. "If the girl will go her own way, let her," he grumbled. "I will give her my blessing when she is married."

"But they can't be married," said one of the monks. "There is no priest here, and the wedding hasn't been properly announced."

"No priest?" roared Friar Tuck from the loft. "I'm as good a priest as you any day of the week! And I'll announce the wedding myself." And the old ballad says that he announced it nine times over, to be sure. Then he came down and married Allan and Ellen.

Robin counted out the two hundred gold coins, and Ellen's father gave his blessing, though not very cheerfully. The outlaws crowded around to shake Allan's hand, and Allan held Ellen's hand, dizzy with happiness.

Then Robin turned to the Bishop. "My Lord Bishop, you promised that if I made this girl love her husband, you would give me whatever I asked. Well, she loves her husband, and she wouldn't have married him without me. So please give me that gold chain around your neck as a wedding present for the bride."

The Bishop's face went red with anger. But when he looked at Robin's face, he took off the chain and handed it over. Robin hung it around Ellen's neck, and it sparkled on her shoulders. "Thank you for your handsome present," said Robin. "You look much better without it. And if you ever come near Sherwood, I hope to give you a feast like none you've ever had."

"Heaven forbid!" cried the Bishop. For he knew that Robin's guests always had to pay a great deal for their dinner.

So the outlaws went home to the greenwood, with Allan and his bride in the middle. On the way, Friar Tuck tugged Robin's sleeve. "You lead a merry life, master," he said. "Don't you think you need a good, strong chaplain like me to look after you all? I do love this life." And Robin laughed and told him he could stay and be one of the band.

That night there was such a feast in the greenwood as Nottinghamshire had never seen. You and I were not invited, and that is a pity. So, in case we both feel too sorry about it, I will say no more.
