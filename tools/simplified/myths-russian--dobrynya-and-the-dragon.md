---
title: Dobrynya and the Dragon
basedOn: the Russian byliny (sung epics) of Dobrynya Nikitich and the dragon Gorynych, which collectors wrote down from village singers in northern Russia in the nineteenth century
workId: russian-dobrynya
order: 5
---

Long ago, in the days of Prince Vladimir of Kiev, there lived a young hero called Dobrynya Nikitich. His father had died, and he lived with his mother.

One day Dobrynya told his mother that he was going riding out on the open plain. His mother was worried. "My son," she said, "listen to me. Don't ride as far as the Sorochinsk mountains, where the dragon lives. Don't trample the dragon's young ones. And whatever you do, don't swim in the Puchai river. It is an angry river."

But Dobrynya did not listen. He rode far out across the plain, all the way to the Sorochinsk mountains, and trampled the little dragons under his horse's hooves. Then he came to the Puchai river. The day was hot, and the water looked cool, so he took off his clothes and his armour, left his horse and his weapons with his servant on the bank, and jumped in to swim.

All at once a dark cloud came over the sky, with thunder and lightning. It was no cloud. It was the dragon Gorynych, and he had three heads, and he came flying down at Dobrynya with fire and smoke pouring from his mouths.

"Now, Dobrynya," roared the dragon, "you are in my power! Shall I burn you up, or shall I carry you off to my cave?"

Dobrynya's servant was so frightened that he ran away, and took the horse and all the weapons with him. Dobrynya swam to the bank, but there was nothing there to fight with. Then he saw, lying on the sand, a big, heavy hat, the kind that pilgrims brought back from the land of the Greeks. He picked it up, and swung it round, and struck the dragon with it, and knocked the dragon down onto the ground.

"Mighty Dobrynya, don't kill me!" cried the dragon. "Let's make a promise to each other. I will never fly to Russia again, or carry off any Russian people. And you will never come to my mountains, or trample my young ones."

Dobrynya agreed, and he let the dragon go.

But the dragon did not keep his promise. He flew straight over the city of Kiev, and there he saw the Prince's niece, Zabava, walking in the garden. He swooped down, caught her up, and carried her away to his cave in the Sorochinsk mountains.

Prince Vladimir was heartbroken. He held a great feast for all his knights and heroes, and asked, "Which of you will ride to the Sorochinsk mountains and bring Zabava home?"

The knights all hid behind each other, and nobody answered. Then one of them said, "Dobrynya is the one to go. They say he and the dragon have made promises to each other. The dragon will listen to him."

So the Prince ordered Dobrynya to go. "Bring Zabava home," he said, "or you will lose your head."

Dobrynya went home very sad. "What's the matter, my son?" asked his mother. And he told her everything.

"Don't be sad," said his mother. "Go to sleep now. The morning is wiser than the evening."

In the morning, she gave him his father's old horse, which had been standing in the stable for years. And she gave him a silken whip. "When you come to the Sorochinsk mountains," she said, "the little dragons will wind themselves around your horse's legs, so that he cannot move. Then whip him with this silken whip, between his ears and between his legs, and he will shake them off."

So Dobrynya rode to the Sorochinsk mountains. And it happened just as his mother had said. Hundreds of little dragons came wriggling out and wound themselves around the horse's legs, and the horse could not take a single step. So Dobrynya whipped him with the silken whip, and the horse leaped and kicked, and shook off all the little dragons, and trampled them.

Then out of his cave came the dragon Gorynych. "Dobrynya!" he roared. "You have broken your promise! You have come to my mountains and trampled my young ones!"

"And you broke your promise first," said Dobrynya. "You flew to Russia, and carried off Zabava. Give her back!"

"I won't give her back without a fight," said the dragon.

So they fought. They fought for one day, and for two days, and for three days. Dobrynya grew so tired that he was ready to give up. Then he heard a voice from heaven. "Dobrynya, you have fought for three days. Fight for just three hours more, and you will win."

So Dobrynya fought on for three more hours, and at last he struck the dragon down, and the dragon never rose again.

The dragon's black blood flooded out over the ground, deeper and deeper, until it came up to the horse's knees. And the earth would not soak it up. Dobrynya was stuck there for three days. At last he struck the ground with his spear and cried, "Open up, Mother Earth, and drink up this dragon's blood!" And the earth opened up and swallowed all the blood.

Then Dobrynya went into the dragon's caves. There he found hundreds of Russian prisoners, whom the dragon had carried off over the years. He set them all free, and sent them home. In the very last cave, he found Zabava. And he lifted her onto his horse and carried her all the way home to her uncle, Prince Vladimir, in the city of Kiev.
