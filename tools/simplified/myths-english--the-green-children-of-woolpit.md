---
title: The Green Children of Woolpit
basedOn: the accounts of William of Newburgh and Ralph of Coggeshall, who wrote down the story in the late 1100s and early 1200s
workId: english-green-children
order: 6
---

This is a true story, or so the people who first wrote it down believed. Two monks wrote about it, more than eight hundred years ago, and they both said they had heard it from people who had seen the green children with their own eyes.

In the days of King Stephen, in Suffolk, in England, there was a village called Woolpit. It was named after some old, deep pits nearby, which people said had been dug long ago to trap wolves.

One harvest time, the reapers were out in the fields, cutting the corn, when two children came climbing out of one of the wolf pits: a boy and a girl. They were dressed in strange clothes, made of cloth that nobody had ever seen before. They spoke a language that nobody could understand. And their skin, from head to toe, was green.

The reapers were astonished. They took the children to the house of a knight who lived nearby, Sir Richard de Calne. The children cried and cried. Food was put in front of them, bread and all sorts of good things, but they would not eat any of it, though they were very hungry.

Then some beans were brought in from the fields, still on their stalks. The children grabbed them eagerly. But they didn't know how to get the beans out. They broke open the stalks, looking for the beans inside, and found nothing, and cried again. Then someone showed them how to open the pods, and they ate the beans happily. For a long time they would eat nothing else.

Little by little, the children learned to eat bread and other food. And as they did, their green color slowly faded, until they looked just like everybody else. They were baptized, and they learned to speak English.

But the boy, who was the younger of the two, did not grow strong. He fell ill, and not long afterwards, he died. His sister was very sad. But she grew up healthy, and she worked for many years in the knight's house. In the end she married a man from King's Lynn, and lived there.

When she had learned English, people asked her, "Where did you come from?"

"We come from a land where everyone is green," she said. "We call it St Martin's Land. The sun never rises there. It is always dim, like twilight just after sunset. Across a wide river, we could see another land, full of light, but we never went there."

"And how did you come here?" they asked.

"One day we were looking after our father's sheep," she said. "We heard a wonderful sound of bells, like church bells ringing. We followed the sound, and we came into a long, dark cave, and we walked and walked until we came out at the other end. And there, all of a sudden, was your bright sunshine, and your warm air. We were so dazzled that we lay down on the ground, until the reapers found us. We wanted to go back. But we could never find the way into the cave again."

Nobody has ever found out who the green children really were, or where St Martin's Land could be. People still wonder about it today. And if you go to Woolpit, the village sign shows the two green children, standing hand in hand.
