Long ago, when Christianity was new in Ireland, a holy man called Finnian heard about a chief in Ulster who still followed the old gods. "He is a wise man, and a powerful one," people told him. "Some say he is a magician."

"Then I will go and see him," said Finnian.

But when he got there, the chief would not let him in. He barred his door and shuttered his windows. So Finnian sat down on the ground outside the door, and said he would not eat a single thing until he was let in.

All that day he sat there. The chief kept sending his servants to peep through the spy-hole. "Is he still there?" "He is still there." "He'll be gone by morning," said the chief. But in the morning Finnian was still there. And all that day too. The chief walked up and down all night, unable to sleep, thinking of the hungry man outside his door. And on the third morning he gave in. He opened the great door, and his servants carried Finnian inside, for he was too weak to walk. They fed him and took care of him until he was well again.

Then Finnian and the chief became good friends, and the chief became a Christian. His name was Tuan.

One day Finnian said to him, "Tell me about yourself, dear friend. Tell me about your past."

"Let the past be," said Tuan. "People need to forget, as well as to remember."

"But a person is his past," said Finnian. "Tell me."

So Tuan said, "I am called Tuan, the son of Cairill, and this land belonged to my father." Then, after a while, he said, "I am also Tuan, the son of Starn, whose brother was Partholon."

"But that can't be," said Finnian. "Partholon came to Ireland not long after Noah's flood."

"I came with him," said Tuan quietly.

Finnian's blood ran cold, and his hair stood on end. But he was a brave man, and soon he was calm again. "This is a wonder," he said. "Tell me more. Tell me about the beginning of Ireland."

And this is the story Tuan told.

"Partholon came to Ireland in a ship, with twenty-four men and twenty-four women. Before that, no one had ever lived in Ireland. As we sailed towards it, it looked like one endless forest, full of the singing of birds, with the warm sun shining over it. To us, tired from the sea, it seemed like Paradise. We landed, and followed a river through the dark forest until we came to a sunny clearing, and there Partholon built a home.

"There were fish in the rivers, and animals in the woods: strange, shy, enormous creatures. We lived there for a long time, and we grew from twenty-four couples to five thousand people. Then, between one night and the next morning, a terrible sickness came. And in seven days, all the people of Partholon were gone, except one man.

"I was that man.

"I was so alone that my own shadow frightened me. The wild creatures of the forest knew I was alone. The long grey wolves chased me to my cave in the rocks. So I learned to live like them. I could creep as quietly as any of them, and run as far. I could hide as still as a wild cat among the leaves. And I lived like that for twenty-two years, and grew old.

"Then one day I saw a great fleet of ships coming round the coast. It was Nemed, the son of Agnoman, with thirty-four ships full of people. My heart leaped for joy, and I ran along the cliffs, jumping from rock to rock like a wild goat, to follow them. Then I bent down to drink from a pool, and I saw my own face in the water. I was hairy and bristly like a wild boar, thin as a bare bush, grey as a badger, and wrinkled like an empty sack. And I was ashamed to let them see me.

"Then a storm came up. I have never seen such a storm. The great fleet was tossed about like leaves in the wind. All night the wind roared and the waves crashed, and trees were torn out of the ground and flew through the air like birds. And in the morning, the sea was empty.

"I crept back to my cave. And there I slept, and I dreamed I was changing into a stag. I dreamed I felt a new heart beating inside me, and that my neck was strong and my legs were powerful. And when I woke up, I was what I had dreamed. I was a stag, young and strong again.

"Oh, the joy of it! I stood on a rock, stamping my hoof, with my antlers high, smelling all the smells of the world. Every breeze brought me news. I could run in great leaps, ten yards at a time. When the old wolf saw me coming, he slunk away. When the bear saw my antlers, he changed his mind and trotted off. I was the king of the deer of Ireland.

"Nemed and four couples had been saved from the storm after all, and they grew into a great people. Sometimes I crept close to watch them, hidden among the leaves. But then, one day, I came back, and they were all gone. There was nothing left of them.

"Old age came on me again. My head grew heavy and my legs grew weak. I went back to my cave, and the wolves came and waited outside. 'Tomorrow,' they said, 'we will eat you.' For I understood their language now.

"'Tomorrow,' I said, 'I will come out, and you can do as you like.' And the wolves howled with joy.

"That night I slept, and I dreamed I was changing into a wild boar. And when I woke up, I was what I had dreamed. In the morning the wolves called, 'Come out, skinny stag, and die!' And I poked out a black bristly snout, with curving tusks and a fierce red eye. And the wolves ran away yelping, tumbling over each other in their fright, and I chased them!

"I became the lord of the wild boars of Ireland. Everything I met ran away from me, except one kind of creature. For people had come to Ireland again: Semion, the son of Stariath, and his people. I never chased them. And when they chased me, I ran away. Often I would go and watch them in their fields, and I would think, 'When the people of Partholon met to make plans, my voice was heard, and everyone was glad to listen. But now I am only a beast with tusks.'

"Then old age came on me again, and I went back to my cave in Ulster, and I dreamed my dream, and I became a hawk.

"The air was my kingdom then. My bright eye could see a hundred miles. I soared and swooped and hung motionless in the sky, and I lived in joy. While I was a hawk, new people came to Ireland: Beothach, the son of the prophet Iarbonel, and his people. These are the people of the fairy hills, and some say they are the gods. I watched their battles from high in the sky. For long, long years I was a hawk, and I knew every hill and stream, every field and valley in Ireland. And I was still a hawk when the sons of Mil came, and drove the people of the fairy hills under the ground.

"Then I grew old again, and in my cave close to the sea I dreamed my dream, and I became a salmon. The green sea rose over me, and I woke in deep water, and I was what I had dreamed.

"How I loved the water! The water holds you up and never lets you fall. I became the king of the salmon, and I swam all the oceans of the world. I saw great monsters of the deep go heaving by. I knew the icy floods that make a salmon jump back as if he had been stung, and the warm currents where we rocked and dozed.

"But then, far away in the sea, I remembered Ulster, and I had to go back there. I swam day and night. It was terribly hard. I was so tired that I could have let go and drifted away for ever. But the love of Ireland kept me going, and at last I came into her rivers.

"Oh, the joy of lying in the shallow water, basking in the sun, watching the dragonflies dart and turn! But the people saw me too. They waited for me at the waterfalls, where I leaped up like a silver flash. They held out nets for me and set traps. Otters chased me, and wildcats fished for me, and hawks dived at me.

"Then one day the fisherman of Cairill, the king of Ulster, caught me in his net. Oh, how happy he was when he saw me! 'Be quiet, king of the river,' he said, as I struggled. When I came out into the air, it was like fire. It scorched my scales and burned my throat. 'Be at peace,' said the fisherman, and he carried me to the palace, singing a song in praise of the king of the waters.

"And when the queen saw me, she wanted me. So I was cooked, and she ate me. And after a while, she had a baby boy. And the baby was me. I was born again as her son, the son of Cairill, the king. And I remembered everything: being a man, and a stag, and a boar, and a hawk, and a salmon. And I have remembered it all ever since."

"And now," said Finnian, "you will be born again one more time, when I baptize you."

So far the story of Tuan. Nobody knows whether he died long ago, in the days of Finnian, or whether he is still living in his fort in Ulster, watching everything that happens, and remembering it all, for the glory of God and the honour of Ireland.
