When the Trojan War was over, Odysseus set sail for home with his men in twelve ships. Home was the little island of Ithaca, where his wife, Penelope, and his son, Telemachus, were waiting. But the gods had many adventures in store for him, and it would be ten long years before he got there.

First, a storm blew them to the land of the Lotus-Eaters. The people there were friendly, and they gave Odysseus's men some of the sweet lotus fruit to eat. But anyone who ate the lotus forgot all about home. They only wanted to stay and eat more lotus forever. Odysseus had to drag his men back to the ships and tie them under the benches until they sailed away.

Next they came to the island of the Cyclopes. The Cyclopes were giants with only one round eye, right in the middle of their foreheads. They lived in caves and kept sheep and goats.

Odysseus took a few of his men and a big jar of strong wine as a present, and they went to explore. They found a huge cave full of cheeses, buckets of milk and pens of baby lambs. Nobody was home.

Then the ground shook. The owner of the cave came back, carrying a load of firewood as big as a tree. It was a Cyclops named Polyphemus. He drove his sheep inside and then rolled an enormous rock across the doorway, so big that twenty oxen could not have moved it. Odysseus and his men were trapped.

Polyphemus milked his sheep. Then he turned his one big eye and saw the men hiding in the corner.

"Who are you?" he growled.

"We are Greeks, on our way home from Troy," said Odysseus. "Please be kind to us, as the gods want everyone to be kind to travelers."

But Polyphemus did not care about the gods, or about being kind. He snatched up two of the men and ate them for his supper. Then he lay down and went to sleep, snoring like thunder.

Odysseus thought hard. They could not escape while the giant was awake. And even if he was asleep, they could never move that rock by themselves.

In the cave was a big wooden pole, as long as a ship's mast. Odysseus and his men sharpened one end of it to a point and hid it.

The next evening, Odysseus offered the giant a bowl of his strong wine. Polyphemus drank it and smacked his lips. "More!" he said. "And tell me your name, little man."

"My name," said Odysseus, "is Nobody."

"Well, Nobody," said the giant, "since you gave me such good wine, I will eat you last." Then he fell asleep.

Odysseus and his men heated the point of the pole in the fire until it glowed. Then they lifted it up and poked it right into the giant's one eye.

Polyphemus roared so loudly that the other Cyclopes came running from their caves. "What's wrong, Polyphemus?" they shouted through the rock. "Is someone hurting you?"

"Nobody is hurting me!" howled Polyphemus. "Nobody!"

"Well, if nobody is hurting you," they said, "then stop making such a racket." And they all went home to bed.

In the morning, the blinded giant rolled away the rock to let his sheep out to the meadow. He sat in the doorway and felt the back of every sheep as it went past, so the men could not slip out.

But Odysseus had a plan for that, too. He tied the sheep together in threes. Under the middle sheep of each three, one of his men held on tight to the wool of its belly. The giant felt the sheep's backs, but he never thought to feel underneath. Odysseus went out last of all, holding on underneath the biggest ram.

They ran down to their ship and rowed away. When they were a little way out to sea, Odysseus could not resist shouting, "Cyclops! It wasn't Nobody who beat you. It was Odysseus!"

Polyphemus tore a huge rock off the mountain and hurled it at the sound of his voice. It splashed down just behind the ship, and the wave nearly swept them back to shore. They rowed away as fast as they could.

Next they came to the floating island of Aeolus, the keeper of the winds. Aeolus was kind to them. When they left, he gave Odysseus a leather bag tied up tight with a silver string. Inside it he had trapped all the stormy winds. Then he sent a gentle breeze to blow them home.

For nine days they sailed, and Odysseus stayed awake at the steering oar the whole time. On the tenth day, they could see Ithaca. They were so close that they could see people on the shore. And Odysseus, worn out at last, fell asleep.

His men looked at the leather bag. "I bet it's full of treasure," they whispered. "Why should he keep it all for himself?" And they untied the silver string.

Whoosh! Out rushed all the stormy winds at once. They blew the ships far, far away from Ithaca, all the way back to the island of Aeolus.

"Please help us again," begged Odysseus.

But Aeolus was angry. "The gods must be against you," he said. "Go away." So they had to row on, with no wind to help them, and their long journey home was only just beginning.
