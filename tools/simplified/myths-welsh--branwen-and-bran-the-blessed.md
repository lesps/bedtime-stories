Long ago, the king of Britain, which was then called the Island of the Mighty, was a giant called Bran the Blessed. He was so huge that no house had ever been built that could hold him. He had a sister, Branwen, the most beautiful young woman in the world, and a brother, Manawydan. And he had two half-brothers who were twins, but not at all alike. One, Nisien, was gentle, and always made peace when people quarrelled. The other, Efnisien, always made trouble when people were at peace.

One afternoon, Bran was sitting on the rock of Harlech, looking out over the sea, when he saw thirteen ships coming from Ireland, with beautiful satin flags. On the first ship, someone held up a shield with its point upwards, which was a sign that they came in peace.

"Who is your chief?" called Bran. "And why has he come?"

"Matholwch, the king of Ireland, is here," they answered. "He has come to ask for Branwen as his wife, so that Ireland and the Island of the Mighty may be friends."

So Bran's council agreed, and Branwen was married to Matholwch, with a great feast in tents, because no house could hold Bran.

But Efnisien was away at the time. When he came back and found that his sister had been married without his permission, he flew into a rage. He went to where the Irish king's horses were kept, and did cruel things to them, and spoiled them so that they were no use any more.

Matholwch was deeply insulted, and set off for his ships. But Bran sent messengers after him. "This was done against my will," Bran said. "It was done by my own brother, and I cannot punish him as he deserves. But you shall have a new horse for every one that was spoiled, and a staff of silver as tall as yourself, and a plate of gold as wide as your face." And so Matholwch came back, and they made peace.

But Matholwch still seemed sad. So Bran gave him one more gift: a magic cauldron. "If any of your warriors are killed," he said, "put them in this cauldron, and the next day they will be as good as ever, except that they will not be able to speak."

Then Matholwch was happy, and he sailed home to Ireland with Branwen. Everyone in Ireland welcomed her, and she gave rich presents to all the great men and women who came to see her. And soon she had a baby son, called Gwern.

But in the second year, the people of Ireland began to grumble about how their king had been insulted in Britain. They grumbled so much that Matholwch gave in to them. And they took their revenge on Branwen. They sent her away to work in the kitchen, and every day the butcher came and boxed her ears. And Matholwch stopped all the ships from sailing to Britain, so that Bran would never hear about it.

For three years Branwen worked in the kitchen. But she found a young starling, and she kept it on the edge of her kneading trough and taught it to speak. She told the bird all about her brother, and what he looked like. Then she wrote a letter about her troubles, and tied it under the starling's wing, and sent it across the sea.

The starling flew all the way to Wales, and found Bran, and landed on his shoulder, and ruffled its feathers until he saw the letter. When Bran read it, he was filled with grief and anger. He called together the warriors of the whole island, and they sailed to Ireland. Bran was too big for any ship, so he waded across the sea, carrying supplies on his back.

Matholwch's swineherds came running to him. "Lord!" they said. "We've seen a forest on the sea, where there was never a tree before! And next to it, a mountain that moves! And on the mountain there is a high ridge, with a lake on each side of it!"

Nobody could explain it, so they asked Branwen.

"The forest is the masts of the ships of the Island of the Mighty," said Branwen. "They have come because they heard how I am treated. The mountain is my brother Bran, wading through the sea. The ridge is his nose, and the two lakes are his eyes, looking angrily at Ireland."

The Irish army fled across a river and broke down the bridge behind them. And there was a magic stone at the bottom of that river, so that no boat could cross it.

"How shall we get across?" asked Bran's men.

"He who would be a leader, let him be a bridge," said Bran. And he lay down across the river, and they laid planks over him, and the whole army walked across on his back. And people in Wales still say, "He who would be a leader, let him be a bridge."

Then Matholwch sent messengers to make peace. "Matholwch will give the kingdom of Ireland to Gwern, your sister's son," they said. "And he will build a house big enough to hold you, the first house that ever could."

Branwen advised her brother to agree, so that the country would not be destroyed. So they made peace, and the Irish built a huge house. But they played a trick. On each of the hundred pillars, they hung two leather bags, and hid an armed warrior inside each bag.

Efnisien came into the house first, and looked around with his fierce eyes, and saw the bags. "What's in this bag?" he asked.

"Flour, my friend," said an Irishman.

Efnisien felt the bag, and felt the warrior's head inside it. And he squeezed it so hard that the warrior never moved again. Then he went to the next bag. "What's in this one?" "Flour." And he did the same thing. And so he went on, all round the house, until not one of the hidden warriors was left.

Then the two armies came in, the Irish on one side and the men of the Island of the Mighty on the other, and they sat down together in peace. And little Gwern was made king of Ireland. Bran called the boy to him, and the boy went to him gladly, and then to his uncle Manawydan, and to his uncle Nisien, and everyone who saw him loved him.

"Why doesn't my nephew come to me?" said Efnisien.

"Go to him, then," said Bran. And the little boy went happily to Efnisien.

But Efnisien, in a sudden fit of wickedness, did a terrible thing to the little boy, and in a moment Gwern was gone. Branwen would have thrown herself after her son, but Bran held her back. And the peace was broken, and everyone in the house leaped up and seized their weapons, and a terrible battle began.

The Irish lit a fire under the magic cauldron, and put their dead warriors into it, and the next day they came out fighting again, as strong as ever, only silent. Then Efnisien saw that the men of the Island of the Mighty had no cauldron to bring them back, and at last he was sorry. "Oh, woe is me," he said, "that I have brought my people into such trouble. I must save them." And he lay down among the dead Irishmen, and the Irish, thinking he was one of them, threw him into the cauldron. And Efnisien stretched himself out with all his strength, and he burst the cauldron into four pieces. And his own heart burst too.

Because of that, the men of the Island of the Mighty were not all destroyed. But nobody could call it a victory. Only seven of them came out of it alive, among them Bran's brother Manawydan, and Pryderi, the son of Pwyll and Rhiannon. And Bran himself was wounded in the foot by a poisoned spear.

Bran knew that he was dying. "When I am gone," he said, "take my head with you, and carry it to the White Mount in London, and bury it there, looking towards France. You will be a long time on the way. In Harlech you will feast for seven years, and the birds of Rhiannon will sing to you. And all that time, my head will talk to you and be as good company as I ever was. Then you will stay on the island of Gwales for eighty years, until you open the door that looks towards Cornwall. When you open that door, you cannot stay any longer. Then you must go straight to London and bury my head."

So the seven men set off home, carrying Bran's head, and Branwen went with them. When they landed in Wales, Branwen looked back towards Ireland, and then at the Island of the Mighty. "Oh, woe is me that I was ever born," she said. "Two islands have been destroyed because of me." And she gave a great sigh, and her heart broke. They buried her in a four-sided grave on the bank of the River Alaw.

Then the seven men went on to Harlech, and sat down to eat and drink. And three birds came and sang to them, and their song was sweeter than any song they had ever heard. The birds seemed to be far away over the sea, and yet they could hear them as clearly as if they were close by. And there they feasted for seven years, and Bran's head talked with them and was good company.

Then they went to the island of Gwales, where there was a fine hall looking out over the sea. It had three doors. Two were open, but the third, which looked towards Cornwall, was closed. "That is the door we must not open," said Manawydan. And there they lived for eighty years, so happily that they forgot all their sorrows, and never grew tired or old, and never even noticed how much time had passed. And Bran's head was with them, as good company as Bran had ever been.

But one day one of them said, "Bad luck to me if I don't open that door, and see if what they say about it is true!" And he opened it, and looked out towards Cornwall. And at once they remembered everything: every sorrow they had ever had, every friend they had lost, and above all, the death of their lord, Bran. They could not stay there any longer. So they set off for London, and they buried Bran's head in the White Mount, looking towards France, just as he had told them.

And people said that as long as Bran's head stayed buried there, no enemy from across the sea would ever invade the Island of the Mighty.
