The Anglo-Saxons who came to England told stories about Wayland the Smith, the greatest smith there ever was, and so did the people of the North. The oldest whole story of him that still exists is a Norse poem, where his name is Volund. This is what it tells.

Long ago there were three brothers, sons of a king of the Finns. Their names were Slagfid, Egil and Volund. They went hunting on skis, and they built themselves a house in a valley called Wolfdale, beside a lake.

Early one morning, they found three young women sitting on the shore of the lake, spinning flax. Beside them lay three cloaks made of swan feathers. They were swan maidens, warrior maidens who could fly. The three brothers took them home, and they married them, and Volund's wife was called Alvit.

For seven years they lived happily together. But in the eighth year the swan maidens began to long for the faraway forests, and in the ninth year they put on their swan cloaks and flew away, and they did not come back.

Egil went east on his skis to look for his wife, and Slagfid went west to look for his. But Volund stayed in Wolfdale, waiting for Alvit. He was the most skilful smith in the world. While he waited, he made rings of red gold set with shining jewels, seven hundred of them, and he hung them all on a cord of bark, hoping that one day Alvit would come home.

But a greedy king named Nidud heard that Volund was living all alone in Wolfdale with his gold. One night, by the light of the moon, the king's men came riding to his house while Volund was out hunting. They found the rings, and they took one away.

When Volund came home, he roasted some bear meat by the fire, and then he sat down on a bearskin and counted his rings. One was missing! "Alvit must have come back and taken it," he thought joyfully. He sat waiting for her for so long that he fell asleep.

When he woke up, his joy was gone. There were heavy chains on his hands and his feet. King Nidud's men had come back and tied him up.

The king gave the stolen ring to his own daughter, Bodvild, and he took Volund's fine sword for himself. And the cruel queen said, "Look how he shows his teeth when he sees the sword. Look how his eyes flash like a snake's. Make sure he can never run away." So they hurt Volund's legs so that he could never walk properly again, and they put him on a little island near the shore. There he had to make treasures for the king, and no one but the king was allowed to visit him.

"My sword shines in Nidud's belt," said Volund, "and Bodvild wears my wife's gold ring." He sat at his forge and never slept, and as he hammered, he planned how he would pay the king back.

And Volund did pay the cruel king back, in ways that are too dark for a bedtime story, but which made King Nidud very sorry for what he had done.

Then Volund rose up into the air, laughing, and flew away over the water. (Some old stories say he made himself a pair of wings.) King Nidud could only stand and watch him go. "There is no man tall enough to pull you down from the sky," he said sadly, "and no archer good enough to shoot you down."

And Volund flew away, and he was never anyone's prisoner again.

In England, on a hill called White Horse Hill, there is a very old stone tomb that people have called Wayland's Smithy for more than a thousand years. The old story says that if your horse lost a shoe, you could leave it there overnight with a silver coin, and in the morning the coin would be gone and the horse would have a new shoe. Nobody ever saw who did it.
