Long ago, in a little city by a mountain, there lived a boy named Theseus. He lived with his mother, Aethra, and his wise old grandfather, the king. Theseus had never met his father.

"Your father is Aegeus," his mother told him. "He is the king of Athens, a great city far away."

"Then why can't I go and see him?" Theseus asked.

His mother took him into the woods, to a big rock covered in moss. "When you can lift this rock," she said, "you may go."

Theseus pulled and pushed until he was out of breath. The rock did not move at all.

Every year he tried again. Every year he grew a little taller and a little stronger. Then one day he took hold of the rock, and pulled with all his heart, and it rolled over onto its side.

Under the rock was a hollow, like a stone box. Inside lay a sword with a golden handle and a pair of sandals.

"These were your father's," said Aethra, with tears in her eyes. "He left them here for you. Wear his sandals, and follow in his footsteps. Carry his sword, and be brave."

Theseus set out for Athens on foot. The road was full of robbers, but Theseus was not afraid. One robber, named Procrustes, tricked travelers into lying on his bed and then hurt them. Another threw people off a cliff. Theseus stopped them both, so they could never hurt anyone again. He even caught a giant wild pig that had been scaring the farmers, and he gave it to the hungry people for bacon.

By the time he reached Athens, everyone was talking about the brave young hero.

But danger was waiting in the palace. King Aegeus had married a wicked enchantress named Medea. She wanted her own son to be king one day. When she heard that Theseus had come, she told the old king a lie.

"That young man has come to take your crown," she said. "Give him a cup of my special wine."

The wine was poison. A fly landed on the cup to take a sip, and it fell in, dead.

Theseus came before the throne. He was so happy to see his father that he could hardly speak. The king held out the cup.

"Drink," said King Aegeus.

Theseus reached out his hand. But just then the king saw the golden sword at his side.

"That sword!" cried the king. "Where did you get it?"

"It was my father's sword," said Theseus. "My mother is Aethra. I lifted the stone and came to find my father."

"My son!" cried King Aegeus. He threw the poison cup away and hugged Theseus tight.

Medea ran to her window. She jumped into a flying chariot pulled by four winged snakes, and off she went into the sky. As she flew away, she dropped the jewels she had stolen, and they rained down on the people of Athens like colored hail.

For a while, Theseus and his father were very happy. Then one morning, Theseus woke to the sound of crying all over the city.

"What is wrong?" he asked.

"Today is the saddest day of the year," said his father. "Far across the sea, on the island of Crete, lives a monster called the Minotaur. He has the head of a bull and the body of a man. Every year, we must send seven young men and seven young women to Crete, to be eaten by the Minotaur."

Theseus stood up tall. "This year, send only six young men," he said. "I will go as the seventh. And I will fight the Minotaur."

The old king cried and begged him to stay. But Theseus would not change his mind.

The ship had black sails, because it was a sad trip. "If you win," said his father, "take down the black sails and put up bright ones. I will watch for you from the cliff every day."

"I promise," said Theseus.

When the ship came near Crete, Theseus saw a flash of light. It was a giant made of shining brass, walking around the island with a huge club. His name was Talus. He guarded Crete for King Minos.

"Where are you from?" boomed the brass giant.

"From Athens," called the captain. "We bring the young people for the Minotaur."

"Pass!" said the giant, and the ship sailed in.

King Minos was cold and cruel. He poked each young person with his scepter to see if they were plump enough for the monster. But when he looked at Theseus, he saw a face that was calm and brave.

"Aren't you afraid of the Minotaur?" asked the king.

"You are the real monster," said Theseus, "for sending children to be eaten, year after year."

King Minos only laughed. "Tomorrow you will be the Minotaur's breakfast," he said.

Next to the throne stood the king's daughter, Ariadne. She was kind and gentle, and she cried for the young people from Athens.

That night, the prison door opened softly. There stood Ariadne with a torch.

"Come with me, Theseus," she whispered. She gave him back his golden sword and led him through a dark garden to a tall stone wall. She pressed one stone with her finger, and a secret door opened.

"This is the Labyrinth," she said. "It is a maze so twisty that no one can find the way out. The Minotaur lives in the middle. Take the end of this silk string. I will hold the other end. If you win, the string will lead you back to me."

Theseus walked into the maze. He held the string in one hand and the sword in the other. The paths twisted and turned. He climbed up steps and crawled under arches until his head was spinning. Somewhere ahead he heard a roar, part bull and part man.

Every so often he felt a little tug on the string. It was Ariadne, telling him she was still there. That made him braver.

At last, in the very middle of the maze, he found the Minotaur. The monster stomped around all alone, roaring about how hungry and how angry he was. He had no friends at all. Theseus felt afraid of him, and a little sorry for him too.

The Minotaur saw Theseus. He lowered his sharp horns and charged.

Theseus jumped aside. The Minotaur crashed into the wall and broke off one of his horns. He bellowed so loudly that people all over Crete thought it was thunder.

They fought for a long time, sword against horn. Once the Minotaur knocked Theseus down. But Theseus sprang back up and struck with all his strength. The Minotaur fell, and he never got up again.

The moon came out bright. Theseus felt another little tug on the string, and he followed it all the way back to Ariadne.

"You did it!" she cried.

They woke the others and hurried to the ship. Theseus held Ariadne's hand. "Come with us," he said. "Everyone in Athens will love you."

But Ariadne shook her head. "My father is old, and I am all he has," she said. "He will be angry at first. But one day he will be glad that no more children have to come to Crete. Goodbye, Theseus."

So Theseus sailed away without her. The brass giant Talus tried to smash the ship with his club. But he swung too far, lost his balance, and fell into the sea with an enormous splash. Some say he is lying there still.

On the way home, the young people sang and danced on the deck. They were so happy that they forgot all about the sails.

Every day, old King Aegeus had climbed the cliff to watch the sea. At last he saw the ship. But its sails were still black. He thought his dear son had been eaten by the Minotaur, and his heart broke. He fell from the cliff into the sea. To this day, that sea is called the Aegean Sea, after him.

So when Theseus came home, he was king. It was a sad way to begin. But he sent for his mother, Aethra, and he listened to her good advice. He became a wise and gentle king, and the people of Athens loved him.
