Every morning, before the sun comes up, Eos, the goddess of the dawn, opens the gates of the east. She paints the sky pink and gold, so that everyone knows a new day is coming.

Eos fell in love with a young prince of Troy named Tithonus, and she took him to live in her palace at the edge of the world. But Tithonus was a person, and people do not live forever.

"Zeus," said Eos, "please make Tithonus live forever, like us."

Zeus agreed. But Eos had forgotten something very important. She had asked for Tithonus to live forever. She had not asked for him to stay young forever.

So Tithonus did not die. But year after year, he grew older and older. His hair turned white. His back bent over. He grew so weak and frail that he could not walk. His voice grew thin and small.

Eos still took care of him. She gave him the food of the gods and dressed him in beautiful robes. But at last he was so tiny and frail, and his voice so thin and chirpy, that she turned him into a grasshopper. That way, he could hop about in the sunshine and sing.

Eos and Tithonus had a son named Memnon. He grew up to be a brave king in a land far to the east. One day he went to help his father's family in a great war at Troy. He fought bravely, but he did not come home.

Eos was so sad that she cried and cried. And she still cries for her son today. When you go outside early in the morning and see tiny drops of water sparkling on the grass, that is the morning dew. The old Greeks said that the dew was the tears of the Dawn, crying for Memnon.

Far away in Egypt, there are two giant stone statues sitting in the desert. One of them was called the statue of Memnon. Long ago, travelers said that when the first rays of the rising sun touched it, the statue made a strange sound, like the twang of a harp string. They said it was Memnon, greeting his mother, the Dawn.
