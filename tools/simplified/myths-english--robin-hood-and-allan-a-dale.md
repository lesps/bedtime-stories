One bright morning, Robin Hood sat under the greenwood tree with Little John and Will Scarlet. "It's been a long time since we had a guest for dinner," said Robin. "And our money is running low, because nobody has come to pay for a feast. Will Stutely, take six men and go to the highway, and bring us back a rich guest for supper. Take Will Scarlet with you, so he can learn the ways of the forest."

So Will Stutely and Will Scarlet and five others went off to the great road and hid behind a hawthorn bush. All day they waited. They saw chattering girls go by, and a tinker, and a shepherd boy, and a farmer. But no fat abbot or rich lord came along, the kind of guest who could pay for a feast.

As the sun was setting, they gave up and started home. But on the way, Will Stutely suddenly stopped. "Listen!" They heard a faint, sad sound, like someone crying.

"Someone is in trouble," said Will Scarlet. "Let's go and see."

They came to a little clearing, where a stream made a clear pool. Beside the pool, under a willow tree, lay a young man, face down on the grass, crying as if his heart would break. His golden hair was tangled and his clothes were untidy. Hanging from the willow was a beautiful harp, decorated with gold and silver.

"Hello there!" shouted Will Stutely. "Who are you, killing all the green grass with your salty tears?"

The young man jumped up and grabbed his bow. "I know him," said one of the outlaws. "He's a minstrel, a singer. A week ago I saw him skipping over the hill with a flower behind his ear. But he doesn't look so happy now."

"Wipe your eyes, man!" said Will Stutely gruffly. "We won't hurt you."

But Will Scarlet put his hand kindly on the young man's shoulder. "You're in trouble," he said. "Don't mind these rough fellows. Come with us. We know someone who may be able to help you."

So the young man took down his harp and came with them through the darkening forest, until they reached the great fire in the clearing where Robin Hood's men were roasting venison and pheasants and fish.

"Good evening, friend," said Robin. "Have you come to have supper with me?"

The young man looked around as if he were dreaming. "Aren't you the great Robin Hood?" he asked.

"That's right," said Robin. "And you know, then, that whoever feasts with me must pay for it. I hope you have a full purse!"

"I have no money at all," said the young man, "except half of a sixpence. My own true love wears the other half on a silk thread around her neck."

Everyone laughed, and the poor young man looked as if he would die of shame. "Is this the rich guest you brought us?" Robin asked Will Stutely.

"Not me," grinned Will Stutely. "Will Scarlet brought him."

So Will Scarlet told how they had found him crying. Then Robin looked closely at the young man's face. "A young face, a kind face, a good face," he said quietly. "But I see that sadness comes to the young as well as the old. Cheer up, lad! I'm sure your trouble can be mended. What is your name?"

"My name is Allan a Dale."

"Allan a Dale! Why, you must be the minstrel whose singing everyone is talking about. Come and sit by me, and tell us your troubles."

So Allan told them everything. He was a wandering singer. One evening he had sung at a farmhouse, where there lived a farmer and his daughter, Ellen, as pure and lovely as the first snowdrop of spring. Allan and Ellen fell in love, and they broke a sixpence in two and promised to be true to each other forever. But when Ellen's father found out, he took her away, and Allan had not seen her since. And that very morning, Allan had heard that in two days Ellen was going to marry old Sir Stephen of Trent, because her father thought it would be grand for her to marry a knight, even though she did not want to.

Even big Little John felt a lump in his throat. "I've half a mind to go and give that old Sir Stephen a good thrashing!" he growled. "Does he think a young girl can be bought like a chicken at the market?"

Robin had been thinking hard. "I have a plan, Allan," he said. "Tell me, if you and Ellen were together in church, with a priest, would she be brave enough to marry you, even if her father said no?"

"Yes, she would!" cried Allan.

"Then I promise you that her father will give you both his blessing, on the very morning of old Sir Stephen's wedding. But we will need a priest. And priests don't like me very much."

Will Scarlet laughed. "I know a friar who would marry you, if you can get on the right side of him. He is called the Curtal Friar of Fountain Abbey, and he lives in Fountain Dale. It's a long walk, but a strong pair of legs could get there and back in a day."

"Then give me your hand, Allan," said Robin. "I swear that two days from now, Ellen will be your wife. Tomorrow I'll go and find this friar."

Then they all sat down to a merry feast. And afterwards Robin said, "Allan, we've heard so much about your singing. Won't you sing for us?"

So Allan took his harp and sang a song about a girl called May Ellen, who loved a strange bird that sang in a lime tree. At night the bird turned into a fairy prince, the son of the queen of Fairyland. When her family made her marry a lord, nine white swans flew in through the church windows and circled around her three times, and then May Ellen herself turned into a white swan and flew away with them.

When he finished, nobody made a sound, because the song was so beautiful.

"Allan," said Robin at last, taking a deep breath, "you must never leave us. Won't you stay with us here in the forest?"

And Allan took Robin's hand and kissed it. "I will stay with you always, dear master," he said, "for no one has ever been as kind to me as you." And so the famous Allan a Dale became one of Robin Hood's merry men.
