---
title: Baby Hermes and the Stolen Cattle
adaptedFrom: hesiod-hymns--hymn-to-hermes

High on a mountain called Cyllene there was a deep, shady cave, and in it lived a shy goddess named Maia. One morning, at dawn, she had a baby boy. His father was Zeus, the king of the gods, and his name was Hermes.

Most babies sleep all day. Not Hermes. He was born at dawn. By midday he had invented music. And by evening he had stolen fifty cows. This is how it happened.

As soon as his mother laid him in his cradle, baby Hermes climbed out and crawled to the door of the cave. There in the grass he found a tortoise shell, all spotted and shiny.

"What luck!" said Hermes. "You will make a wonderful thing."

He carried the shell inside. He fixed two curved horns to it and a crossbar between them, stretched a piece of leather over the shell, and tied on seven strings. Then he plucked the strings, and they made the sweetest sound anyone had ever heard. Hermes had made the very first lyre! He sat and played it, and he made up a song about his father and mother.

But all the time he was singing, he was thinking about something else. He was hungry, and he wanted meat.

When the sun went down, Hermes put his lyre back in his cradle and slipped out of the cave. He ran all the way to the meadows of Pieria, where the cows of the gods grazed. These were the cows of his big brother Apollo.

Hermes picked out fifty cows and drove them away. But he was too clever to leave a trail. He made the cows walk backward, so that their hoof prints pointed the wrong way. And for himself, he wove a pair of giant sandals out of twigs and leaves, so that his footprints looked like nothing anyone had ever seen.

On the way, an old man digging in his vineyard saw him go by.

"Old man," said Hermes, "you will have lots of grapes this year, if you remember not to have seen what you have seen."

All night long Hermes drove the cows over mountains and through valleys, until he came to the river Alpheus. There he shut them in a cave. Then he rubbed two sticks together very fast, until the hot smoke rose. Hermes was the first one ever to make fire with fire sticks. He built a great fire, and cooked a feast from two of the cows, and laid out twelve portions, one for each of the gods.

When he was done, he threw his strange sandals into the river and covered up the ashes of the fire with sand. And before the sun came up, he was home again. He slipped right through the keyhole of the cave like a puff of mist, crept into his cradle, wrapped his blanket around himself, and lay there holding his lyre, like a sweet little baby.

But his mother was not fooled. "You little rascal!" said Maia. "Where have you been all night? Apollo will be here soon to tie you up, you'll see!"

"Don't worry, Mother," said Hermes. "I'm going to make us rich. Why should we live in a dark cave, when the other gods live in palaces?"

Meanwhile, Apollo discovered that his cows were gone. He went looking for them, and he met the old man in the vineyard.

"Old man," said Apollo, "have you seen anyone driving cows past here?"

"Well," said the old man, scratching his head, "I can't be sure. But I think I saw a child, a little baby, walking along with some cows. He was driving them backward, with their heads toward him."

Then Apollo saw a bird fly overhead, and he knew what it meant. The thief was a son of Zeus! He followed the tracks, and they were very strange. The cows' tracks pointed back toward the meadow. And the other tracks were not the footprints of a man, or a wolf, or a bear, or a lion. What could they be?

The tracks led Apollo to the cave on Mount Cyllene. When baby Hermes saw him coming, he snuggled down in his blanket and squeezed his eyes shut, as if he were fast asleep.

Apollo looked in every corner of the cave. Then he stood over the cradle.

"Baby in the cradle," he said, "tell me where my cows are, or I'll throw you into the deepest, darkest pit under the earth!"

Hermes opened his eyes very wide. "Cows? What cows?" he said. "I haven't seen any cows. I've never even heard of cows. Do I look like a cattle thief? I was only born yesterday! All I care about is sleeping and drinking milk and having warm baths."

Apollo laughed. "You little trickster," he said. "You'll be called the Prince of Thieves." He picked Hermes up to carry him away. But Hermes sneezed loudly, right in his face, and Apollo dropped him.

"Very well," said Apollo. "Let's go and ask our father to decide."

So the two of them went up to Mount Olympus, where all the gods were gathered, and stood before Zeus.

"Father," said Apollo, "this baby stole my cows."

"Father," said Hermes, holding his blanket and looking very innocent, "I always tell the truth. I don't even know what cows are. He came into our house and bullied me, and I'm only a baby!"

Zeus laughed and laughed, to hear his clever little son telling such fibs. Then he told them both to stop quarreling, and told Hermes to show Apollo where the cows were hidden. And Hermes had to obey.

So they went to the river Alpheus, and Hermes led the cows out of the cave. Apollo was amazed. "How could a newborn baby do all this?" he said. "You are going to be very strong one day." He tried to tie Hermes up with willow branches. But the branches fell off him, and they took root and grew all around the cows.

Then Hermes had an idea. He took out his lyre and began to play.

The music was so lovely that Apollo forgot all about being angry. He laughed with joy, and a soft, happy feeling came over him. Hermes played and sang a song about how the world began and how the gods were born.

"That music is worth fifty cows!" said Apollo. "Where did you get that wonderful thing? Who taught you to play it?"

"I made it," said Hermes. "And I'll give it to you, brother, if you like it so much."

So Hermes gave Apollo the lyre, and Apollo gave Hermes his shining whip and made him the keeper of the cows. And from then on, Apollo and Hermes were the best of friends. Apollo became the god of music, and he played the lyre better than anyone. And Hermes went off and invented another instrument, the shepherd's pipes.

Apollo still worried a little. "Promise you will never steal my lyre or my bow," he said. And Hermes promised.

Then Apollo gave Hermes a golden staff, and Zeus made Hermes the lord of animals, of flocks and herds, and the messenger of the gods. And if you ever see a picture of Hermes, you will know him by his golden staff, and the little wings on his sandals, which carry him wherever he needs to go, as fast as a thought.
