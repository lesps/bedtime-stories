Long ago, in the far north, there lived a farmer who had seven sons and one daughter. The youngest son had a strange name. People called him Assipattle, which means "the one who grovels in the ashes".

Perhaps he deserved it. Assipattle never worked on the farm like his brothers. He ran about in ragged clothes, with his hair uncombed, and his head was full of stories about trolls and giants, elves and goblins. On hot summer afternoons he would lie on the ash heap, letting the ashes run through his fingers like sand on the beach, and tell stories to himself.

His brothers laughed at him, and pushed him about, and said how well his name suited him. Even his mother made him do all the little jobs that nobody else wanted to do. So Assipattle would have been very unhappy, if it hadn't been for his sister. She loved him dearly. She listened to all his stories, and she never laughed at him.

But one day the king's daughter, Princess Gemdelovely, saw Assipattle's sister standing by the garden gate as she rode past. She liked her so much that she asked her to come and live at the castle and be her maid. The whole family was delighted, except Assipattle. He watched sadly as his sister rode away.

Not long afterwards, terrible news came. Some fishermen had seen the Mester Stoorworm. The Stoorworm was the first and biggest and most terrible of all sea serpents. It was so long that if its tail touched Iceland, its nose would reach the North Cape of Norway. Its breath was so poisonous that it burned up everything it touched. And now it lay in the sea with its head turned towards the land, yawning hungrily.

The king called his wise men together, and for three days they talked, but none of them could think how to drive the monster away. Then the queen came in. She was the king's second wife, and nobody liked her, because she was proud and unkind to the princess, and she spent more time with a great sorcerer than with the king.

"You are brave men," she said, "but your swords are like straw against this enemy. Only magic can beat it. Ask the sorcerer."

The king and his wise men hated the sorcerer. He was long and thin and frightening, with a beard down to his knees, and a face the colour of mortar, as if he had never seen the sun. But they did not know what else to do, so they asked him.

And his answer was dreadful. The Stoorworm must be given seven young girls every Saturday. And if that did not make it go away, there was one other thing they could try, but it was too terrible to tell them yet.

So every Saturday, seven girls were taken down to a rock by the sea, and the Stoorworm reached out its long, forked tongue and swept them away. And all the people watched from a hill, with the women weeping into their aprons.

"Isn't there one grown man who will fight that monster and save the girls?" cried a boy's voice in the crowd. "I would do it. I'm not afraid of the Mester Stoorworm!"

It was Assipattle, with his blue eyes blazing. His eldest brother gave him a hard knock on the head. "You, fight the Stoorworm?" he said. "Go home to your ash heap!"

But all the way home Assipattle kept saying that he would kill the Stoorworm. His brothers were so annoyed that they threw stones at him until he ran away. That evening, when he came to call them in for supper, they jumped on him and piled straw on top of him, and if their father hadn't come to see what was going on, he could have been smothered.

At supper his mother scolded them for picking on their little brother. But Assipattle looked up from his porridge. "Don't worry, Mother," he said. "I could have fought them all, and beaten them too, if I had wanted to."

"Then why didn't you?" they all cried.

"Because I am saving my strength to fight the Stoorworm," said Assipattle. And they all laughed louder than ever.

Every Saturday more girls were taken, until it was plain that soon there would be none left. So the wise men asked the sorcerer about his other plan. Now, the wicked queen hated the princess, and the sorcerer knew it. So he pretended to be very sorry, and said that the only thing left to do was to give the Princess Gemdelovely herself to the Stoorworm.

Everyone fell silent. The princess was the apple of the king's eye. But he was a fair king. Other fathers had given up their daughters, and he knew it would not be right to save his own child. So, after he had talked with the princess, he stood up, with his voice trembling, and said that they were both ready.

Then the king's champion, his old fighting man, stood up too. "And if the Stoorworm still doesn't go away," he said, "then the next thing we give it should not be a young girl, but that tough old sorcerer!" Everyone cheered, and the sorcerer's pale face grew even paler.

The princess had three weeks. The king sent messengers to every country nearby, to say that whoever could drive away the Stoorworm and save the princess could marry her, and have the kingdom too, and the king's famous sword, Sickersnapper. It had once belonged to the god Odin, and nothing could stand against it.

Thirty-six champions came. But when they saw the Stoorworm lying in the sea with its enormous mouth open, twelve of them suddenly felt ill, and twelve of them ran away home, and the twelve who were left were too frightened to try.

On the night before the princess was to be given to the Stoorworm, the old king went to the great chest where he kept his treasures, and took out the sword Sickersnapper.

"Why are you taking out the sword?" asked his old champion gently. "You are ninety-six years old. Your fighting days are over."

"Do you think I can watch my only child being eaten by a monster, and not lift a finger to save her, when no other man will?" said the king. "Get my boat ready, with the sail up. I will go and fight the Stoorworm myself."

That same night, at the farm, everybody went to bed early, because the next morning the whole family was going to the hill by the sea to watch. Everybody except Assipattle, who had to stay at home and mind the geese. He was so cross that he couldn't sleep. And as he lay in the ashes, he heard his father and mother talking in their bed.

"It's such a long way to the hill," said his mother. "I don't think I can walk it."

"You can ride behind me on my mare, Go-Swift," said his father.

"I don't think you love me any more," said his mother, "because you won't tell me your secrets. For five years I've been asking you why Go-Swift runs faster than the wind when you ride her, but goes along like a tired old nag for anyone else."

The farmer laughed. "All right, I'll tell you," he said. "When I want her to stand still, I clap her once on the left shoulder. When I want her to go like an ordinary horse, I clap her twice on the right shoulder. And when I want her to fly like the wind, I whistle through the windpipe of a goose. I always keep one in the left-hand pocket of my coat."

"So that's what happens to all my goose windpipes!" said his wife. "Now I can go to sleep."

But Assipattle was sitting up in the dark, with his eyes shining. His chance had come.

When his parents were asleep, he crept over to his father's coat, took the goose's windpipe out of the pocket, and slipped out to the stable. Go-Swift reared and kicked when she saw him, but he clapped her once on the left shoulder, and she stood as still as a stone. He jumped on her back and clapped her twice on the right shoulder, and off she trotted, with a loud neigh.

The neigh woke everybody up. The farmer and his six sons came tumbling down the stairs, shouting that someone was stealing Go-Swift. "Stop, thief!" shouted the farmer. "Go-Swift, whoa!"

And Go-Swift stopped at once. The farmer and his sons came running. But Assipattle pulled out the goose's windpipe and whistled through it, and Go-Swift leapt forward like the wind, and was over the hill and away before they had taken ten more steps.

The sun was rising when Assipattle came to the sea. And there was the Stoorworm, lying in the water. It was so big that people said it reached a quarter of the way round the world. And Assipattle was only a skinny boy, with no weapon at all.

But he was not afraid. "I must be careful," he said to himself, "and do with my wits what I can't do with my strength."

He tied Go-Swift to a tree and walked on until he came to a little cottage, where an old woman lay fast asleep. He borrowed an iron pot from her shelf, and he put a glowing peat from her fire into it. "She wouldn't mind," he thought, "if she knew it was to save the princess."

Down on the shore lay the king's boat, with its sail up, and one boatman guarding it.

"It's a cold morning," said Assipattle. "Why don't you run about on the sand to warm yourself up, and I'll mind the boat?"

"Certainly not," said the boatman. "The king will be here any minute. What would he say if he found his boat left with a scruffy boy like you?"

"As you like," said Assipattle. He began gathering mussels for his breakfast, and digging a hole in the sand to roast them in. The boatman watched him, feeling hungry.

Suddenly Assipattle gave a shout and jumped into the air. "Gold!" he cried. "Gold, here in the sand!"

That was too much for the boatman. He forgot all about the king, and jumped out of the boat, and pushed Assipattle aside, and began digging as hard as he could. And while he was digging, Assipattle grabbed his pot, jumped into the boat, pushed off, and was half a mile out to sea before the boatman noticed. Of course, there was no gold.

The boatman was very angry, and the old king was angrier still when he came down to the shore with his sword and found his boat gone. All he could do was stand with the crowd and watch.

Assipattle sailed slowly towards the Stoorworm, watching it carefully. He noticed that whenever it yawned, a great flood of seawater rushed down its throat, and came out again through its gills. So he took down his sail, and pointed his boat straight at the monster's mouth. And the next time it yawned, he and his boat were sucked right in and swept down its throat, into the darkness inside.

On and on the boat floated, until the water drained away and it stuck fast. Assipattle jumped out with his pot and went exploring. At last he came to the Stoorworm's liver. He had heard that a fish's liver is full of oil. So he made a hole in it, and put in the glowing peat.

What a fire that was! Assipattle only just got back to his boat in time. The Stoorworm twisted and heaved so hard that it threw the boat right out of its mouth, and it landed high and dry on the shore.

The sea boiled and churned so terribly that the king and the princess, who was dressed all in white, and all the people, had to run to the top of the hill to be safe. And from there they watched.

The poor Stoorworm tossed and twisted in the sea. Its tongue fell out and hit the earth so hard that it made a great dent, and the sea rushed in. And that dent became the sea between Denmark and Norway and Sweden. Then some of its teeth fell out, and they became the Orkney Islands. Then some more teeth fell out, and they became the Shetland Islands. At last the Stoorworm curled itself up into a great lump and died. And that lump became Iceland. The fire that Assipattle lit is still burning underneath it, and that is why there are volcanoes in that cold land.

When the king saw that the Stoorworm was really dead, he threw his arms around Assipattle and called him his son. He put his own royal cloak on him, and buckled the sword Sickersnapper round his waist. And he put the princess's hand in his, and said that when the time came she would be his wife, and he would rule the kingdom.

They all rode back to the castle, with Assipattle riding Go-Swift beside the princess. But at the gate, Assipattle's sister ran out and whispered something to the princess, and the princess rode straight back to tell her father. The wicked queen, glad to be rid of the princess at last, had spent the whole morning with the sorcerer while the king was away. And now the two of them had run off together, on the fastest horses in the stables.

"I'll catch them," cried Assipattle, and off he went on Go-Swift like the wind.

He soon caught up with them. When they saw that it was only the boy from the ash heap, they laughed. The sorcerer turned and rode at him, because he knew that no ordinary weapon could hurt him. But Assipattle's sword was no ordinary weapon. It was Odin's sword, Sickersnapper, and the sorcerer's magic was no use against it. With one stroke, Assipattle knocked him off his horse, and that was the end of the wicked sorcerer.

The king's men came up and led the queen back to the castle. And she was shut up in a high tower for the rest of her life.

As for Assipattle, when the time came he married the Princess Gemdelovely, with great feasting and rejoicing. And when the old king died, they ruled the kingdom together for many long years.
