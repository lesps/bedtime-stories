Long ago, in a town called Santen on the river Rhine, there lived a young prince named Siegfried. His father was King Siegmund, and his mother was the kind Queen Sigelind. Siegfried was strong and quick and brave. Nobody could throw a spear as far as he could, or run as fast, or ride as well.

But the wise old king said, "All work is good work. Even a prince should know how to earn his living with his own two hands."

So he sent Siegfried away to learn to be a smith. Siegfried went to live with a smith named Mimer, in a smoky workshop among the hills, near a great forest. Mimer was the cleverest smith in the whole world. People said he was related to the dwarves, who knew every secret of the earth. He was so old that nobody could remember when he had come.

Siegfried took off his fine clothes. He put on a rough blue shirt, a leather apron and clumsy wooden shoes, and he slept on a pile of straw in the corner of the smithy. But he did not complain. He loved the roar of the fire and the ring of his hammer. Soon he could make the heaviest chains and the finest gold bracelets better than anyone.

One morning Mimer came into the smithy looking very worried. "A smith named Amilias, in the land of Burgundy, has made a suit of armor," he said, "and he says no sword in the world can cut it. He says that unless someone can, all the other smiths must bow down to him and be his servants. I have tried and tried to make a sword good enough, and I cannot. Can any of you?"

The other apprentices shook their heads. But Siegfried said, "I will make such a sword."

The others laughed at him. But Mimer let him try.

For seven days Siegfried worked at his forge, and on the eighth day he brought Mimer a shining sword. They threw a thread of wool, as light as thistledown, onto some water, and Mimer struck it with the sword. The thread was cut in two!

"Well done!" said Mimer. But Siegfried was not happy yet. He broke the sword into pieces and made it again. This time they threw a whole ball of wool into the rushing brook, and when it whirled against the blade, it was cut in half as easily as water.

Still Siegfried was not happy. He went back to his forge, and this time he let nobody come near. But some of the other boys said that one evening they saw a stranger talking to Siegfried at the smithy door. He was a tall man with a long beard, a grey cloak and a sky-blue hood, and he had only one eye, which shone like the evening star. He put some bright broken pieces of a sword into Siegfried's hands. And then he faded away and was gone.

For seven weeks Siegfried worked day and night. Then, pale and tired but smiling, he brought Mimer a wonderful sword. Its handle was red, and magic letters called runes were carved on its blade. "Its name is Balmung," said Siegfried.

He swung it high and brought it down on Mimer's great iron anvil. The anvil split in two, but the blade did not have a single scratch! Then they threw the wool of ten sheep into the brook, and the sword cut through it all.

"Now I am not afraid of Amilias!" cried Mimer.

So Mimer and Amilias met on a hill by the river, between the two kingdoms, and crowds of people came to watch. Amilias was almost a giant, and his armor was so huge that it looked like a castle tower. He sat on a rock and folded his arms and smiled, because little Mimer looked like a black speck beside him.

"Are you ready?" asked Mimer.

"Ready," said Amilias. "Strike!"

Mimer swung the sword Balmung. There was no clang of metal, only a soft hiss, like hot iron dipped into cold water. Amilias sat quite still. But the smile went off his face.

"How do you feel?" asked Mimer.

"Rather strange," said Amilias, "as if cold iron had touched me."

"Shake yourself!" said Mimer.

Amilias shook himself, and his famous armor fell apart in two halves, for the sword had cut clean through it. That was the end of Amilias and his boasting. And people say that the empty armor still sits on that hilltop, and in the moonlight it looks like the ruins of an old castle.

Everybody praised Mimer for his wonderful sword. Nobody knew that the boy Siegfried had made it. And Veliant, the head apprentice, who was a great boaster, told everyone that he had made it himself. That is why some old stories say that Mimer made the sword Balmung, and a few say Veliant. But it was really Siegfried.

In the evenings, Siegfried and Mimer sat by the glowing fire, and Mimer told him stories about long ago. He told him about Siegfried's own family, the Volsungs, the bravest kings of old. Once, he said, a stranger with one eye came into King Volsung's hall and pushed a sword deep into the great tree that grew there. "Whoever can pull it out may keep it," said the stranger, who was Odin himself. Everyone tried and failed, until the king's youngest son, Sigmund, pulled it out as easily as if it had been lying loose. Sigmund became a mighty king. But in his last battle, when he was old, a one-eyed man in a grey cloak stood in his way, and Sigmund's sword broke into pieces.

"The Norns have spoken," said Mimer, and his face grew dark. "Another hero will come from the family of the Volsungs. And my fate is tied to his."

"When will he come?" asked Siegfried. "Who will he be? Aren't you the wise Mimer whose well Odin drank from, the one who knows everything?"

"No," said Mimer sadly. "That Mimer is a giant, far away. I am a different Mimer, related to the dwarves. If only I could drink from that well, I would know what is going to happen. But I must wait for what the Norns have decided."

And he got up and went out. And Siegfried sat alone by the fire, thinking about all he had heard, until morning came.
