namesNote: The Bodhi tree, which Rhys Davids calls the Bo-tree, is the Tree of Wisdom, a great fig tree. Sakka and Brahma are gods, and the Nagas are the snake people who live under the water. Mara is the Tempter. Prince Vessantara, whom Rhys Davids calls Wessantara, was the Buddha in an earlier life, a prince famous for his gifts.
---

For six years, Siddhartha had lived in the forest at Uruvela, fasting and hurting his body, hoping to find wisdom that way. Five holy men stayed with him and served him, sure that he would soon become a Buddha. He ate so little that he grew as thin as a skeleton, and one day he fainted and fell to the ground. But he got up again.

Everyone had heard about his six years of fasting. But now he understood that hurting his body was not the way to wisdom. So he went begging for ordinary food in the villages and towns, and ate it, and his body grew strong and golden again.

Then the five holy men thought, "Six years of fasting did not make him a Buddha. How can he become one now, when he goes begging for food and eats it? He has given up." So they left him, and went far away, to Benares.

Now in a village near Uruvela there lived a young woman called Sujata. Before she was married, she had prayed to a great banyan tree: "If I marry well, and my first child is a son, I will bring you a rich offering every year." And her prayer came true.

So on the full-moon day of May, Sujata got up early to make her offering. When she went to milk her cows, the milk poured out of them all by itself. She cooked rice in the milk, and as it cooked, the bubbles went round and round, and not a drop spilled, and no smoke rose from the fire. The gods came and kept watch over the fire, and put the sweetness of life into the rice.

"Never have I seen such wonders!" Sujata said to her servant girl, Punna. "Go quickly to the holy tree, and keep watch there."

That night Siddhartha had had five dreams, and he knew, "Today I shall become a Buddha." In the morning, he went and sat at the foot of the banyan tree, and the whole tree shone like gold with the light that came from him.

When Punna came to the tree and saw him sitting there, shining, she thought, "The god of the tree has come down to take our offering with his own hands!" She ran back and told Sujata. Sujata was so delighted that she gave Punna jewels, and said, "From now on, you shall be like a daughter to me!"

Then Sujata poured the rice and milk into a golden bowl, and covered it, and carried it on her head to the tree. When she saw Siddhartha, she thought that he was the god of the tree, and she was filled with joy. She bowed, and put the golden bowl into his hands. "My lord," she said, "please accept my gift. May you be as happy as I am!" And she went away, caring no more for her golden bowl than for a dry leaf.

Siddhartha took the bowl down to the river Neranjara, and bathed. Then he sat down facing east, and made the rice into forty-nine little balls, and ate it all. That was the only food he ate for forty-nine days.

When he had finished, he held up the golden bowl and said, "If I am to become a Buddha today, let this bowl float up the river. If not, let it float down." And he threw it into the water. The bowl went up the middle of the river, against the current, as fast as a galloping horse. Then it sank into a whirlpool, down to the palace of the Black Snake King, and clinked against the bowls of three Buddhas who had lived long before. Click, click! The Snake King heard it, and said, "Yesterday a Buddha came into the world, and now, today, there is another!"

Siddhartha spent the hot part of the day in a grove of flowering sal trees. In the evening, like a lion getting up from rest, he set off towards the Tree of Wisdom, along a wide path that the gods had decorated. The snake people, the spirits and the winged creatures of the sky brought him flowers and sang heavenly songs.

On the way he met a grass-cutter called Sotthiya, who gave him eight bundles of grass. Siddhartha took them, and walked around the Bo-tree, looking for the right place to sit. On the south side, and the west side, and the north side, the whole earth seemed to tip and sway beneath him. "This can't be the right place," he said each time. But on the east side the ground was steady. That was where all the Buddhas before him had sat, and it never trembled or shook.

He scattered the grass there, and at once it made itself into a seat more beautiful than any carver could make. He sat down with his back to the trunk of the tree, facing east, and made a strong promise: "My skin and my bones may dry up, and my blood may dry up too, but I will not leave this seat until I have found perfect wisdom!" And he sat there cross-legged, as firm as if a hundred thunderbolts held him there.

Then Mara, the Tempter, thought, "Prince Siddhartha wants to escape from my power. I will not let him go!" He beat his war drum, and called up his army. Mara's army stretched for miles in front of him and to his right and left, and behind him to the very edge of the world, and it was nine leagues high. Its war cry sounded like an earthquake. Mara climbed onto his enormous elephant, Girded-with-Mountains, gave himself a thousand arms, and took up every kind of weapon. And his army came on, in every shape and every colour, to overwhelm Siddhartha.

All the gods had gathered around Siddhartha, singing his praises. Sakka, the king of the gods, was blowing his great trumpet. Brahma held a white royal umbrella over him, and the Snake King sang his praises. But when Mara's army came near, not one of them could stay. Sakka fled to the edge of the world with his trumpet on his back. Brahma left the white umbrella on a rock at the end of the earth, and went back to his heaven. The Snake King dived down into the earth, and lay down with his hands over his face. Siddhartha sat there all alone.

He looked around and saw that all the gods had gone. "This whole great army has come against me alone," he thought. "My father is not here, nor my mother, nor my brother, nor anyone else to help me. But I have the ten great virtues that I have practised for so long. They will be my shield and my sword." And he sat thinking about the ten virtues.

Then Mara said, "I will drive him away," and he sent a whirlwind. Winds rushed in from the four corners of the earth, strong enough to tear down mountains and pull up forests by the roots. But when they reached Siddhartha, they could not even stir the edge of his robe.

Then Mara sent a great rainstorm, and a flood rose higher than the trees. But it could not wet even a dewdrop's worth of Siddhartha's robe.

Then he sent a storm of rocks, and whole mountain peaks came flying through the air, smoking and blazing. But when they reached Siddhartha, they turned into bunches of heavenly flowers.

Then he sent a storm of swords and spears and arrows, flaming through the sky. But they turned into flowers too.

He sent a storm of red-hot coals, and a storm of burning ashes, and a storm of fiery sand, and a storm of blazing mud. But they all fell at Siddhartha's feet as flowers, and sweet sandalwood dust, and perfume.

Last of all, Mara sent a great darkness. But when it reached Siddhartha, it vanished, as darkness vanishes when the sun comes out.

So none of Mara's nine storms could drive Siddhartha away. Then Mara rode up on his elephant and shouted, "Get up from that seat, Siddhartha! It does not belong to you. It is meant for me!"

"Mara," said Siddhartha, "it was not you who practised the ten great virtues. It was not you who gave up everything for others, and searched for wisdom to save the world. This seat does not belong to you. It belongs to me."

Mara was furious, and he hurled his terrible spear at Siddhartha, a spear that could split solid rock. But it turned into a garland of flowers, and hung over Siddhartha like a canopy. Then Mara's army threw great rocks at him, and those turned into flowers too.

The gods were watching from the edge of the world. "Prince Siddhartha is lost!" they cried. "What can he do to save himself?"

Then Siddhartha said to Mara, "Mara, who is your witness that you have given to others?"

Mara pointed to his army. "All these are my witnesses," he said. And his whole army roared like an earthquake, "I am his witness! I am his witness!"

"Siddhartha," said Mara, "who is your witness that you have given to others?"

"You have living witnesses," said Siddhartha, "and I have no living witness here at all. But let this great, solid earth be my witness, to the great gifts I gave in an earlier life, when I was Prince Vessantara."

And he drew his right hand out from under his robe, and stretched it down towards the earth. "Are you my witness, or are you not?" he asked.

And the great Earth spoke, with a voice like the shout of a hundred thousand armies: "I am your witness!"

At that, Mara's elephant fell down on his knees before Siddhartha. And Mara's whole army ran away in every direction, throwing off their clothes and their turbans as they ran, until not even two of them were left together.

When the gods saw that Mara's army had run away, they cried, "The Tempter is beaten! Prince Siddhartha has won! Come, let us honour him!" And the snake people, the winged creatures and the gods all came back to the foot of the Bo-tree, shouting for joy:

> The Blessed Buddha has won,
> and the Tempter is overthrown!

The sun had not yet set when Siddhartha drove away Mara's army. Then, as the Bo-tree dropped its red shoots on his robe like sprigs of coral, he sat on through the night. In the first part of the night he came to know the past. In the middle of the night he came to know the present. And in the last part of the night he came to understand the chain of causes from which all evil comes.

And at the break of day he found perfect wisdom, and became the Buddha.

Then the whole universe was full of glory. Flags and banners stretched from one end of the world to the other, and from the earth up to heaven. Every tree burst into flower and fruit. Garlands of lotus flowers hung from the sky, and lilies sprang up even from the rocks. The dark places that had never seen the sun were filled with light. The water of the sea became sweet, and the rivers stood still. People who had been born blind could see, people who had been born deaf could hear, people who had been born lame could walk, and every chain fell away.

And the Buddha sang the song of victory that all the Buddhas sing:

> Long have I wandered, through life after life,
> looking in vain for the answer:
> where do life and pain come from?
> And it is hard to be born,
> when pain and death lead only to being born again.

> Found! It is found!
> You who made me what I am,
> you shall build no house for me again.
> All your beams are broken,
> and your roof-beam is shattered.
> My mind has passed into Nirvana.
> The end of craving has come at last!
