---
title: The Quails and the Net

namesNote: The Jātaka tells of the Buddha's earlier lives, before he became the Buddha.

Long ago, when Brahmadatta was king of Benares, the Buddha-to-be was born as a quail. He lived in the forest, and he was the leader of many thousands of quails.

In those days a bird-catcher came to that forest. He would call like a quail, until the quails came flocking together to see who was calling. Then he threw his net over them, pulled its edges together, and caught them all in a heap. He put them in his basket, and took them home and sold them.

One day the wise quail said to the others, "This bird-catcher is catching far too many of us. But I know a way to stop him. Next time he throws his net over us, each of you must put your head through one of the holes in the net. Then we will all fly up together, carrying the net with us, and drop it on a thorn bush. Then we can all slip out from underneath."

"Very good," said all the quails.

The next day, when the net came down over them, they did just as he had told them. They lifted the net all together, flew off with it, and dropped it on a thorn bush, and they all got away. The bird-catcher took so long to untangle his net from the thorns that evening came, and he had to go home with nothing. The next day the quails did the same, and the next, and the next. Every day the bird-catcher spent until sunset untangling his net, and every evening he went home with nothing.

His wife grew cross. "Every day you come home with nothing!" she said.

"My dear," said the bird-catcher, "the trouble is that those quails have learned to work together. As soon as my net is over them, off they fly with it, and leave it on a thorn bush. But they won't always get on so well. Don't you worry. As soon as they start quarrelling, I'll catch the lot of them, and then you'll smile again." And he said:

> While they agree, the birds fly off with my net.
> When they quarrel, they'll be caught by me.

Not long after that, as the quails were landing on their feeding ground, one quail stepped on another quail's head by mistake.

"Who stepped on my head?" cried the second quail angrily.

"I did," said the first. "But I didn't mean to. Don't be angry."

But the other quail stayed just as angry. They answered each other back, again and again, and began to say unkind things. "I suppose you lift the whole net all by yourself!" said one.

When the wise quail heard them quarrelling, he thought, "Nobody is safe with a quarreller. Soon they will stop lifting the net together, and then the bird-catcher will catch them. I cannot stay here any longer." So he flew away to another place, with all the quails who followed him.

Sure enough, a few days later the bird-catcher came back. He called like a quail, and when the quails came together, he threw his net over them.

"They say you lifted the net so hard that all the feathers fell off your head," said one quail. "Now's your chance. Go on, lift!"

"They say you lifted the net so hard that all your wing feathers fell out," said the other. "Now's your chance. Go on, lift!"

And while each of them was telling the other to lift the net, the bird-catcher lifted it himself. He put them all in his basket and carried them home. And his wife smiled.

Long afterwards, the Buddha told this story to his own family, when they were quarrelling. "Quarrels in a family are a bad thing," he said. "Quarrelling leads only to ruin. In those days, I myself was the wise quail."
