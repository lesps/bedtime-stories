Long ago, Concobar was king of Ulster, in the north of Ireland. His bravest warriors were called the Knights of the Red Branch.

One night, while the king and his knights were feasting at the house of his storyteller, news came that the storyteller's wife had had a baby girl, a baby of wonderful beauty. The king's druid, Caffa, went outside to read the stars and the clouds. When he came back in, his face was grave.

"This child shall be called Deirdre," he said. "And because of her, there will be great sorrow in Ulster. There will be quarrels and wars, and many heroes will be driven away from their homes."

Some of the knights said the baby should be put to death. But the king said, "No. We will not do an evil thing to stop something that may never happen. I will keep her safe, far away from everyone, and when she is grown up, she will be my wife. Then I will be able to keep away the trouble Caffa sees."

So Deirdre grew up in a lonely house with a high wall all around it, and a lovely garden with a stream running through it. Nobody was allowed to see her except her teacher, her nurse, and a wise woman called Lavarcam, who was the king's poet. And Deirdre grew up to be the most beautiful young woman in all Ireland.

One snowy day, Deirdre and Lavarcam were looking out of the window. Her teacher had killed a calf for dinner, and a raven landed on the snow to drink the drops of blood. "I would like the man I marry to have hair as black as that raven," said Deirdre, "and cheeks as red as blood, and skin as white as snow. Last night I dreamed of a young man just like that. But I don't know where he is, or if he is anywhere in the world."

"He is not far away," said Lavarcam. "He is one of the king's knights. His name is Naisi, the son of Usna."

Naisi and his two brothers, Ainnli and Ardan, were the best loved of all the Red Branch Knights. They were gentle in peacetime, quick in the hunt, and strong in battle.

"Then I will not be happy until I see him," said Deirdre. "Please bring him to me."

"Oh, child," said Lavarcam, "if the king finds out, he will be terribly angry."

Deirdre said nothing more. But she grew sad and quiet, and her eyes filled with tears whenever she thought of her dream. Lavarcam loved her very much and could not bear to see her so unhappy. So at last she found a way for Deirdre and Naisi to meet in secret. And as soon as they met, they loved each other. "I will never marry the king," said Deirdre. "I will marry Naisi."

They knew the king would never forgive them. So Naisi and Deirdre, with his two brothers and all their people and their hounds, ran away across the sea to Scotland.

For a while they lived there in the service of a Scottish king. But when that king saw how beautiful Deirdre was, he wanted her for himself, and he made a wicked plan to get rid of Naisi. The brothers found out just in time, and fled in the night with all their people. They went to live in a wild place by the sea, looking across the water towards Ireland. They built huts in the forest, and lived by hunting and fishing. Deirdre was happy there, because she was with Naisi. They hunted deer in the glens, and fished in the clear streams, and when Deirdre's feet grew tired from walking in the hills, the brothers would carry her home on their shields.

Back in Ulster, King Concobar held a great feast. "Is there anything missing from my house?" he asked his knights.

"Nothing," they said.

"I can think of something," said the king. "The three sons of Usna are missing. Why should they be in exile because of a woman? Let them come home, and I will give them back their lands and their places among the Red Branch."

The knights were glad. But the king was hiding his real thoughts. He had never forgiven Naisi, and he wanted to lure the brothers home so that he could kill them.

He sent his old friend Fergus to bring them back. "Tell them they will be safe," he said, "and promise it yourself." Fergus did not suspect anything, and he was glad to go, for he loved the sons of Usna.

When Fergus arrived in Scotland, he gave a great shout on the shore. Naisi and Deirdre were inside their hut, playing chess.

"That's a man from Ireland," said Naisi.

"No," said Deirdre. "It's someone from Scotland. Let's go on with our game." But she had known Fergus's voice from the first shout. She just did not want to hear what he had come to say.

When Fergus shouted a third time, Naisi jumped up. "That's Fergus!" he said happily. And he sent his brother to bring him in.

"Last night I had a dream," Deirdre told Naisi quietly. "Three birds flew to us from Ulster, with three drops of honey in their beaks. They left us the honey, and they took away three drops of our blood. It means that the king's message will be as sweet as honey, but there are thoughts of blood in his heart."

Fergus told them the king's message. "Don't go," said Deirdre. "Here, nobody rules over you."

"But your own country is better than any other," said Fergus. "And I have promised you will be safe. If every man in Ireland were against you, my promise would protect you."

"Ireland is dearer to me than Scotland," said Naisi. "We will go home with you."

So they sailed back to Ireland. As they landed, Deirdre sat on the cliff and looked back across the sea at the blue hills of Scotland. "I love that land," she said, "with its bright harbours and green hills. Glen Etive, where my first house was built, where the sun loves to shine. Glen Orchy, where Naisi was the happiest of all in the hunt. Glen da Roy, where the cuckoo sings sweetly. I would never have left those places, except to be with Naisi."

Now the king had made a trick. One of his men, Barach, invited Fergus to a great feast that would last for three days. And Fergus had made a solemn vow, long ago, that he would never refuse an invitation to a feast. He went red with anger. "You know I can't say no," he said. "And I promised to take the sons of Usna straight to the king!"

"Then choose," said Deirdre. "Leave your feast, or leave the sons of Usna."

But Fergus could not break his vow. "I will send my own two sons with you to protect you instead," he said. And Naisi went off angrily, with Deirdre and his brothers and Fergus's sons.

On the way, Deirdre begged the brothers to wait somewhere safe until Fergus could come. But they would not. "People will say we are afraid," they said. Then Deirdre saw a cloud as red as blood hanging over the king's palace. "Let's go to the hero Cuchulain and stay with him until Fergus comes," she begged. But again they would not.

"Then I will give you a sign," said Deirdre. "If the king takes you into his own hall to eat with him, he means you no harm. But if he sends you to the House of the Red Branch, he means to betray you."

The king sent them to the House of the Red Branch.

That night, as Naisi and Deirdre sat playing chess, kind Lavarcam came to them in tears. "Something terrible is going to happen tonight," she said. "Lock all the doors, and defend yourselves until Fergus comes!" Then she went back to the king. "Deirdre isn't beautiful any more," she told him. And for a while, the king forgot his jealousy.

But then the king sent another man to spy on them. The man climbed up to a high window and looked in. And he went back and told the king, "Deirdre is the most beautiful woman in the world."

Then the king's jealousy blazed up, and he sent his soldiers to attack the House of the Red Branch. All night long the brothers and their friends fought to defend it. Fergus's son Buinni went out to fight, but the king offered him a rich reward, and he took it and went away. Fergus's other son, Illan the Fair, stayed true and fought bravely, until he fell. Then Ardan, and Ainnli, and Naisi each took a turn, holding back the king's men for a third of the night.

"Lavarcam," called Naisi at dawn, "go up on the wall and look to the east. Can you see Fergus coming?" But Lavarcam came back sadly. "I see nothing," she said, "only the plain, and the cows grazing."

So the brothers made a wall of shields around Deirdre, and they fought their way out. They fought so bravely that the king saw he could never beat them in a fair fight. So he tricked them one last time. He sent the druid Caffa, who loved them, to tell them that if they put down their swords, the king would forgive them. Caffa believed it, and so did they. They laid down their swords and went to the king.

As soon as they were unarmed, the king had them seized. None of the men of Ulster would harm them. But at last the king found a stranger who would, and the three sons of Usna were put to death together, side by side. And all the men of Ulster gave three great cries of sorrow.

Deirdre wept for them. "Three lions of the hill are gone," she said, "and I am left alone. Three strong hawks, three kind princes who always welcomed strangers. Naisi, my dear husband, you cannot hear me. It was joy to be with the sons of Usna, and the day is long and weary without them. Make the grave wide," she said, "a grave for four. For I will sleep for ever beside the one I love."

And she lay down beside Naisi, and her heart broke, and she died. They were buried together, under a great heap of stones, and their names were carved on the stone in Ogham.
