Ceyx was a kind and gentle king. His wife was named Alcyone, and they loved each other dearly. Alcyone's father was Aeolus, the keeper of the winds.

One day Ceyx decided to sail across the sea to visit a faraway temple and ask the gods for advice.

When Alcyone heard this, she turned pale. "Please don't go," she begged. "I grew up in my father's house. I know how wild the winds can be when they get loose. If you must go, take me with you. Then whatever happens, we'll be together."

But Ceyx could not bear to put her in danger. "I promise I will come back," he said, "before the moon is full twice more."

Alcyone stood on the beach and watched the ship sail away. She waved until she could no longer see Ceyx. She watched until she could no longer see the ship. She watched until the very last speck of the sail disappeared. Then she went home and cried.

That night, out at sea, a storm began. The waves grew white and huge. The wind howled so loudly that the sailors could not hear the captain's orders. Rain poured down. Lightning cracked the sky. The ship tossed up and down like a toy.

All through the storm, Ceyx thought only of Alcyone. He was glad she was safe at home. Then a giant wave crashed over the ship, and it sank into the sea. Ceyx's last word was her name.

At home, Alcyone did not know. She counted the days. She got his clothes ready for his return. Every day she prayed to Hera, the queen of the gods, to keep him safe and bring him home.

Hera knew that Ceyx was gone, and she could not bear to hear Alcyone praying for him any longer. So she sent her messenger, Iris, the goddess of the rainbow, to the cave of Sleep.

The cave of Sleep was far away, where the sun never shines. It was dark and still. No rooster crowed there and no dog barked. The only sound was a soft little stream that made everyone drowsy. Poppies grew at the door. In the middle of the cave, on a soft black bed, lay the god of Sleep, surrounded by all the dreams in the world.

Iris's rainbow lit up the dark cave. Sleep opened one eye. "Hera says," said Iris, "send a dream to Alcyone, to tell her what has happened to Ceyx." Then Iris hurried away, before she fell asleep too.

Sleep called one of his sons, Morpheus, who could take the shape of any person. Morpheus flew silently to Alcyone's house. He took the shape of Ceyx, wet from the sea, and stood by her bed.

"Alcyone," he said in her dream, "it's me, Ceyx. I'm so sorry. My ship was lost in the storm. I can't come home."

Alcyone woke up crying and reached for him, but there was no one there.

In the morning she went down to the beach, to the place where she had last seen him. Something was floating in the waves, drifting closer and closer. When it came near, she saw that it was Ceyx.

Alcyone ran out along a stone wall that stretched into the sea. And as she ran, she began to fly. She had wings. She had turned into a bird. She flew over the water, crying sadly, and wrapped her new wings around Ceyx.

The gods were moved by their love. They turned Ceyx into a bird too, so that the two of them could be together again. They are the birds called kingfishers, and the old Greeks called them halcyons.

Every year, in the middle of winter, the kingfishers build a nest that floats on the sea. And for seven days while they sit on their eggs, Alcyone's father, the keeper of the winds, holds the winds back, so the sea is calm and gentle for his grandchildren.

People still call peaceful, happy times "halcyon days."
