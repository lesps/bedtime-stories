title: The Shepherd and the Lady of the Lake
adaptedFrom: jacobs-celtic--the-shepherd-of-myddvai

namesNote: Jacobs spells the lake Lyn y Van Vach and the village Myddvai; in Welsh today they are Llyn y Fan Fach and Myddfai.

High up in the Black Mountains of Wales there is a little dark lake called Llyn y Fan Fach. Long ago, a young shepherd from the village of Myddfai used to bring his lambs to graze beside it.

One day, as he sat by the water, three young women rose up out of the dark lake. They shook the bright drops from their hair and came gliding to the shore, and wandered about among his sheep. They were more beautiful than anyone in the world, and the shepherd fell in love with the one who came nearest to him.

He offered her some of the bread he had brought for his lunch. She took it and tasted it, and then she sang:

"Your bread is baked too hard. It's not so easy to catch me!"

And she ran off, laughing, back into the lake.

The next day, the shepherd brought bread that was hardly baked at all. When the maidens came out of the water, he offered it to her again. She tasted it and sang:

"Your bread is not baked at all. I will not have you!"

And again she vanished into the waves.

The third day, the shepherd found some bread floating in the water near the shore, and he offered her that. This time she was pleased. "I will marry you," she said, "if you can pick me out from my sisters tomorrow."

The next day, the three sisters stood in front of him together. But the shepherd looked carefully, and he knew her by the way the strap of her sandal was tied.

"I will be as good a wife to you as any woman on earth," she said. "But if you ever strike me three times without a reason, I will leave you for ever."

"I would never do that," said the shepherd.

Then she called out of the lake her wedding gifts: three cows, two oxen, and a bull. And she went home with him as his bride.

They lived happily for many years, and they had three sons. Then one day they were going to a christening. "It's too far to walk," she said.

"Then go and fetch the horses," said her husband.

"I will," she said, "if you'll bring me my gloves from the house."

But when he came back with the gloves, she still hadn't gone for the horses. So he tapped her lightly on the shoulder with the gloves and said, "Go on, go on."

"That's one," she said.

Another time, they were at a wedding. Everyone was happy and laughing, but suddenly she began to cry. Her husband tapped her on the shoulder. "Why are you crying?" he asked.

"Because those two are starting out on a life of troubles," she said. "And your troubles are starting too. That is the second time you have struck me without a reason. Be careful. The third time will be the last."

After that, the shepherd was very careful. But one day they were at a funeral, and suddenly she burst out laughing. Her husband forgot himself, and touched her on the shoulder, rather roughly. "Is this a time for laughing?" he said.

"I laugh," she said, "because when people die, their troubles are over. But your troubles have begun. That was the third blow. Our marriage is over. Goodbye." And she got up and went home.

There she looked around at the farm, and she called to her cattle:

"Brindle cow, white speckled cow, spotted cow, freckled cow, old white-face, and grey Geringer, and the white bull from the king's land, grey ox and black calf, all of you, follow me home!"

And all the cattle she had brought with her came. The little black calf had just been killed for meat, and was hanging on a hook. But it jumped down, alive and well, and followed her. And the oxen were out ploughing, but they came too, dragging the plough behind them. And she walked back up the mountain to the lake, with all of them following her, and they plunged into the dark water together, and were never seen again.

People say you can still see the furrow that the plough made, as it was dragged across the mountain to the lake.

She came back only once more, when her sons were grown up. And she gave them the gift of healing. And they became the famous Physicians of Myddfai, and their family were healers for hundreds of years.
