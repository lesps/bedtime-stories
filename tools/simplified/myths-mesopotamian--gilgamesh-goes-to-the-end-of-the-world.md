title: Gilgamesh Goes to the End of the World

namesNote: The fifth of six parts of the Epic of Gilgamesh. Budge spells the names Uta-Napishtim, Siduri-Sabîtu and Ur-Shanabi.

Gilgamesh wandered over the land, weeping for his dear friend Enkidu. And as he walked, he thought, "One day I will die too. Then I will be like Enkidu. I am afraid of death, and that is why I wander over the land."

Now Gilgamesh had an ancestor called Utnapishtim, who had lived long, long ago. And the gods had made Utnapishtim one of themselves, so that he would never die. "I will go and find Utnapishtim," thought Gilgamesh, "and he will tell me the secret of living for ever."

Nobody knew where Utnapishtim lived. But Gilgamesh set out towards the west. "If I meet lions in the mountain passes at night," he said, "I will pray to the gods to keep me safe." On his way, men or animals attacked him, but he beat them, and went on.

At last he came to Mount Mashu, the mountain where the sun rises and sets. The way into the mountain was guarded by Scorpion-Men. They were so terrible to look at that just one look from them could kill a man, and even mountains crumbled when they stared at them. When Gilgamesh saw them, his face went pale with fear. But he was brave, and he bowed low to them.

One Scorpion-Man called to his wife, "The one who is coming to us has the body of a god!"

And his wife said, "Two parts of him are god, and one part is man."

The Scorpion-Man was kind to Gilgamesh. "Where are you going?" he asked.

"I am looking for my ancestor, Utnapishtim," said Gilgamesh, "to learn the secret of living for ever."

"You cannot go that way," said the Scorpion-Man. "No one has ever got through the dark inside the mountain. It takes twelve long hours to cross it, in the dark."

But Gilgamesh was not afraid. He set off along the road through the mountain. Every hour the dark grew thicker. But he struggled on, and at the end of the twelfth hour he came out into bright daylight. He was in a lovely garden, full of trees loaded with sweet fruit. And there he saw the tree of the gods.

Near the garden, beside the sea, lived the goddess Siduri. She wore a fine belt, and she sat on a throne by the sea. When she saw Gilgamesh coming, dirty from his long journey and dressed in a ragged animal skin, she did not like the look of him. So she shut her door.

"What's wrong with you?" called Gilgamesh. "Why have you shut your door? Open it, or I'll smash the bolt and break it down!"

Then Siduri spoke to him. "Why are your cheeks so thin? Why is your face so sad? Why is there sorrow in your heart? You look as if you have come a very long way."

"Why shouldn't my cheeks be thin, and my face sad?" said Gilgamesh. "Death has taken my dear friend Enkidu. He was like a panther of the wild. He crossed the mountains with me, and helped me beat Humbaba in the cedar forest and kill the Bull of Heaven. He fought lions with me, and he was with me through every danger. I wept for him for six days and nights before I would let him be buried. I am afraid of death, so I am travelling far. My friend whom I loved has turned to dust. Must I lie down too, and never get up again?

"Siduri, which is the way to Utnapishtim? Tell me the way! If I can, I will cross the sea. If not, I will go by land."

"There is no way across the sea, Gilgamesh," said Siduri. "Since the beginning of time, nobody has crossed it, except Shamash the sun god. The way is hard, and at the end of it lie the Waters of Death, which are deep. What will you do when you come to the Waters of Death?

"But Urshanabi is here, the boatman of Utnapishtim. Go and see him. If you can, cross the sea with him. If not, come back."

So Gilgamesh went and found Urshanabi the boatman. Urshanabi asked him the same questions that Siduri had asked, and Gilgamesh answered him in the same way. "Which is the road to Utnapishtim?" he asked.

"Take your axe," said Urshanabi, "and go down into the forest, and cut poles, each sixty cubits long."

Gilgamesh cut the poles and brought them back, and he and Urshanabi got into the boat. They sailed on a voyage of a month and a half, and they came to the Waters of Death. "Don't touch the water with your hand," Urshanabi told him.

Meanwhile Utnapishtim saw the boat coming. It looked strange to him, so he went down to the shore to see who was in it. When he saw Gilgamesh, he asked him the same questions as Siduri and Urshanabi, and Gilgamesh gave the same answers. Then Gilgamesh told him why he had come. "I have crossed hard mountains and the wide sea to find you," he said. "I have eaten lions and panthers, jackals and antelopes and mountain goats, and I have dressed in their skins."

Some of Utnapishtim's answer is broken off the old tablets. But he talked about Gilgamesh's father and mother. And he told Gilgamesh that nothing on earth lasts for ever. "The gods have decided when people will live and when they will die," he said, "and nobody can find out the day of their death, or escape it."
