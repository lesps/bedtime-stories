namesNote: From the Ramayana, the epic of Rama, who is an avatar of the god Vishnu. Dutt spells some of the names differently (Dasa-ratha, Janak, Lakshman, Bharat). The Dandaka forest is the great wild forest of southern India.

Long ago, in the city of Ayodhya, there lived a good king called Dasharatha. He had four sons, and the eldest was Rama, who was brave and gentle and always told the truth.

In another kingdom, Mithila, the king, Janaka, had a beautiful daughter called Sita. And King Janaka sent word far and wide: "Whoever can bend my great bow will marry Sita." This bow had once belonged to the god Rudra. Princes and warriors came from far away to try, but not one of them could even bend it, and they all went home ashamed.

Then the wise sage Vishvamitra came to Mithila, and with him came the young prince Rama and his brother Lakshmana. "King Janaka," said Vishvamitra, "please show Rudra's bow to Prince Rama."

So King Janaka sent for the bow. It was so heavy that it lay in an iron cart with eight wheels, and strong men had to pull the cart into the hall. "This is the bow of my fathers," said Janaka. "Gods and demons have tried to bend it, and failed. No man will ever string it."

"Let me try," said Rama.

He lifted the lid of the iron cart, and raised the great bow high. Everyone stared as he bent it, and strung it, and pulled back the string, further and further, until the bow snapped in two! There was a crash like thunder. The earth shook, the hills echoed, and the kings in the hall fell down in fright.

"I have seen a wonder," said King Janaka. "Sita will be Rama's wife." And he sent messengers to Ayodhya, to tell King Dasharatha the news.

So Rama married Sita.

Years passed, and King Dasharatha grew old. He decided that Rama should be crowned, to rule after him. The whole city was glad, and got ready for a great celebration.

But the king had three queens, and the youngest, Queen Kaikeyi, was the mother of Rama's brother Bharata. Kaikeyi's maid told her that if Rama became king, she and her son would be ruined. And Kaikeyi remembered something.

That evening the old king came happily to Kaikeyi's rooms. He walked through her garden, where peacocks wandered and parrots flew from tree to tree, and through her beautiful halls. But Kaikeyi wasn't there. At last he found her, lying on the bare floor of the room kept for mourning, weeping.

"My queen, what is wrong?" said the king. "Has someone hurt you? Tell me what you want, and you shall have it, I promise."

Kaikeyi made him swear it, by the gods and the sun and the moon and the stars. Then she said, "Long ago, you were badly wounded in a battle, and I nursed you until you were well. You promised to give me two wishes, whatever I asked. Now I will ask them. First: let my son Bharata be crowned king, and not Rama. Second: let Rama go away and live in the Dandaka forest for fourteen years, dressed in bark like a hermit."

The king was heartbroken. All night long he begged her to change her mind, but she would not.

In the morning Rama came to greet his father. The old king tried to speak, but he could only cry out "Rama!" and weep.

"Mother," said Rama to Kaikeyi, "what has happened? Have I done something wrong?"

"Your father made me a promise long ago," said Kaikeyi, "but he is afraid to tell you what it is. Will you promise to obey him?"

"Tell me," said Rama. "I will do whatever my father has promised, for I have never told a lie."

So Kaikeyi told him: Bharata would be king, and Rama must go and live in the forest for fourteen years.

Rama listened calmly. He was not sad, and he was not angry. And he got ready to leave his home.

He went to his wife and said, "Sita, I must go into the forest for fourteen years. Stay here, my dear, and be kind to Bharata, and look after my mother."

"What are you saying?" said Sita. "A wife goes wherever her husband goes. If you go into the forest, I will walk in front of you, and clear the thorns from your path! I don't want palaces or chariots. I would be happier with you in the wild woods, where the deer run, than in my father's house. I will gather fruit for us, and we will swim in the lotus lakes together." Rama told her about all the dangers of the forest, but she would not listen.

Then Rama's brother Lakshmana came, with tears in his eyes. "Let me come too," he begged. "I will carry my bow and guard you. I will cut down trees and build your hut, and find fruit and roots for you to eat. And I will keep watch over you every night."

"Stay here, Lakshmana," said Rama, "and look after our mothers."

"Our mothers have thousands of servants and soldiers to look after them," said Lakshmana. "Let me come and look after you and Sita."

So Rama agreed. He sent Lakshmana to fetch their weapons, and he gave away all his riches to the priests. And Rama, Sita and Lakshmana set off into the forest.

Soon afterwards, the old king died of grief. Bharata, who had been far away, came home and found out what had happened. He would not take the throne. Instead, he went into the forest to find Rama, and begged him to come home and be king.

"Everyone wants you to be king, Rama," said Bharata. "The lords and the farmers, the cities and the villages. Please come home."

But Rama would not break his father's promise. "The moon may lose its light," he said, "and the Himalayas may lose their snow, and the ocean may spill over its shores. But I will not break my word."

"Then give me your sandals," said Bharata. "I will put them on the throne, and they will rule in your place until you come home."

So Rama gave Bharata his sandals, and Bharata bowed to them. "For fourteen years," he said, "I will live in the palace like a hermit, and eat only wild fruit, and wait for you to come back."
