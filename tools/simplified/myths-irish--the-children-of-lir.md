Long, long ago, before there were any kings in Ireland as we know them, the land belonged to a magical people called the Dedannans. They lived in great halls inside the green hills, and they knew all kinds of enchantments.

One of their chiefs was Lir, a good and kind man. When his first wife died, the king of the Dedannans, Bove Derg, gave him one of his own foster daughters to marry. Her name was Eve, and Lir loved her dearly.

Eve had four children: first twins, a girl called Finola and a boy called Aed, and then twin boys, Ficra and Conn. But when the little boys were born, Eve died. Lir would have died of sorrow himself, if it had not been for his four children. He loved them more than anything in the world.

So that the children would have a mother, the king gave Lir Eve's sister, Eva, as his new wife. At first Eva loved the children too. Lir used to get up at dawn and go to their beds, to talk to them and cuddle them. The king came to visit them many times every year. And whenever the Dedannans held a feast at Lir's house, everyone loved the four children best of all, for there were no lovelier or gentler children anywhere.

But Eva began to feel that nobody cared about her any more, because everybody loved the children. Jealousy crept into her heart like poison, and it turned her love into hatred. For a whole year she lay in bed, pretending to be ill and brooding. Then one day she got up and ordered her chariot, and said she would take the children to visit the king.

Finola did not want to go. She had dreamed something dark about her stepmother. But she could not stay behind.

On the way, Eva tried to make her servants kill the children. "Never!" they said. "That is a terrible thing even to think of." So Eva drove on until they came to a lake called Lake Darvra. She told the children to go and bathe in the clear water. And as soon as they were in the lake, she struck them one by one with a magic wand, and turned them into four beautiful snow-white swans.

The four swans turned to look at her, and Finola spoke. She still had a human voice. "This is an evil thing you have done, Eva," she said. "Our friends will punish you for it. Now tell us: how long must we stay like this?"

"It would be better if you had not asked," said Eva. "But I will tell you. Three hundred years on this lake. Three hundred years on the Sea of Moyle, the cold, stormy sea between Ireland and Scotland. And three hundred years on the wild Western Sea. Nothing can free you until a prince from the north marries a princess from the south, and until you hear the sound of a Christian bell."

Then Eva felt a little sorry for what she had done. "I can't undo it," she said, "but I will give you this. You will keep your own voices and your own minds. And you will sing music sweeter than any music in the world, so sweet that everyone who hears it will fall gently asleep."

And she drove away and left them on the lake.

When she arrived at the king's palace, Bove Derg asked, "Where are Lir's children?"

"Lir won't let them come to you any more," she said. "He doesn't trust you."

The king was astonished, for he loved the children better than his own. He sent messengers to Lir at once. When Lir heard that the children had never arrived, his heart went cold, and he set out to find them. On the way, he came to the shore of Lake Darvra. And there were four white swans, swimming towards him, and speaking to him in the voices of his children.

"Father," said Finola, "we are your children. Our stepmother turned us into swans because she was jealous."

Lir and all his people cried out in sorrow. "Can you be changed back?" he asked.

"No one can do it," said Finola, "until the time is over, nine hundred years from now."

"Then come home with me," said Lir, "and live with me, even as swans."

"We can't," said Finola. "We must stay on the water. But stay with us tonight, Father, and we will sing for you." So Lir stayed by the lake, and the swans sang their sweet, sad music, and everyone who heard it fell into a calm and gentle sleep.

In the morning Lir went to the king's palace, and told him what Eva had done. The king looked at Eva, and he could see in her face that it was true. He was very angry. "What is the shape you hate most in the whole world?" he asked her. And she had to tell the truth: "A demon of the air."

"Then that is what you will be," said the king, and he struck her with his own magic wand. Eva spread a pair of dark wings and flew screaming up into the clouds. And she is a demon of the air still, and she will be until the end of time.

Then the king and Lir and all the Dedannans came and camped beside Lake Darvra, to be near the swans. People came from all over Ireland to hear their singing. In the daytime the swans talked with their friends, and at night they sang, and anyone who was sad, or ill, or in pain forgot their troubles and fell asleep, and woke up happy. And so three hundred years went by, almost as happily as if the children had been at home.

But at last the time came for them to fly to the Sea of Moyle. They said goodbye to their father and all their friends, and rose up into the air, and flew away north. And the people of Ireland were so sad to see them go that they made a law: from that day on, no one in Ireland was ever to kill a swan.

Life on the Sea of Moyle was hard. The coast was all steep, dark rocks, and the sea was wild, and the swans were cold and hungry and lonely. One night, Finola saw black clouds gathering. "Brothers," she said, "a great storm is coming, and it may blow us apart. If it does, let's meet at the Rock of the Seals." And so they agreed.

At midnight the storm came. The wind roared, lightning flashed, and huge waves tossed the swans this way and that, until none of them knew where the others were. In the morning the sea was calm again, and Finola swam to the Rock of the Seals. But there was no one there. She looked all around over the wide sea, and she could not see her brothers anywhere, and she cried as if her heart would break.

Then she saw Conn swimming towards her, with his head drooping and his feathers soaked. Then came Ficra, so cold and tired that he could not say a word. Finola tucked the two of them under her wings. "If only Aed were here," she said. And then along came Aed, with his head up and his feathers dry and shining. Finola tucked him under the feathers of her breast. And so they stayed, close together, the four of them.

"This was a bad night," said Finola, "but we will have many more like it."

And they did. One January night it was so cold that the whole sea froze. The swans' feet and wings froze to the rock, and when they pulled themselves free in the morning, they left their feathers behind. It was a long time before their feathers grew back. But Finola always kept her brothers warm under her wings, the two younger ones under each wing, and Aed under her breast.

Once, a troop of riders came along the shore on white horses, and the swans swam over to see who they were. They were Dedannans, the sons of King Bove Derg, who had been searching for them for years. "How is our father?" the swans asked.

"He is well," they said. "Everyone is at his house for a great feast. They would be perfectly happy, if only you were with them."

"We have been so cold and so lonely," said Finola. "We used to wear soft robes, and drink from cups of gold, and harpers sang us to sleep. Now our beds are wet rocks, and our lullaby is the roar of the waves." Then the riders had to go home without them, for the swans could not leave the Sea of Moyle.

At last that second three hundred years was over too, and the swans flew west to the wild Western Sea. It was just as cold and stormy there. But there they met a young man called Ebric, who loved to listen to their singing, and they told him their whole story. That is how it came to be remembered and told, as it is told here.

One night, the frost was so bitter that even Finola could not comfort her brothers. Then she said, "Let us believe in the great God who made the earth and the sea, and trust in Him, and He will help us." And they did. And from that night on, neither cold nor storm could hurt them any more.

When their third three hundred years were over, the swans flew home at last, to their father's house. But when they got there, it was all in ruins. There were no people, no fires, no horses, no hounds, only grass and nettles growing over the old walls. Nine hundred years had passed, and their father and all his people had gone long ago. The four swans stayed there that night and sang their sad, sweet music. Then they flew back to the west and settled on a little lake on the island of Inis Glora. They sang so beautifully there that birds came from all around to listen.

Now in those days Saint Patrick had come to Ireland, and a holy man called Kemoc came to live on Inis Glora. On his first morning there, he rang his little bell for prayers. The swans had never heard such a sound. The brothers were frightened, but Finola said, "That is the Christian bell! Our troubles are nearly over."

Kemoc heard the swans singing, and he came down to the lake. "Are you the children of Lir?" he asked.

"We are," they said.

"Thank God I have found you," said Kemoc. "It was to find you that I came to this island." He took them home with him, and he had a silver chain made to link Finola and Aed, and another to link Ficra and Conn. The swans lived with him and prayed with him, and they were so happy that they forgot all their sufferings.

Then the last part of Eva's spell came true. The king of the north, Largnen, married Decca, a princess from the south. And Decca heard about the wonderful singing swans, and wanted them for herself. Kemoc would not give them up. So the king came himself and took hold of the silver chains, and began to drag the swans away.

As soon as he touched them, their white feathers fell away. And there stood, not four swans, but four very, very old people: a woman and three men, with white hair and wrinkled skin. For they had lived for nine hundred years. The king was so frightened that he ran away without a word.

"Kemoc," said Finola, "we are going to die very soon. Baptize us, and bury us together. And put my brothers beside me as I used to shelter them when we were swans: Conn on my right, Ficra on my left, and Aed in front of me."

So Kemoc baptized them. And as he did, he saw four lovely children with shining silver wings and faces full of joy. They looked at him for a moment, and then they rose up and were gone, and he knew they had gone to heaven. He was glad for them. But he cried too, because he had loved them.

He buried them together, just as Finola had asked, and he carved their names on the stone above them, in the old Irish letters called Ogham.
