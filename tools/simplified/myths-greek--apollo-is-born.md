---
title: Apollo Is Born
adaptedFrom: hesiod-hymns--hymn-to-apollo

The goddess Leto was going to have a baby, and the baby's father was Zeus, the king of the gods. But Zeus's wife, Hera, was jealous and angry. Everyone was afraid of her. So when Leto went looking for a place to have her baby, no land in the whole world would let her stay.

She went to Crete and to Athens, to mountains and to islands, to rich cities and to rocky shores. Everywhere, the land trembled and said no. They were all afraid of Hera, and they were afraid of the baby too. "He will be a mighty god," they said. "What if he is cruel to us?"

At last Leto came to a tiny island called Delos. It was rocky and bare, with hardly any grass, and it floated alone in the middle of the sea.

"Delos," said Leto, "will you let my son be born here? If you do, he will build a great temple on you. People will come from all over the world to visit, and bring you gifts. You will never be poor again."

Delos was glad, but also afraid. "People say your son will be very proud," said the island. "What if he looks at me and thinks, 'What a poor, rocky little island,' and kicks me down to the bottom of the sea? Then only octopuses and seals would live on me."

"I promise you," said Leto, and she swore the most solemn promise the gods can make, "that my son will build his first temple here, and will love you above all other places."

So Delos let her stay. But Leto's baby did not come. For nine days and nine nights she waited and waited in pain. All the great goddesses came to be with her, except for Hera. And Hera had kept Eileithyia, the goddess who helps babies to be born, up on Mount Olympus, so that she would not hear about Leto at all.

The goddesses sent Iris, the rainbow messenger, to fetch her. Iris ran as fast as the wind up to Olympus. She called Eileithyia out quietly, so that Hera would not notice, and promised her a beautiful golden necklace if she would come. And the two of them flew down to Delos together, like a pair of shy doves.

As soon as Eileithyia set foot on the island, the baby came. Leto put her arms around a palm tree and knelt in the soft grass, and the earth smiled for joy beneath her. Then the baby was born, and his name was Apollo.

The goddesses washed him in clear water and wrapped him in a soft white cloth with a band of gold. Instead of milk, the goddess Themis fed him nectar and ambrosia, the food of the gods. And as soon as he tasted it, the baby burst out of his wrappings. He stood up, and he spoke.

"The lyre and the curved bow shall be mine," said Apollo, "and I will tell people the will of Zeus."

Then he began to walk across the island, and all the goddesses stared in wonder. And the little rocky island of Delos was so happy that it turned golden all over, and blossomed with flowers like a mountaintop in spring.

Apollo grew up quickly. He played his golden lyre so beautifully that when he played on Mount Olympus, all the gods forgot everything else to listen, and the Muses sang, and the goddesses danced.

Then Apollo set off across Greece to find a place for a temple, where people could come to ask him questions about the future. He traveled from place to place, but nowhere seemed quite right.

At last he came to a lovely meadow beside a spring. The spring had a nymph living in it, named Telphusa. "This is the place," said Apollo, and he began to lay out the stones for his temple.

But Telphusa did not want to share her spring. She wanted to keep all the fame for herself. So she tricked him. "This is a noisy place, lord Apollo," she said sweetly. "Horses and mules come here to drink all day, stamping and splashing. People will look at the horses instead of your temple. Go to Crisa, under Mount Parnassus. It is much quieter there."

So Apollo went to the place below Mount Parnassus, where a cliff hung high above a rocky valley. "Here I will build my temple," he said. He laid the foundation stones, and two great builders laid the stone floor, and many people came to help build the walls.

But Telphusa had not told him everything. Near that place, by a sweet spring, lived a huge, fat she-dragon. She was a terrible monster who ate people and their sheep. Once she had even helped Hera to raise another monster, called Typhaon.

Apollo took his bow and shot the dragon with a mighty arrow. The monster twisted and thrashed and hissed, and at last she lay still.

"Now rot here in the sun," said Apollo, "and never hurt anyone again."

And the hot sun made the dragon rot away. So the place was called Pytho, which means "rotting," and Apollo was called the Pythian god. Later it became known as Delphi.

Then Apollo understood that Telphusa had tricked him and sent him toward the dragon. He went back to her spring and pushed a pile of rocks over it, so that her water was hidden. And he built himself an altar there instead.

Now Apollo needed priests to look after his temple. Far out at sea, he saw a ship sailing from Crete, full of good men. Apollo changed himself into a huge dolphin and leaped onto their deck.

The sailors were terrified. They tried to push the dolphin overboard, but it shook the whole ship until the timbers rattled. So they sat very still and did not dare to touch the sails, and a strong wind blew the ship along, around the coast of Greece and into a quiet bay below Mount Parnassus.

Then the dolphin leaped off the ship like a shooting star, with sparks flying all around him. He flashed into his temple and made a bright flame there. Then he came back to the ship as a tall young man with long hair.

"Who are you, strangers?" he asked.

"We are from Crete," said their captain. "We were sailing somewhere else, but some god brought us here instead."

"I am Apollo, son of Zeus," he said. "I brought you here. You will never go back to Crete, but you will be my priests, and look after my temple, and be honored by everyone for all time. And because I came to you as a dolphin, you shall call me Apollo Delphinius, Apollo of the Dolphin."

The men were worried. "But how will we live?" they asked. "Nothing grows on these rocky hills. There are no vineyards and no fields."

Apollo smiled. "Don't worry," he said. "People will come from all over the world, and they will bring more than enough for you. Only look after my temple well, and be fair and good."

So Apollo led them up the mountain, playing his lyre as he went, and the sailors marched behind him, singing. And there, at Delphi, they kept Apollo's temple, and people came from far and wide to ask the god about the future.
