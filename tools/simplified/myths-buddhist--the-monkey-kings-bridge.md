---
title: The Monkey King's Bridge
adaptedFrom: cowell-jataka--the-monkey-kings-bridge

namesNote: The Jātaka tells of the Buddha's earlier lives, before he became the Buddha. A bow-length is the length of a bow, about as tall as a man.
---

Long ago, when Brahmadatta was king of Benares, the Buddha-to-be was born as a monkey. He grew up big and strong, and he lived in the Himalaya mountains as the king of eighty thousand monkeys.

Near the bank of the river Ganges there grew a great mango tree, as big and shady as a mountain. Its mangoes were as big as water jars, and they smelled and tasted like the food of the gods. Some fell on the ground, and some fell back into the tree, but one branch hung out over the river.

The monkey king thought, "If one of these mangoes ever falls into the river, it will bring danger to us." So whenever that branch was in flower, he made the monkeys eat the flowers or throw them down while they were still tiny, so that no fruit would grow there over the water.

But one mango grew there all the same, hidden behind an ants' nest where none of the eighty thousand monkeys could see it. When it was ripe, it fell into the river, and floated all the way down to Benares. There the king of Benares was bathing in the river, with one net stretched across the water above him and another below him. And the mango was caught in the net.

That evening, as the king was leaving, the fishermen pulling in the net found the mango. They did not know what it was, so they showed it to the king.

"What is this fruit?" asked the king.

"We don't know, sire."

"Who will know?"

"The foresters, sire."

So the king sent for the foresters, and they told him it was a mango. The king cut it with a knife. First he made the foresters taste it, and then he ate some himself, and gave some to his wives and his ministers. The taste of that mango filled the king's whole body, and he could think of nothing else. He asked the foresters where the tree grew, and they told him: on a river bank, far away in the Himalayas.

So the king had many rafts tied together, and sailed up the river, with the foresters to show him the way. At last they came to the place. "Sire," said the foresters, "there is the tree."

The king left the rafts and went to the tree with his men. He had a bed made for him at the foot of the tree, and he ate the mangoes and enjoyed their wonderful taste, and lay down to sleep. His men lit fires and kept guard all around.

At midnight, when the men had fallen asleep, the monkey king came with his eighty thousand monkeys, and they went from branch to branch, eating the mangoes. The king woke up and saw them. He woke his men, and called his archers. "Surround those monkeys, so that they can't escape," he said, "and shoot them."

The archers surrounded the tree, with their arrows ready. The monkeys saw them, and they were terrified, for there was no way to escape. They came shivering to their king. "Sire," they said, "the archers are all around the tree, and they say they will shoot us. What shall we do?"

"Don't be afraid," said the monkey king. "I will save you."

He climbed up a branch that went straight up, and along another branch that stretched out over the river. Then, from the very end of it, he leaped. He flew through the air, a hundred bow-lengths, all the way across the river, and landed in a bush on the far bank.

He climbed down, and looked back across the water. "That is how far I have come," he thought. He cut a long bamboo shoot at the root and stripped off its leaves. "This much will be tied to the tree on this side," he thought, "and this much will stretch across the air." But he forgot to count the piece that he would tie around his own waist.

He tied one end of the bamboo to a tree on the river bank, and the other end around his waist. Then he leaped back across the river, as fast as a cloud blown by the wind. But because he had forgotten the piece around his waist, the bamboo was too short, and he could not quite reach the mango tree. So he caught hold of a branch with both hands and held on tight. "Go quickly," he called to his monkeys, "and good luck to you! Run across my back and along the bamboo!"

So the eighty thousand monkeys bowed to their king, and said goodbye, and ran across his back and along the bamboo to safety.

But one monkey in the troop was the monkey king's enemy. "Now's my chance to get rid of him," he thought. He climbed up high, and jumped down hard on the monkey king's back. The monkey king's heart broke, and he was in terrible pain. Then his enemy ran away, and the monkey king was left all alone.

The king of Benares had been lying awake, watching everything the monkeys and their king had done. "This animal did not care about his own life," he thought. "He has saved his whole troop." In the morning, he thought, "It is not right to kill this king of the monkeys. I will bring him down and take care of him."

So he had his rafts brought round on the river and a platform built on them, and gently brought the monkey king down. He had him wrapped in a yellow robe and washed in the water of the Ganges, and gave him sugar-water to drink, and had his body rubbed with the finest oil. Then he laid him on a soft bed, and sat down beside him on a low seat, and said:

> You made yourself a bridge, so that they could cross in safety.
> Monkey, what are you to them, and what are they to you?

And the monkey king answered:

> Victorious king, I am their king. I guard them, and I lead them.
> They were terrified of you, and full of grief.

> So I leaped a hundred bow-lengths, with a bamboo tied around me.
> I reached the tree like a storm-cloud blown by the wind.
> My strength gave out, but I caught a branch and held it fast.

> And as I hung there, stretched out between the bamboo and the branch,
> my monkeys ran across my back, and now they are safe.

> So I am not afraid of dying, and my pain does not trouble me,
> for the ones I ruled are safe and happy.

> Here is a lesson for you, O king, if you want to know the truth:
> if you would truly rule, you must care about the happiness
> of your kingdom and your army, your horses and your city.

And when he had taught the king this lesson, the monkey king died.

The king of Benares gave him a funeral fit for a king. He built a shrine where the monkey king was laid to rest, and had lamps lit there, and flowers and incense brought. When he went home to Benares, he had the whole city decorated, and honoured the monkey king for seven days. And all his life he followed the monkey king's teaching. He gave to the poor, and did good, and ruled his kingdom well.

Long afterwards, the Buddha told this story. "In those days," he said, "the monkeys were my followers, and I myself was the monkey king."
