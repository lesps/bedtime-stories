Long ago, Meave was the queen of Connaught, in the west of Ireland. She was proud and fierce, and all the other princes of Ireland were afraid of her.

One day, Meave and her husband, King Ailill, were arguing about which of them was richer. So they had all their treasures brought out and counted: their cups and bowls, their rings and brooches, their clothes, their sheep and pigs and horses and cattle. And they were exactly equal, except for one thing.

Among Ailill's cattle was a great bull called the White-horned. He was so huge and fierce that it took fifty men to look after him. He had been born in Meave's herd. But, so her herdsman told her, he had heard the cowherds say it was strange for such a mighty bull to belong to a woman, and he had broken loose and gone over to Ailill's herd instead.

Meave was furious. Without that bull, all her riches seemed worth nothing to her.

"I know where there is a bull as good as the White-horned," said her herald, Mac Roth. "Even better. He is called the Brown Bull of Cooley, and he belongs to a man called Daire, in Ulster. He is as dark as night, and so big that fifty boys can play on his back. And these two bulls are no ordinary cattle. They are the cattle of the gods, and they have come into the world to start a war."

"Then go and ask Daire to lend him to me," said Meave. "Offer him whatever he wants. And if he won't give the bull, we will come and take it."

So Mac Roth and nine of his men went to Daire. "Lend the Brown Bull to Queen Meave," said Mac Roth, "and she will give you fifty young cows, and a piece of the best land in Connaught, and her friendship besides."

Daire was so pleased with this offer that he threw himself down on his couch and laughed until it burst at the seams. "I'll lend it to her gladly!" he said.

That night he gave the messengers a great feast. But they drank too much wine, and they began to talk carelessly. "It's lucky Daire is giving us the bull," said one, "because all the warriors in Ireland could not take it from Ulster by force."

"Don't be so sure," said another. "If he hadn't given it to us, we would have taken it anyway."

Daire's steward heard this, and he went and told his master. Daire was furious. In the morning, when the messengers came to fetch the bull, he said, "You will not have it. Your own men said that if I didn't give it, you would take it by force. Let Meave come and take it if she can!"

When Mac Roth came home and told Meave, she said, "I thought as much. If we can't get the bull by fair means, we'll get it by foul." For this was exactly the excuse she wanted for a war.

And Meave had chosen her time well. For the men of Ulster were under a curse. Long before, a goddess called Macha had come to live among them, and they had laughed at her and mocked her. So she had cursed them: in their hour of greatest danger, all their warriors would fall weak and helpless. Only the boy Cuchulain was not under the curse, for he was too young.

So Meave gathered a great army from all over Ireland and marched into Ulster to take the Brown Bull. And the men of Ulster lay weak and helpless, and could not fight. Only Cuchulain was left to defend Ulster. He stood alone at a ford, where the army had to cross a river, and every day he fought one of Meave's champions, one at a time, and every day he won. All winter long he held back the whole army of Ireland.

Now in Meave's army there was a great warrior called Ferdia. When they were boys, Ferdia and Cuchulain had gone together to a warrior queen called Scáthach, in a land of shadows across the sea, to learn the arts of war. She had taught them both everything she knew. They had been best friends, closer than brothers. So Ferdia stayed in his tent, and would not fight his friend.

But at last Meave sent for him. She gave him a great feast, and seated him beside her lovely daughter, Finnabar, with her golden hair. "Fight Cuchulain," said Meave, "and I will give you a chariot fit for a king, and land in Connaught, and my daughter for your wife." She even took off her own royal brooch and pinned it on his cloak.

Ferdia remembered his friend, and his heart was heavy. But he was dazzled by Finnabar and by all the gifts, and he said, "Even if I fall, I will go and meet the Hound."

Old Fergus, who had been Cuchulain's foster father, heard this. He jumped into his chariot that very night and drove to warn Cuchulain. "Tomorrow your friend Ferdia will come to fight you," he said. "Be careful. His skin is so tough that no sword can cut it."

"This is bad news," said Cuchulain. "Not because I'm afraid of him, but because I love him."

In the morning the two friends met at the ford, Cuchulain on the north bank and Ferdia on the south.

"Ferdia," said Cuchulain, "you should not have listened to Meave. When we were with Scáthach, we went into every battle side by side. We were brothers, and each of us always kept the other safe."

"Don't remind me of all that, Hound," said Ferdia. "It won't help you now. Let's choose our weapons."

All the first day they threw their little shields and darts at each other, from dawn until midday, and they were so skilful that every one was caught on the other's shield. Then they threw spears until evening, and each of them was wounded. When night came, they threw down their weapons, ran to each other, put their arms round each other's necks and gave each other three kisses, like the old friends they were. That night their horses shared the same field, and their charioteers sat by the same fire. Healers came to tend their wounds, and for every herb that was put on Cuchulain's wounds, he sent the same over the ford to Ferdia. And for all the good food and drink that Meave's people sent to Ferdia, he sent half across the ford to Cuchulain, who had nobody to bring him anything.

The second day they fought from their chariots with heavy spears, until their horses were exhausted. "Let's stop, Ferdia," said Cuchulain at evening. "We are not giants of the sea, who must fight for ever. Let us be friends again." And again they kissed each other three times, and shared their healing herbs and their food.

On the third day, Cuchulain saw that Ferdia's face was dark and gloomy. "Turn back, Ferdia," he begged him. "Not all of Meave's riches would have made me fight you. Today it must be a fight to the death, and I don't have the heart to fight you. Turn back. Meave's promises are false."

"Don't ask me to turn back, gentle Hound," said Ferdia. "It isn't you who has done me wrong. It is Meave. But I cannot go back now."

So they fought with their heavy swords all day, until they were both wounded from head to foot. And that night they parted in sadness. Their horses were not in the same field, and their charioteers did not share a fire.

On the fourth day they fought the fiercest fight of all. They fought so hard that the river was pushed out of its bed. The horses of the army broke loose and galloped away into the woods in fright. Then Ferdia struck Cuchulain a great blow, and the water of the ford was red with Cuchulain's blood. And Cuchulain called for his terrible spear, the Gae Bolga, which Scáthach had taught to him alone. He threw it, and Ferdia fell.

"It is over," said Ferdia. "But how sad it is that I should fall by your hand, Hound."

Cuchulain ran to him and lifted him in his arms and carried him across the ford to the northern bank, to the side of Ulster. And there he laid him down, and a great faintness came over Cuchulain. "What good is it for me to get up," he said, "now that my friend has fallen by my hand? We promised each other, when we were boys, that we would never harm each other. I wish I had died instead of you."

He asked to see the brooch Meave had given Ferdia, the brooch he had fought his friend for. And he held it in his hand and wept.

"All my fights until now were only play," said Cuchulain, "compared with this fight with Ferdia."

Then Cuchulain lay wounded for a long time. But at last the curse was over, and the men of Ulster rose up, strong again, and marched out to battle. And Meave's great army was beaten, and ran away westward towards home.

As evening fell, Cuchulain came to the river Shannon and saw Meave's army crossing it in flight. He went into a wood to rest, and there, lying on the ground, worn out and alone, he found Queen Meave herself.

"Why are you lying here, Meave?" he asked sternly. "Your army is running away, and the men of Ulster are close behind it."

The proud queen had no pride left. "I am worn out," she said. "Help me, generous enemy. Guard the ford, and hold back the men of Ulster, so my people can go home in safety. I have boasted many times about my strength and my great army, and now it has all come to nothing. I turn to you, my enemy, to protect me."

"Nobody will ever say that I refused a woman who asked me for help," said Cuchulain. "Rest here. I will protect your army."

So all through the twilight, Cuchulain stood alone between Meave's army and the men of Ulster, and he held back his own people until every last one of Meave's soldiers had crossed the river safely.

When Fergus came looking for the queen, he found her resting peacefully under the trees, her army safe, and Cuchulain standing guard on the riverbank, leaning on his sword. And Fergus laughed until the sky rang. "What a strange end to the day!" he said. "Our army has been swept away, our queen is having a rest, and the one who guards us is our worst enemy!"

"It's time you came, Fergus," said Cuchulain. "I leave her to you now. My work is done." Then he swung his sword and sliced the top clean off a little hill nearby. "Let that hill stand between Connaught and Ulster for ever," he said, "to show our strength and our gentleness." And he drove home to Ulster, to his wife, Emer. And Meave never made war on Ulster again.

But what happened to the Brown Bull? He had been carried off to the west, and when the White-horned heard him bellowing, he came to meet him. As soon as they saw each other, the two great bulls charged. They fought all day and all night, and nobody in the whole country could sleep for the noise of their bellowing. At last the Brown Bull beat the White-horned. Then he turned and ran all the way back home to Cooley, bellowing as he went. And when he got there, worn out, the great Brown Bull gave one last mighty roar, and fell down dead.

And so the two bulls of the gods, who had come into the world to start a war, left it again.
