Far up the river Nile, on the island of Elephantine, the people worshipped the god Khnum. Khnum had the head of a ram, with long flat horns. He was one of the oldest gods of all.

Khnum was a potter. Long ago, he had made the first man on his potter's wheel, shaping him out of clay. He had also made the egg from which the sun was born, and he made the bodies of the gods.

And Khnum was the god of the river Nile, and of the great flood that came every year, when the Nile rose up and spread over the fields, so that the crops could grow.

Long ago, in the days of King Djoser, a terrible thing happened. For seven years, the Nile did not rise. There was no flood. The fields were dry, and nothing grew. There was no grain, and no vegetables, and the people had nothing to eat. Strong men tottered like old men. The children cried with hunger. And people became so desperate that they stole food from their neighbours.

King Djoser sat on his throne, full of sorrow. He remembered the wise Imhotep, who had once saved Egypt from a famine like this. But when he called on Imhotep for help, no answer came.

So the king sent a message to Mater, his governor in the south. "Where does the Nile come from?" he asked. "And what god or goddess rules over it?"

Mater came to the king himself, to tell him. He told him about the island of Elephantine, where the very first city was built, and where the sun rose when he wanted to give life to people. On that island, Mater said, there was a double cave called Querti, shaped like two round hills. And from that cave the flood of the Nile came pouring out every year, when the god drew back the bolts of the door at the right time. "And that god," said Mater, "is Khnum."

So King Djoser went to Khnum's temple, and made offerings to him, and prayed to him.

And Khnum heard him, and came to the king. "I am Khnum the Creator," he said. "My hands are upon you, to protect you and keep you well. I made myself. I am the deep ocean, and I am the Nile, who rises when he wishes, to give health to people who work hard."

And Khnum promised the king that from now on, the Nile would rise every year, as it did in the old days. The famine would end, and good times would come back to the land.

But Khnum also told the king that his temple was falling down, and that nobody had bothered to mend it, even though there were plenty of stones lying around.

The king did not forget. He gave Khnum's temple the fields on both sides of the river near the island, and sent priests to serve there, and made the people of the land nearby bring part of their crops to the temple. And he had all this carved on a great stone, so that everyone would remember how Khnum had saved Egypt from the seven years of famine.
