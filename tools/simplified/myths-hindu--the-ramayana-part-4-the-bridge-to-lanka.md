When Hanuman came back with news of Sita, Rama and King Sugriva marched south with the great army of the Vanars, the monkey people, until they came to the shore of the sea. Ravana's good brother Vibhishana had come over to their side too. He had begged Ravana to give Sita back, but Ravana would not listen, and drove him away.

Now there was only the sea between the army and the island of Lanka. But how could they cross it?

Rama spread out a bed of holy grass on the shore, and lay down on it with his face to the east, his arm for a pillow. And he prayed to the Ocean to help them. For three nights he lay there, praying. But the Ocean did not come.

At last Rama grew angry. "Look, Lakshmana," he said. "When you are gentle and patient, proud people think you are weak. The Ocean will not show himself to me. Bring me my bow! I will dry up the sea with my arrows, and the monkeys can walk across."

He shot his arrows into the sea, and the sea began to heave and boil. Great waves rose up, and the serpents and the creatures of the deep were terrified. Then Lakshmana took Rama's bow away. "Don't do this, brother," he said. "There must be a better way." And the holy sages in the sky called out, "Don't! Don't!"

But Rama fitted a mighty arrow to his bow, and drew it back. Heaven and earth seemed to split apart. The mountains trembled, darkness covered the world, and the stars came out beside the sun. And the sea swept up over its shores.

Then, out of the middle of the sea, the Ocean himself rose up, like the sun rising over a mountain. He shone like a cool blue jewel, with a garland of flowers on his head and golden ornaments, and all around him flowed the great rivers that run into the sea. He joined his hands and bowed to Rama.

"Rama," he said, "it is my nature to be deep, and too wide to cross. I cannot change that. But I will tell you how your army can cross me, as if it were dry land. And while your army crosses, I will not let any of my creatures harm them."

"But I have already drawn back this arrow," said Rama. "Where shall it fall?"

"Far away in the north," said the Ocean, "there is a place where wicked robbers drink my water. Shoot your arrow there."

So Rama shot the arrow, and where it fell, the water dried up, and the place became a desert. But Rama blessed the desert, so that it would be full of good grass for the animals, and sweet fruit and roots, and healing herbs.

"Among your monkeys is Nala," said the Ocean. "He is the son of Vishvakarma, the builder of the gods, and he is as clever as his father. Let him build a bridge across me, and I will hold it up." And the Ocean sank back under the waves.

Then Nala stood up. "It's true," he said. "My father gave my mother a promise that her son would be just like him. I can build a bridge across the sea."

So Rama gave the order, and the monkeys rushed into the forest, hundreds and thousands of them. They pulled up trees, palm trees and mango trees and flowering trees, and dragged them down to the shore. They tore up rocks as big as elephants, and threw them into the sea, and the water splashed up to the sky. Some monkeys held long cords to keep the bridge straight, and others carried the wood. And Nala built the bridge.

On the first day they built fifteen yojanas of bridge. On the second day they built twenty. On the third day they built twenty-one, and on the fourth day twenty-two. And on the fifth day they built twenty-three yojanas more, and reached the shore of Lanka.

The great bridge stretched across the sea like the Milky Way across the night sky. The gods came to watch, and they were amazed. Then the whole army crossed over. Rama rode on Hanuman's back, and Lakshmana rode on the back of Prince Angada. Some monkeys marched along the bridge, some swam in the sea, and some leaped through the air like birds. And the noise of the army was louder than the roar of the sea.

On the shore of Lanka, Vibhishana stood guard with his mace. And the gods came down and sprinkled Rama with holy water. "Victory to you, Rama!" they said.
