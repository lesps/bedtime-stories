Long ago, in the south-west of Wales, there was a prince called Pwyll, who ruled the land of Dyfed.

One day Pwyll went hunting in the woods. He lost his companions, and as he followed his hounds, he heard another pack of hounds coming the other way. In a clearing, he saw a stag, and the other hounds caught it and brought it down. And they were the strangest hounds he had ever seen: shining white, with bright red ears. Pwyll drove the strange hounds away from the stag, and set his own dogs on it instead.

Then a horseman came riding up on a big grey horse, dressed in grey hunting clothes, with a horn around his neck. "I know who you are, prince," he said, "and I will not greet you. I never saw anyone so rude. You drove away my hounds from the stag they had caught, and gave it to your own."

"If I have done wrong," said Pwyll, "I will make it up to you. But who are you?"

"I am a king," said the horseman. "My name is Arawn, and I am a king of Annwn, the Otherworld."

"Lord," said Pwyll, "how can I win your friendship?"

"Like this," said Arawn. "There is another king in Annwn, called Hafgan, who is always making war on me. Every year we fight at a ford. I want you to go to Annwn in my place, looking exactly like me, and rule my kingdom for a year. And in a year's time, meet Hafgan at the ford. Strike him one blow, and he will never trouble me again. But if he begs you to strike him a second time, don't do it, however much he asks. Once I did that, and the next day he was as strong as ever."

"But what about my own kingdom?" asked Pwyll.

"I will go there in your place," said Arawn, "looking exactly like you, and no one will know."

So Arawn put his own shape upon Pwyll, and showed him the way to his palace in the Otherworld. It was the most beautiful place Pwyll had ever seen. The servants came and dressed him in silk and gold, and everyone greeted him as their king, and nobody guessed that he was not Arawn. And the queen was the loveliest and kindest lady he had ever met. So Pwyll spent the year in Annwn, hunting and feasting and listening to music.

When the year was over, Pwyll went to the ford to meet Hafgan. The two kings rode at each other in the middle of the river. And with his first stroke, Pwyll split Hafgan's shield in two and threw him off his horse.

"Why have you done this to me?" groaned Hafgan. "Since you have begun, finish it. Strike me again!"

"I may be sorry for what I have done," said Pwyll, "but I will not strike you again." So Hafgan's men carried him away, and he never fought again. And all the lords of Hafgan's kingdom came to Pwyll and promised to serve him, so that by the next day the two kingdoms of Annwn were one.

Then Pwyll went back to the clearing in the woods, where Arawn was waiting for him. Each gave the other back his own shape, and they were very glad to see each other. "Heaven reward you for your friendship," said Arawn. "When you get home, you'll see what I have done for you."

When Pwyll got home, he asked his lords how he had ruled them that year.

"Lord," they said, "you were never so wise, or so kind, or so generous, or so fair as you were this year!"

So Pwyll told them the whole story. "You must thank the one who was here in my place," he said.

"Then please," said his lords, "go on ruling us the way he did!"

"I will," said Pwyll. And from then on, Pwyll and Arawn were firm friends, and sent each other horses and hounds and hawks and jewels. And Pwyll was called Pwyll, Chief of Annwn.

Near Pwyll's palace at Narberth, there was a magic mound. People said that anyone who sat on it would either be hurt, or see a wonder. "I'm not afraid of getting hurt with all my men around me," said Pwyll. "But I would like to see a wonder." So he sat on the mound.

And as he sat there, he saw a lady coming along the road on a tall, pure white horse, dressed in shining gold. Her horse seemed to be walking slowly and steadily. "Does anyone know that lady?" asked Pwyll. Nobody did. So he sent one of his men to find out who she was. But by the time the man got to the road, she had passed by. He ran after her as fast as he could, but the faster he ran, the farther away she was.

So Pwyll sent a man on the fastest horse in his stables. He galloped after her as hard as he could. But though her horse never went any faster, he came no nearer to her, until his own horse was worn out.

"There is magic here," said Pwyll.

The next day, the same thing happened. And on the third day, Pwyll got on his own horse and rode after her himself. He galloped as fast as he could, and still he could not catch her. At last he called out, "Lady, for the sake of the one you love best, wait for me!"

"I'll wait gladly," she said, "and it would have been better for your horse if you had asked me sooner!"

She stopped, and threw back her veil, and Pwyll thought she was the most beautiful woman he had ever seen.

"Where are you going, lady?" he asked.

"I came to find you," she said. "I am Rhiannon, the daughter of Hefeydd the Old. They want me to marry a man I don't love. But I will marry no one but you, unless you refuse me."

"If I could choose from all the ladies in the world," said Pwyll, "I would choose you."

"Then come to my father's palace a year from today," said Rhiannon, "and there will be a wedding feast ready for you."

A year later, Pwyll went to the palace of Hefeydd with a hundred knights, and there was a great feast. Pwyll sat between Rhiannon and her father. And while they were feasting, a tall young man with auburn hair came into the hall.

"I have come to ask you a favour, prince," he said to Pwyll.

"Whatever you ask, if I can give it, you shall have it," said Pwyll.

"Oh!" cried Rhiannon. "Why did you say that?"

"I want the lady you are going to marry," said the young man, "and this feast as well."

Pwyll could not say a word. He had given his promise in front of everyone.

"Never did a man make worse use of his wits!" said Rhiannon. "This is Gwawl, the man they wanted me to marry. But don't worry. I have a plan." And she whispered to him. Then she said to Gwawl, "This feast is not Pwyll's to give. But come back in a year, and there will be a wedding feast for you."

A year later, Gwawl came back for his wedding feast. And while he was eating and drinking, a poor man came into the hall in ragged clothes and big old shoes, carrying a little bag. It was Pwyll in disguise.

"Lord," he said to Gwawl, "I am very poor. All I ask is enough food to fill this little bag."

"That's easily done," said Gwawl. "Fill it for him!"

So the servants began to put food into the bag. But however much they put in, it was never any fuller. For Rhiannon had given Pwyll a magic bag.

"Will your bag ever be full?" asked Gwawl.

"Never," said Pwyll, "unless a great lord, with lands and riches, gets up and treads the food down with both his feet, and says, 'Enough has been put in.'"

"Go on, quickly," said Rhiannon to Gwawl.

So Gwawl got up and put both his feet in the bag. And at once Pwyll pulled the bag up over his head, and tied it shut, and blew a blast on his horn. And Pwyll's hundred knights, who had been hiding in the orchard, came rushing into the hall. Every one of them gave the bag a kick or a thump as he came in, and asked, "What's in here?"

"A badger!" said the others. And that was the first time anyone ever played the game of Badger in the Bag.

"Lord," called the man in the bag, "let me out! I don't deserve to end my days in a bag!"

So Gwawl promised that he would never try to take revenge, and that he would give gifts to all the poets and musicians at the feast. And then they let him out, all covered in bruises, and he went home.

So Pwyll and Rhiannon were married at last, and there was a great feast, and Rhiannon gave rich gifts to everyone. They went home to Narberth, and ruled the land happily.

In the third year, they had a baby son. But on the night he was born, a terrible thing happened. Six women were sitting up to watch over Rhiannon and the baby, and before midnight they all fell asleep. And when they woke up, the baby was gone.

The women were terrified that they would be punished. So they made a wicked plan, and they told everyone that Rhiannon herself had done away with her own baby. Rhiannon begged them to tell the truth, but they would not.

The lords asked Pwyll to send Rhiannon away. But Pwyll would not. So Rhiannon was given a punishment instead. For seven years she had to sit by the mounting block outside the palace gate, and tell her sad story to every stranger who came by, and offer to carry them into the palace on her back. But hardly anyone would let her.

Now, far away in another part of Wales, there lived a good lord called Teyrnon. He had a beautiful mare, and every year, on the first night of May, she had a foal. But every year the foal disappeared, and nobody ever knew what happened to it.

"This year," said Teyrnon, "I will find out." So on the first night of May he brought the mare indoors, put on his armour, and sat up to watch. In the night the mare had a big, beautiful foal. Then there was a terrible noise outside, and a huge claw came in through the window and seized the foal by its mane. Teyrnon drew his sword and struck at the claw, and the foal was saved. Then he heard a great wailing outside, and he rushed out into the dark after it. But he could see nothing. And when he came back, there on the doorstep was a baby boy, wrapped in a satin cloak.

Teyrnon and his wife had no children, so they brought the boy up as their own. They called him Gwri of the Golden Hair, because his hair was as yellow as gold. He grew amazingly fast. When he was one, he was as big as a three-year-old, and when he was four, he was already helping with the horses. So Teyrnon gave him the foal that had been born on the night he was found.

Then Teyrnon heard the story of Rhiannon and her punishment. And as he looked at the boy, he saw that he looked exactly like Pwyll, whom Teyrnon had once served. "It isn't right," he told his wife, "for us to keep this boy, while that good lady is punished because of him." His wife agreed.

So Teyrnon rode to Narberth, with the boy riding beside him on his own horse. Rhiannon was sitting by the mounting block. "Don't go any further," she said. "I will carry each of you into the palace. That is my punishment, for they say I killed my own son."

"Lady," said Teyrnon, "I will never let you carry me."

"Nor will I," said the boy.

That night at the feast, Teyrnon told Pwyll and Rhiannon the whole story. "And here is your son, lady," he said. And everyone in the hall could see that the boy was Pwyll's son.

"If this is true," cried Rhiannon, "then my anxiety is over!" And in the old Welsh language, the word for anxiety is "pryder". So the boy was named Pryderi.

Pwyll thanked Teyrnon with all his heart, and offered him the finest horses and hounds and jewels. But Teyrnon would not take any of them. He went home glad.

And Pryderi grew up to be the finest and handsomest young man in all the kingdom. And when Pwyll died, Pryderi ruled Dyfed after him, and all his people loved him.
