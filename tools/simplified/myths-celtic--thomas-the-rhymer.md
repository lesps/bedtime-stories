Long ago, in the south of Scotland, there lived a young lord called Thomas, in a tower called Ercildoune. He loved books and poetry and music, and most of all he loved to watch the birds and animals in the woods and fields around his home.

One sunny May morning, Thomas went walking in the woods by a little stream that came rushing down from the Eildon Hills. The new leaves were bright green, primroses were opening in the moss, the birds were singing as if their hearts would burst, and the water-rats were poking their noses out of their holes. Thomas was so happy that he lay down under a tree to watch it all.

Then he heard a horse coming through the bushes. He looked up, and there was the most beautiful lady he had ever seen, riding towards him on a grey horse. She wore a dress of shining silk, as green as the spring grass, and a crown of jewels that flashed like fire. Her saddle was of ivory, her stirrups were of crystal, and her reins were of gold, hung with little silver bells that rang like fairy music as she rode. She carried a hunting horn, and she led seven greyhounds on a leash.

"I am the Queen of Fairyland," she said.

Thomas knew it was dangerous for people to have anything to do with fairies. But she was so beautiful that he forgot all about being careful, and he asked her for a kiss. And that was just what she wanted. For as soon as he kissed her, she had him in her power.

At once a terrible change came over her. Her green silk faded into a long dress as grey as ashes. Her beauty faded too, and half her golden hair turned grey. And she laughed at his frightened face.

"I'm not so pretty now," she said. "But that doesn't matter. Whoever kisses the Queen of Fairyland must come with her to Fairyland, and serve her there for seven long years. You asked for the kiss, Thomas, and now you must pay for it. Get up behind me."

So Thomas climbed up behind her, trembling, and the grey horse galloped away, faster than the wind, until they left the land of the living behind. They came to the edge of a great desert, dry and bare. There the Queen stopped.

"Get down, Thomas," she said, "and lay your head on my knee, and I will show you things that no living person can see."

So Thomas laid his head on her knee, and looked out over the desert. And now he saw three roads across it.

The first road was broad and smooth and straight. "Many people choose that road," said the Queen, "because it is easy. But it leads to a bad end."

The second road was narrow and winding, with thorns on one side and briars on the other, so tangled that it was hard to get along it at all. "Few people choose that road," said the Queen. "But that is the road of goodness, and though it is hard, it leads to a glorious city, the City of the Great King."

The third road was a bonnie road, winding up a hillside among the ferns and heather and golden gorse. "And that road leads to Fairyland," said the Queen, "and that is the road we will take. But listen to me, Thomas. When we get there, you must not say a single word to anyone except me. If you do, you will have to stay in Fairyland for ever."

They rode up the bonnie road. But soon it led them down into a deep, dark valley that went down under the ground, where there was no light at all. They could hear rushing water everywhere. Then the horse plunged into a river, and the cold water rose up over Thomas's feet, and then over his knees. Thomas was so frightened that he nearly fainted, and he held on tight to the Queen's grey dress.

At last the darkness grew lighter, and they came out into bright sunshine, in a beautiful orchard full of apples and pears and figs. Thomas was so hungry and thirsty that he reached out to pick some fruit.

"Don't touch it!" said the Queen. "If you eat anything here, you will have to stay in Fairyland for ever. The only thing that is safe for you is this." And she picked a red apple from a little tree and gave it to him. "This is an apple of truth," she said. "Whoever eats it will never be able to tell a lie again."

So Thomas ate the apple. And from then on he could only ever tell the truth, and that is why people called him True Thomas.

Then they came to a wonderful castle on a hillside. The Queen blew her hunting horn, and at once her grey dress fell away, and she was young and beautiful again, in her green silk. And Thomas's own rough clothes turned into a fine suit, with satin shoes.

The doors of the castle flew open, and the Fairy King came out to meet the Queen, with crowds of knights and ladies and musicians and pages. Thomas slipped into the castle among them, and nobody noticed him.

In the great hall, the fine lords and ladies were dancing, while huntsmen carried in deer from the hunt, and cooks prepared a feast. It was such a strange and wonderful sight that Thomas forgot all about time. He just stood and watched, and never said a word to anyone.

After three days, the Queen came over to him. "It's time to go, Thomas," she said, "if you ever want to see your home again."

"But you said seven years!" said Thomas. "I've only been here three days."

The Queen smiled. "Time passes quickly in Fairyland," she said. "It is seven years since we met. And I must take you home tonight, for your own sake. Every seventh year, an evil spirit comes from the land of darkness and carries away one of our people, and I'm afraid he might choose you."

So she took him home on her grey horse, back to the tree by the stream where they had met.

"Give me something," said Thomas, "so that people will know I have really been to Fairyland."

"I have already given you the gift of truth," said the Queen. "Now I give you the gift of poetry, so that you will be able to make wonderful verses, and the gift of prophecy, so that you will be able to see what is going to happen. And here is a harp from Fairyland. Goodbye, Thomas. Perhaps one day I will come back for you."

And she vanished.

Thomas lived for many years in his tower, and he became famous all over Scotland for his poems and for his prophecies, which always came true. People called him True Thomas, and Thomas the Rhymer. Long before it happened, he foretold the great battle of Bannockburn, when the little stream ran red. And he foretold that one day a son of a French queen would be king of all Britain. And it came true, hundreds of years later, when King James, the son of Mary, Queen of Scots, became king of both Scotland and England.

Fourteen years went by. Then, one night, the Scottish army was camped near Thomas's tower, and Thomas held a great feast for all the lords. And after the feast he took his fairy harp and sang to them, song after song, about the old days. They listened without a sound, because they felt they would never hear such music again.

That very night, a soldier on guard saw a snow-white stag and a snow-white doe walking slowly down the road past the camp in the moonlight, as if they were keeping time to music that nobody else could hear. The soldiers sent a page running to fetch Thomas, to ask him what it meant.

When Thomas heard, his face grew grave. "It is a message," he said softly, "from the Queen of Fairyland. I have waited a long time for it, and now it has come."

He went out, and walked straight up to the white stag and the white doe. They stopped for a moment, as if to greet him. Then the three of them walked slowly down the steep bank into the river Leader, and disappeared into the rushing water.

People searched and searched, but Thomas was never seen again. And to this day the country people believe that he went back to Fairyland with the white deer, to be with the Fairy Queen.
