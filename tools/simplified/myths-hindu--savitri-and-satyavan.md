---
title: Savitri and Satyavan
adaptedFrom: ganguli-mahabharata--savitri-and-satyavan
order: 28
namesNote: From the third book of the Mahabharata. Narada is a wandering sage who knows what is to come, and Yama is the god of death and the lord of justice.
---

Long ago, King Aswapati of the Madras had no children, until the goddess Savitri gave him a daughter. He named her Savitri, after the goddess.

When Savitri grew up, no one asked to marry her. So her father said to her, "Go out into the world, my daughter, and choose a husband for yourself." And Savitri set off with her father's counsellors, to travel through the forests and holy places.

One day, when she came home, she found her father talking with the wise sage Narada. "Where has your daughter been?" asked Narada. "And why isn't she married?"

"She has been looking for a husband," said the king. "Ask her whom she has chosen."

"In the forest," said Savitri, "there lives a king called Dyumatsena. He went blind, and an enemy took his kingdom, so he went to live in the forest with his wife and their baby son. That son has grown up in the forest, and his name is Satyavan. I have chosen him for my husband."

"Oh no!" said Narada. "Savitri has made a terrible mistake."

"Why?" asked the king. "Is Satyavan not good and brave and wise?"

"He is as bright as the sun, and as wise as the teacher of the gods," said Narada. "He is brave and generous and truthful and kind, and as handsome as the moon. When he was little, he loved horses, and made horses out of clay, and drew pictures of them."

"Then does he have any faults?" asked the king.

"Only one," said Narada, "but it outweighs all his goodness. In one year from today, Satyavan will die."

"Then go, Savitri," said the king, "and choose someone else."

"A person can only die once," said Savitri. "And a daughter can only be given in marriage once. I have chosen my husband, and I will not choose twice. Whether his life is long or short, he is my choice."

"Her heart is set," said Narada. "Nothing will change it. Let her marry Satyavan." And he rose up into the sky and went back to heaven.

So Savitri married Satyavan. She took off her jewels and dressed in the plain clothes of the forest, and went to live with him and his blind parents in their hermitage. She looked after them all lovingly, and everyone loved her. But she never forgot Narada's words, and she counted the days.

When there were only three days left before the day Satyavan was to die, Savitri began a fast. For three days and nights she ate nothing, and she grew as thin as a wooden doll. On the morning of the last day, she made her prayers, and bowed to the old people, and the holy men of the forest blessed her.

That day Satyavan picked up his axe to go into the forest to gather fruit and firewood.

"I'm coming with you," said Savitri. "I can't bear to be apart from you today."

"But you have never been into the forest before," said Satyavan, "and you are weak from fasting."

"I'm not tired," said Savitri. "Please don't stop me." So Savitri asked his parents, and they let her go.

Savitri walked beside her husband through the beautiful woods, full of peacocks. "Look at the rivers, and the trees in flower!" said Satyavan. And Savitri smiled at him. But her heart was breaking, and she watched him every moment.

Satyavan picked fruit, and filled his bag. Then he began to chop wood, and as he worked, his head began to ache. "Savitri," he said, "my head hurts terribly, and I feel ill. I must lie down and sleep."

So Savitri sat on the ground, and laid his head in her lap. And then she saw a man standing beside them. He was dressed in red, with a crown on his head, and he shone like the sun. His eyes were red, and he carried a noose in his hand, and he was staring at Satyavan.

Savitri gently laid her husband's head on the ground, and stood up, trembling. "You must be a god," she said. "Who are you, and what do you want?"

"I am Yama, the god of death," he said. "Satyavan's days are over, and I have come to take him away. Usually I send my messengers, but Satyavan is so good that I came myself." And Yama drew Satyavan's soul out of his body, a tiny figure no bigger than a thumb, and tied it with his noose, and set off towards the south.

Savitri followed him.

"Go back, Savitri," said Yama. "You have come as far as you can."

"Wherever my husband goes, I will go," said Savitri. And as she walked, she spoke to Yama about goodness and wisdom.

Yama was pleased with her words. "Ask me for a gift," he said, "anything except your husband's life."

"Let my husband's father see again," said Savitri, "and be strong."

"So be it," said Yama. "Now go back. You must be tired."

"How can I be tired when I am with my husband?" said Savitri. "And it is always good to be in the company of the good." And she went on following him.

"Your words are wise," said Yama. "Ask for a second gift, except your husband's life."

"Let my husband's father win back his kingdom," said Savitri.

"So be it," said Yama. "Now go back."

But Savitri followed him still, and spoke to him of kindness, and of how the good show mercy even to their enemies.

"Your words are like water to the thirsty," said Yama. "Ask for a third gift, except your husband's life."

"My father has no sons," said Savitri. "Let him have a hundred sons."

"So be it," said Yama. "Now go back. You have come far enough."

"When I am with my husband, the way does not seem long," said Savitri. And she spoke to Yama of how everyone trusts the righteous.

"I have never heard such words from anyone but you," said Yama. "Ask for a fourth gift, except your husband's life."

"Let Satyavan and me have a hundred strong and brave sons," said Savitri.

"So be it," said Yama. "Now go back, Savitri. You have come too far already."

But Savitri went on following him, and spoke of how the good do good to others without asking for anything in return.

"The more you speak, the more I honour you," said Yama. "Ask me for anything you like."

"Then I ask for this," said Savitri. "You have promised me a hundred sons with Satyavan. But how can that come true, if you take my husband away? Without my husband, I want no happiness, and no riches, not even heaven. So let Satyavan live again, and then your words will come true."

"So be it," said Yama, and he untied his noose, and smiled. "Your husband is free," he said. "He will be well again, and he will live with you for four hundred years. You will have your hundred sons, and your father will have his hundred sons too." And Yama went away to his own home.

Then Savitri went back to the place where Satyavan lay, and sat down, and laid his head in her lap again. And Satyavan opened his eyes and looked at her, like someone who has come home from a long journey.

"I have slept for a long time," he said. "Why didn't you wake me? And where is that dark person who was dragging me away?"

"You have slept a long time on my lap," said Savitri. "Yama has gone. Get up if you can. Look, it's night."

It was dark in the forest now, and the jackals were howling. But Satyavan's headache was gone, and he wanted to go home to his parents, who would be so worried about him. So Savitri hung his bag of fruit on a tree, and picked up his axe, and put his arm over her shoulder, and helped him along the path through the moonlit forest.

When they reached the hermitage, late that night, they found that Satyavan's father could see again. And Savitri told the holy men everything that had happened, and all the gifts she had won from Yama.

The next morning, messengers came from Dyumatsena's old kingdom. "The enemy who took your kingdom is dead," they said, "and your people want you back as their king, whether you can see or not. Here is your chariot, and your army. Come home!" And they were amazed to see that the old king could see again.

So Dyumatsena went home and became king again, with Satyavan as his prince. And in time, Savitri and Satyavan had a hundred sons, and Savitri's father had a hundred sons too. And so Savitri, by her love and her wisdom, brought happiness to everyone she loved.
