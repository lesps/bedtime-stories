---
title: The Binding of Fenrir
adaptedFrom: blackwell-prose-edda--the-binding-of-the-wolf-fenrir

namesNote: Blackwell spells Fenrir “Fenir” in places, and calls Odin “All-father.”
---

Loki, the trickster god, had three very strange children with a giantess in the land of the giants. The first was a wolf, called Fenrir. The second was a serpent. The third was a girl named Hel, who was half alive-looking and half dead-looking.

The gods learned from their prophecies that these three would bring great trouble one day. So Odin, the father of the gods, sent for them.

He threw the serpent into the sea that surrounds the world. And there it grew and grew, until it was so long that it reached all the way around the world and could bite its own tail. It became the Midgard Serpent.

He sent Hel down to the cold, dark world beneath the earth. There she became queen of the land of the dead.

But the wolf, Fenrir, the gods kept with them in Asgard, to keep an eye on him. And he grew, and grew, and grew. He grew so big and so fierce that only one god dared to go near him to give him his food. That was Tyr, the bravest of all the gods.

The prophecies said that one day Fenrir would be the death of Odin himself. So the gods decided they must tie him up.

They made a strong iron chain, called Laeding, and brought it to him. "Fenrir," they said, "let's see how strong you are. Can you break this?"

Fenrir looked at the chain and thought it would be easy. He let them wrap it around him. Then he stretched, and snap! the chain broke.

So the gods made another chain, twice as strong, called Dromi. "If you can break this one," they said, "you'll be famous for your strength."

Fenrir saw that this chain was much stronger. But he had grown stronger too, and he wanted to be famous. So he let them put it on. Then he shook himself, and stretched, and rolled on the ground, and crash! the chain burst into pieces that flew in every direction.

Now the gods were worried. No chain in the world could hold that wolf.

So Odin sent a messenger down to the dwarfs, who were the best craftsmen in all the worlds. And the dwarfs made a magic rope called Gleipnir. They made it out of six things:

the sound of a cat's footsteps,
the beard of a woman,
the roots of a mountain,
the sinews of a bear,
the breath of a fish,
and the spit of a bird.

You may never have heard of these things before. But you can see for yourself that they are true. Have you ever heard a cat's footsteps? Or seen a woman with a beard, or a mountain with roots? No! Because the dwarfs used them all up to make Gleipnir.

The rope was as smooth and soft as a ribbon of silk. But it was the strongest thing ever made.

The gods took Fenrir to a lonely island in the middle of a lake. They showed him the silky ribbon. "Try to break this," they said. They passed it around and pretended to try to break it themselves. "We can't do it!" they said. "Only you are strong enough."

Fenrir looked at the thin ribbon. "There's no glory in breaking a thin thing like that," he said. "But if there's some trick or magic in it, then it isn't coming anywhere near my legs."

"Don't be silly," said the gods. "You broke those big iron chains. You can break a silk ribbon. And if you can't, well, then you're too weak to frighten us, and we'll let you go."

"I'm afraid," said the wolf, "that if you tie me up so I can't get free, you'll be in no hurry to untie me. I don't want this ribbon on me. But I don't want you to think I'm a coward either. I'll let you tie me, if one of you puts his hand in my mouth, as a promise that there's no trick."

The gods looked at each other. Nobody wanted to put a hand in the wolf's mouth. Then Tyr stepped forward, brave as always, and put his right hand between the wolf's great jaws.

So the gods tied Fenrir up with the silky ribbon. Fenrir stretched and strained with all his might. But the harder he pulled, the tighter the ribbon held him. He could not break it. And all the gods laughed with relief, except for Tyr. Because when the wolf knew he had been tricked, he bit down, and Tyr lost his hand.

The gods fastened the ribbon deep down in the earth, under two great rocks. Fenrir howled and snapped at them, trying to bite. So they wedged a sword in his mouth, to hold his jaws open. And there he lies, howling, with the drool running from his mouth so fast that it makes a whole river.

"Why didn't the gods just get rid of him for good?" you might ask. Because the island was a holy place of peace, and the gods would not break the peace there. So Fenrir is still tied up on his island. And there he will stay until the end of the world.

But everyone remembers Tyr, the god who gave his hand to keep the world safe. He was the bravest of them all.
