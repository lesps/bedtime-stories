The goddess Leto had twin babies, a boy and a girl. The boy was Apollo, who would one day be the god of the sun. The girl was Artemis, who would one day be the goddess of the moon.

But Hera, the queen of the gods, was jealous of Leto, and she made sure Leto had nowhere to rest. Every island was too afraid of Hera to let Leto stay. At last a little floating island called Delos said yes. Zeus tied the island to the bottom of the sea with strong chains, so it would never float away, and Leto's twins were born there.

Even then, Hera would not leave her alone. Leto had to travel on, carrying her two babies in her arms.

One day she came to the land of Lycia. The sun was blazing. Leto was hot and tired, and the babies had drunk all her milk. Then, at the bottom of a valley, she saw a little pond of clear, cool water. Some country people were there, cutting reeds and rushes.

Leto knelt by the edge to drink. But the people shouted at her. "Go away! You can't drink here!"

"Why not?" said Leto gently. "Water belongs to everyone, like the sunshine and the air. I'm not asking to bathe. I only want a drink. My mouth is so dry I can hardly speak. Please, one sip of water would save my life. And look at my babies. They are thirsty too."

As she spoke, the babies held out their little arms.

Anyone would have felt sorry for them. But these people did not. They laughed at her and called her names. They told her to go away or they would hurt her. Then they waded into the pond and stomped around in it, stirring up the mud, until the water was so dirty that no one could drink it.

Leto was so angry that she forgot she was thirsty. She lifted her hands to the sky.

"Since you love this pond so much," she said, "you can stay in it forever!"

And that is just what happened. The people shrank and shrank. Their necks disappeared. Their backs turned green. Their bellies turned white. Their mouths grew wide, and their eyes bulged out of their heads. They had all turned into frogs.

They still live in ponds today. Sometimes they dive down into the mud. Sometimes they sit on the bank and then jump back in with a splash. And they still have big, loud, rude voices. Listen near a pond on a summer night, and you can hear them, still arguing with each other: "Croak! Croak! Croak!"
