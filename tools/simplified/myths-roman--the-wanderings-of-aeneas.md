After Troy fell, Aeneas and the Trojans who had escaped with him hid in the forests of Mount Ida. All through the winter they cut down trees and built ships. When summer came, old Anchises said, "It is time." So they said goodbye to the land where they had been born, and sailed away to look for a new home.

Their first stop was Thrace, across the sea. Aeneas began to build a city there. But one day, as he pulled up a little green bush to decorate an altar, drops of dark red blood dripped from its roots. Aeneas was frightened. He pulled up another, and it bled too. When he pulled up the third, a sad voice came out of the ground.

"Aeneas, why are you hurting me? I am Polydorus, a prince of Troy. A wicked king lives in this land. He loves gold more than anything, and he did me great wrong. Leave this cruel country!"

So the Trojans held a funeral for poor Polydorus, so that his spirit could rest. Then they sailed away.

Next they came to the holy island of Delos, where the god Apollo was born. Aeneas prayed in Apollo's temple: "Where should we go? Where will we find our home?"

The ground shook, and a voice came out of the temple. "Seek your ancient mother. The land where your people first came from will welcome you back. There the children of Aeneas will rule over the whole world."

"Our ancient mother?" said old Anchises. "Our people first came from the island of Crete. That must be it!"

So they sailed to Crete and began to build a city there. But soon a terrible sickness came, and their crops dried up in the fields. One night the little statues of the gods that Aeneas had carried out of Troy spoke to him in a dream, shining in the moonlight.

"You have made a mistake," they said. "Crete is not the place. There is a land far to the west called Italy. That is where your great-great-grandfather Dardanus came from long ago. That is your ancient mother. Go there."

So the Trojans set out again. But a storm caught them. For three days and nights they saw neither sun nor stars. On the fourth day they landed on an island where herds of cattle were grazing with no one looking after them. The hungry Trojans cooked a feast.

But this was the island of the Harpies. Harpies are horrible creatures, with the faces of women, the wings of birds and long, sharp claws, and they are always hungry. Down they swooped with a rush of wings, snatched the food and made a filthy mess of everything. The Trojans moved to another spot, but the Harpies came again. So Aeneas told his men to draw their swords. When the Harpies swooped down a third time, the men slashed at them. But it was like fighting the wind. Their swords could not even touch them.

The Harpies flew off, all except one, named Celaeno, who perched on a rock and screeched at them. "You stole our cattle, and now you attack us? Listen to my prophecy, then. You will reach Italy. But you will not build your city until you are so hungry that you eat your own tables!"

Then she flew away. The Trojans did not like the sound of that at all.

They sailed on, until they came to a city that looked just like a little copy of Troy. There they found two old friends. Helenus, a son of King Priam, was king there now, and with him was Andromache, who had been the wife of the great hero Hector. They had built a little Troy, with a stream that they named after a river of Troy, and a gate they called the Trojan Gate, so that they would always remember home. They made Aeneas welcome, and gave him a great feast.

Helenus was a prophet, and before Aeneas left, he told him what was coming.

"Italy is still far away," said Helenus. "Here is how you will know when you have found the place for your city. You will see a huge white pig lying on a riverbank, with thirty white piglets. And don't worry about eating your tables. The gods will find a way. Do not sail between the two sea monsters, Scylla and Charybdis. Go the long way round the island of Sicily instead. And when you reach Italy, find the Sibyl of Cumae, the wise woman who writes the future on leaves. She will tell you everything."

Then he loaded Aeneas's ships with presents of gold and silver, and they said a sad goodbye.

At last the Trojans saw Italy, low and blue on the horizon. "Italy!" cried old Anchises, and everyone cheered. But this was not yet the place. They landed only long enough to pray to Minerva and to Juno, as Helenus had told them. Then they sailed on around Sicily, the long way.

One night they anchored near the great volcano Etna, which rumbled and roared and spat out smoke and fire. In the morning, a strange, ragged man came running out of the woods, thin and dirty, with his clothes held together with thorns.

"Save me!" he cried. "I am a Greek. I sailed with Odysseus. When my friends escaped from the Cyclops, they left me behind by mistake. I have been hiding in these woods for three months, eating berries and roots. This is the land of the Cyclopes, giants with one eye who eat people! Take me with you, I beg you!"

Just then they saw an enormous shape coming down the hillside among his sheep. It was the Cyclops Polyphemus, the one Odysseus had blinded, walking with a pine tree for a stick. He waded out into the sea to wash his hurt eye, and even far out, the water did not come up to his waist.

The Trojans took the Greek on board and rowed away as quietly as they could. But Polyphemus heard the oars. He roared, and all the other Cyclopes came running down to the shore, as tall as a forest of oak trees. Luckily, a north wind blew up and carried the ships safely away.

They sailed on around Sicily. And there, at a place called Drepanum, old Anchises died. Aeneas buried his dear father and wept for him.

Then, at last, they set off for Italy. But up in the sky, Juno, the queen of the gods, saw them, and she was furious. She hated the Trojans, and she did not want them ever to reach Italy. She flew to Aeolus, the king of the winds, who kept all the winds locked up inside a mountain.

"Let your winds loose," said Juno, "and sink those ships!"

Aeolus struck the mountain with his spear, and out rushed all the winds at once. Black clouds covered the sky. The thunder crashed. Huge waves picked up the ships and threw them about. One ship was sunk, and others were smashed against the rocks or stuck in the sand.

But down at the bottom of the sea, Neptune, the god of the sea, felt the storm. He lifted his head above the waves and saw the Trojan ships being tossed about.

"Winds!" he roared. "Who said you could stir up my sea? Go home, and tell your king that the sea belongs to me, not him!"

The winds fled. Neptune calmed the waves, and pushed the clouds away, and brought back the sun. Then he lifted the stuck ships off the rocks with his three-pronged spear, and drove away across the smooth sea in his chariot.

Aeneas and his tired men steered for the nearest land. It was the coast of Africa. There they found a quiet bay with a cave and a spring of fresh water. Only seven of their ships were left. Aeneas climbed a rock and shot seven fine deer, one for each ship, and they cooked a feast on the beach.

"Cheer up, my friends," said Aeneas, though his own heart was heavy. "We have been through worse than this. One day perhaps it will even be fun to remember."

And they ate, and talked about their missing friends, and wondered if they would ever see them again.
