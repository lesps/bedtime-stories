When Romulus, the first king of Rome, was taken up into the sky, the Romans needed a new king.

In those days there was a man named Numa, who lived among the Sabines in the hills. Everyone knew him as the fairest and most honest man in the land, and the one who cared most about the gods. So the Romans asked him to be their king.

Numa said yes, but only if the gods agreed. So a priest called an augur, who knew how to read signs from the gods, led him up to the top of the hill. Numa sat on a stone, facing south. The augur covered his head, and took a curved staff in his left hand, and pointed out a part of the sky. Then he laid his right hand on Numa's head and prayed: "Father Jupiter, if you want this man, Numa, to be king of Rome, send us a clear sign in the part of the sky I have marked."

The sign came. And so Numa became the second king of Rome.

Now, Rome had been built by fighting. Romulus had been a warrior king, and the Romans were a rough, fierce people who were always ready for a fight. Numa wanted to build Rome all over again, this time with fairness and laws, and with respect for the gods. And he wanted the Romans to learn to live in peace.

So Numa built a temple to Janus, the god of doors, with two great gates. "When Rome is at war," he said, "these gates will stand open. When Rome is at peace, they will be closed." Then he made peace with all the neighbors, and he closed the gates. After Numa's time, in all the long years of Rome's history, they were closed only twice more.

Numa did many other things for his people. He divided the year into twelve months, by the moon, and added an extra month every so often, to keep the year in step with the sun. He decided which days were for work and which were holy days, when no business could be done.

He chose priests to look after the gods: a special priest for Jupiter, with splendid robes and an ivory chair, and priests for Mars, and for Romulus, who was now a god himself. He chose the Vestal Virgins, the priestesses who looked after the sacred fire of Vesta, which must never go out.

Then one day, the story goes, a shield fell down from the sky. It was a gift from the gods, and it had to be kept safe. But what if someone stole it? Numa had a clever idea. He had eleven more shields made, exactly like the one from heaven. Now there were twelve shields, and no thief could ever tell which was the real one! He chose twelve priests, called the Salii, to look after the shields. They carried them through the city, wearing bright tunics and bronze breastplates, leaping and dancing and singing hymns.

Where did Numa get all his wise ideas? He told the people that they did not come from him at all. He said they came from a goddess, a nymph named Egeria. He used to go to meet her in a quiet grove of trees just outside the city, and there, he said, she taught him all the laws and customs that the gods wanted the Romans to keep.

Because they believed the laws came from the gods, the Romans obeyed them with great respect.

Numa ruled Rome for forty-three years. And the fierce people of Rome learned to be fair and good, and to honor the gods.
