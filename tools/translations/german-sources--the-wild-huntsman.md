Note: All over Germany people told of the Wild Hunt: a ghostly hunter who rides through the sky on stormy nights with his hounds, and who must never be greeted or watched. Some said he was the old god Wodan, some a cruel lord named Hackelberg who loved hunting more than heaven. The poet Gottfried August Bürger made the legend into this ballad, which Walter Scott translated as "The Wild Huntsman". A Wildgrave and Rhinegrave was a German count, a lord of the forests and of the Rhine.

## Gottfried August Bürger, "The Wild Huntsman"

> The Wildgrave and Rhinegrave blew his horn:
> "Halloo, halloo, on foot and horse!"
> His stallion reared up neighing;
> with a great clatter his followers rushed after him;
> loud yelped and bayed the hounds, loosed from the leash,
> through corn and thorn, through heath and stubble.

> The dome of the high cathedral
> shone in the light of Sunday morning.
> Deep and clear, the solemn ringing of the bells
> called the people to High Mass.
> Far off, sweetly, came the hymns
> of the faithful Christian crowd.

> Pell-mell they crossed the crossroads,
> with "Horrido!" and "Hussasa!"
> Look there! Look there! Came, right and left,
> a rider here, a rider there!
> The right one's horse was shining silver;
> a fire-colored horse carried the left.

> Who were the riders, left and right?
> I can guess, but I do not know.
> Bright as light the rider on the right appeared,
> with a gentle face like spring.
> Grim and sallow, the knight on the left
> shot lightning from his eye like a storm.

> "Welcome here, and just in time,
> welcome to the noble hunt!
> On earth or in heaven there is
> no sport that pleases more."
> So he cried, and slapped his thigh,
> and swung his hat up in the air.

> "Your horn's sound goes badly,"
> said the one on the right, gently,
> "with the Sunday bells and the choir.
> Turn back! You will hunt nothing good today.
> Let your good angel warn you,
> and do not let the evil one ensnare you!"

> "Hunt on, hunt on, my noble lord!"
> the knight on the left broke in quickly.
> "What bells? What bawling of choirs?
> The joy of the hunt will please you far better!
> Let me teach you what is fit for a prince,
> and do not let that one fool you!"

> "Yes! Well spoken, man on the left!
> You are a hero after my own heart.
> Whoever cannot love the hunt,
> let him go off to his prayers!
> It may vex you, you pious fool,
> but I will have my pleasure all the same!"

> And hurry, hurry, on they went,
> in and out of the fields, down hill and up.
> Still the riders rode, right and left,
> beside him on either side.
> Far off a white stag leaped up,
> with sixteen points on its antlers.

> And louder the count blew his horn,
> and faster they flew, on foot and horse;
> and look! now behind and now in front,
> one of his followers fell down dead.
> "Let them fall! Let them fall to hell!
> That must not spoil a prince's pleasure."

> The stag crouches in a field of corn
> and hopes to find a safe place there.
> Look there! A poor farmer
> comes forward, pitiful to see.
> "Mercy, dear lord, mercy!
> Spare the poor man's bitter sweat!"

> The knight on the right gallops up
> and warns the count, gently and kindly.
> But the man on the left urges him all the more
> to cruel, gloating mischief.
> The count scorns the warning of the right
> and lets the left one ensnare him.

> "Away, you dog!" the count snorts
> terribly at the poor ploughman,
> "or, by the devil, I will hunt you myself!
> Halloo, comrades, at him, at him!
> To show that I have sworn in earnest,
> crack your whips about his ears!"

> No sooner said than done! The Wildgrave leaped
> over the hedge ahead of them all,
> and after him, with crack and clang,
> came the train of hound and horse and man;
> and hound and man and horse trampled
> the corn until the field steamed.

> Startled up by the noise nearby,
> in and out of the fields, down hill and up,
> chased and pursued but never caught,
> the stag reaches the open meadow
> and slyly mingles with the tame herds,
> so as to be spared.

> But here and there, through field and wood,
> and there and here, through wood and field,
> the swift hounds follow and soon scent
> its trail.
> The herdsman, full of fear for his herd,
> throws himself on the ground before the count.

> "Mercy, lord, mercy! Leave
> my poor quiet cattle in peace!
> Think, dear lord, here grazes
> many a poor widow's cow.
> Spare the poor their one and only!
> Mercy, dear lord, mercy!"

> The knight on the right gallops up
> and warns the count, gently and kindly.
> But the man on the left urges him all the more
> to cruel, gloating mischief.
> The count scorns the warning of the right
> and lets the left one ensnare him.

> "You bold dog, to stand in my way!
> Ha, if only you were grown fast
> to your best cow,
> and every old woman with you!
> It would delight my heart
> to hunt you all straight into heaven.

> Halloo, comrades, at them, at them!
> Yo! Doho! Hussasa!"
> And every hound attacked in fury
> whatever it saw before it.
> Dripping with blood the herdsman sank to the ground,
> and, dripping with blood, his herd, head by head.

> From the murderous tumult the stag
> barely escapes, running ever more weakly.
> Spattered with blood and covered with foam,
> it is taken in by the night of the forest.
> Deep in the heart of the forest it hides,
> in a hermit's chapel.

> On without rest, with crack of whip,
> with "Horrido!" and "Hussasa!",
> and yelp and bay and blare of horns,
> the wild swarm follows it there too.
> The pious hermit steps out of his hut
> to meet them with a gentle plea.

> "Leave off, leave off this trail!
> Do not profane God's sanctuary!
> The creature groans to heaven
> and asks God to judge you.
> For the last time, be warned,
> or ruin will ensnare you!"

> The rider on the right gallops up in alarm
> and warns the count, gently and kindly.
> But the man on the left urges him all the more
> to cruel, gloating mischief.
> And woe! For all the warning of the right,
> he lets the left one ensnare him!

> "Ruin here, ruin there!
> That," he cries, "scares me very little.
> And if it reached the third heaven,
> I would not care a bat for it.
> It may vex God and you, you fool,
> but I will have my pleasure all the same!"

> He swings his whip and blows his horn:
> "Halloo, comrades, at it, at it!"
> Whoosh! Hermit and hut vanish in front of him,
> and behind him horse and man vanish;
> and crack and clang and the roar of the hunt
> are swallowed at once by a deathly silence.

> Terrified, the count looks round;
> he blows his horn, and it makes no sound;
> he shouts, and cannot hear himself;
> his whip swings without a whistle;
> he digs his spurs into his horse
> and can ride neither forward nor back.

> Then it grows dark around him,
> and darker still, like a grave.
> There is a dull roaring like a faraway sea.
> From high above his head
> a voice of thunder, as fierce as a storm,
> calls out this terrible judgment:

> "You tyrant, devilish by nature,
> cruel to God and man and beast!
> The cries and grief of the creatures,
> and your crimes against them,
> have called you loudly to the judgment seat,
> where the torch of vengeance blazes high.

> Flee, monster, flee, and from now on,
> from now until the end of time,
> be hunted by hell and the devil himself,
> a terror to the princes of every age
> who, to serve their wicked pleasure,
> spare neither the Creator nor His creatures!"

> A sulphur-yellow flash of lightning
> runs round the leaves of the forest.
> Fear trickles through his marrow and bones;
> he feels so stifled, so dull and deaf!
> Cold horror blows against him,
> and a roaring storm follows at his neck.

> The horror blows, the storm roars,
> and up out of the earth, whoo!
> comes a huge black fist;
> it opens wide, it clenches like a claw;
> whoo! it reaches to seize him by the head;
> whoo! his face is twisted round to his back.

> It flickers and flames all round him,
> with green and blue and red glow;
> a sea of fire surges round him,
> swarming with the brood of hell.
> Suddenly a thousand hounds of hell
> rush up from the pit, loudly urged on.

> He tears himself away through wood and field
> and flees, howling with grief and woe;
> but through the whole wide world
> hell rushes barking after him,
> by day deep through the clefts of the earth,
> at midnight high through the air.

> His face stays twisted to his back,
> however fast his flight pulls him on.
> He must watch the monsters,
> loudly urged on by the evil spirit;
> he must watch the gnashing and the gaping
> of the jaws that snap at him.

> This is the hunt of the Wild Host,
> which will last until the Day of Judgment,
> and often still rushes past a wicked man
> at night, to his terror and dread.
> Many a huntsman could tell of it,
> if he did not have to keep silent.
