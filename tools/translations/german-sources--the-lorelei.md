Note: The Lorelei is not an old folk tale. The poet Clemens Brentano made her up for a ballad in his novel Godwi (1801), naming her after the Lorelei, a tall rock above a narrow, dangerous bend of the Rhine where many boats were wrecked. About twenty years later Heinrich Heine wrote the poem that made her famous, and people began to tell her story as if it were a very old legend.

## Clemens Brentano, "At Bacharach on the Rhine" (1801)

> At Bacharach on the Rhine
> there lived a sorceress;
> she was so fair and lovely
> and carried many hearts away.

> She brought to ruin many
> of the men for miles around;
> from the bonds of love she wove
> there was no escape again.

> The bishop had her summoned
> before the court of the church,
> and had to grant her mercy,
> so lovely was her form.

> He spoke to her, much moved:
> "You poor Lore Lay!
> Who was it led you astray
> into wicked sorcery?"

> "Lord Bishop, let me die;
> I am weary of my life,
> for every man must come to ruin
> who looks into my eyes.

> My eyes are two flames,
> my arm a magic staff;
> oh, lay me in the flames!
> Oh, break the staff on me!"

Note: In old German courts, a judge broke a staff in two when he sentenced someone to death.

> "I cannot condemn you
> until you first confess to me
> why in these same flames
> my own heart burns already.

> I cannot break the staff,
> you lovely Lore Lay!
> For then I would have to break
> my own heart in two."

> "Lord Bishop, do not mock
> poor me so cruelly,
> but pray instead for mercy
> for me from our dear God.

> I may not live any longer,
> I love no one any more;
> you are to give me death,
> and that is why I came to you.

> My sweetheart has betrayed me,
> has turned away from me,
> has gone away from here,
> away to a foreign land.

> Eyes gentle and wild,
> cheeks red and white,
> words quiet and mild:
> that is my magic circle.

> I myself must perish in it;
> my heart aches so;
> I could die of the pain
> when I see my own face.

> So let me have my justice,
> let me die as a Christian;
> for everything must vanish,
> because he is not with me."

> He sends for three knights:
> "Take her to the convent.
> Go, Lore! To God I commend
> your bewitched mind.

> You shall become a little nun,
> a little nun in black and white;
> prepare yourself on earth
> for your journey into death."

> So to the convent they rode,
> the knights, all three,
> and sad, there in the middle,
> the lovely Lore Lay.

> "O knights, let me climb
> up onto this great rock;
> I want to look once more
> toward my beloved's castle.

> I want to look once more
> down into the deep Rhine,
> and then go to the convent
> and be a maiden of God."

> The rock is so sheer,
> so steep is its face,
> yet she climbs up and up
> until she stood on top.

> The three knights tie
> their horses down below
> and climb on and on,
> up onto the rock as well.

> The maiden said: "There goes
> a little boat upon the Rhine;
> the one who stands in the little boat,
> he must be my beloved.

> My heart grows so glad,
> he must be my beloved!"
> Then she leans far over
> and falls into the Rhine.

> The knights had to die;
> they could not get down;
> they all had to perish,
> with no priest and no grave.

> Who sang this song?
> A boatman on the Rhine,
> and always it has echoed
> from the Three Knights' Rock:

> Lore Lay!
> Lore Lay!
> Lore Lay!

## Heinrich Heine, "The Lorelei" (1824)

> I do not know what it means
> that I am so sad;
> a tale from olden times
> will not leave my mind.

> The air is cool and darkening,
> and quietly flows the Rhine;
> the peak of the mountain sparkles
> in the evening sun.

> The fairest of maidens is sitting
> up there, wonderful to see;
> her golden jewels are glittering,
> she combs her golden hair.

> She combs it with a golden comb
> and sings a song as she combs,
> a song that has a wondrous,
> mighty melody.

> The boatman in his little boat
> is seized with a wild ache;
> he does not see the rocky reefs,
> he only looks up to the heights.

> I think the waves will swallow
> the boatman and his boat at last;
> and that, with her singing,
> is what the Lorelei has done.
