title: The Legend of the Wondrous Stag
order: 1
workId: hungarian-wondrous-stag
sources: the Hungarian of János Arany's "Rege a csodaszarvasról," the sixth canto of his epic poem Buda halála (The Death of Buda, 1864), as published by Project Gutenberg (eBook #41699)

Note: The old Hungarian chronicles told how the Huns and the Magyars came from two brothers, Hunor and Magor, who followed a wondrous deer into a new land. In 1864 the poet János Arany retold the legend as a song that a minstrel sings at King Attila's feast in his epic poem The Death of Buda. Arany calls the brothers Hunor and Magyar, and their father Ménrót (Nimrod). The river Kur is the Kura, in the Caucasus; the Maeotian sea is the Sea of Azov; and the Scythian land is the great plain north of the Black Sea.

> The bird flies on from branch to branch,
> the song flies on from mouth to mouth;
> the grass grows green on the old grave-mound,
> and on the minstrel's lute the hero wakes.

> Out to hunt the game there came
> the hero sons of fair Enéh:
> Hunor and Magyar, two fine youths,
> two true brothers, sons of Ménrót.

> Fifty good young warriors each
> they chose to ride along with them;
> as if they rode to bloody war,
> they armed themselves for nimble game.

> The game before them lies in blood,
> no roe or deer escapes from them;
> they have felled the roebuck already,
> and now they chase the great red stag.

> After the stag they press on and on
> along the bare shore of the salt sea,
> where the wolf and where the bear
> have never gone, and would be lost.

> But the panther and the savage lion
> roar across the great waste land;
> the yellow tiger whelps there,
> and eats her cubs when she is hungry.

> The bird flies on, the song flies on
> of the two sons of fair Enéh;
> the singing bird from branch to branch,
> the singing song from mouth to mouth.

> And now the sun, as it went down,
> had lit a fire among the clouds;
> still they chase the stag and chase it,
> and at dusk, behold, it vanishes.

> By nightfall they had come
> to the bank of the river Kur;
> beside the running water
> their horses could graze well.

> Said Hunor: "Here let us dismount,
> water the horses, and spend the night."
> Said Magyar: "At the break of day
> let us turn back with all our band."

> "Ho, warriors! Ho, young men!
> What land is this, what country here,
> where the sun goes down into the east,
> and not, as elsewhere, in the west?"

> One champion said: "As I see it,
> the sun went down there in the south."
> Another said: "I would not think so:
> it glows red on the northern peaks."

> On the river bank they dismounted,
> watered the horses, and spent the night,
> meaning in the morning, at break of day,
> to turn for home with all their band.

> A breeze springs up in the cool dawn,
> the edge of the sky turns crimson red;
> and there, as bold as bold can be,
> the stag is leaping, across the water.

> The bird flies on, the song flies on
> of the two sons of fair Enéh;
> the singing bird from branch to branch,
> the singing song from mouth to mouth.

> "Up and at it, my swift lads!
> Let us catch that stag at last."
> And, whether they will it or no,
> they chase it again without a pause.

> They swam across the river Kur;
> the wastes beyond are wilder still;
> there a man can find no blade of grass,
> and not a single drop of water.

> The back of the earth is crumbling there,
> and sweats out salt on its bare ridges;
> the water of the springs is not fit to drink,
> and the lake breathes out a stink of sulphur.

> The springs bubble up with oil;
> here and there the oil is burning,
> and like many watch-fires in the dark night
> the flames flare up on every side.

> Every evening they are sorry, sorely sorry,
> that they ever wanted this quarry,
> and why they keep on chasing it
> into a sad wilderness that leads them astray.

> And yet, and yet, when morning came,
> they had to chase the stag again,
> as the wind chases a thistle in play,
> as its shadow chases a bird.

> The bird flies on, the song flies on
> of the two sons of fair Enéh;
> the singing bird from branch to branch,
> the singing song from mouth to mouth.

> They scour the wilds, they cross the Don,
> as far as the little Maeotian sea;
> they push their way into an island
> among deep and boggy lakes.

> There the stag, like a breath of vapour,
> mist before it, mist behind,
> before a man could look around,
> vanished from before their eyes.

> "Hoha! Hoha! Where's the game?"
> One shouts: "There it is, running!"
> Another shouts: "It's here, right here!"
> The third shouts: "It is nowhere!"

> They search through every nook and corner,
> they spear their way through every bush;
> a lizard stirs, a grouse flies up,
> but the stag is not among them.

> Said Magyar: "Oh, who knows
> which way the road to our home lies?
> The sky is round on every side.
> Mother, mother, it will be the death of you!"

> Said Hunor: "Let us stay here!
> Let us make our camp; we are at home.
> The grass is silk, the water sweet,
> and honey drips from the hollow trees;

> "the blue river gives shining fish,
> the red deer gives a tasty morsel;
> the bow is taut, the arrow swift,
> and in war's adventures, plunder is the prize."

> The bird flies on, the song flies on
> of the two sons of fair Enéh;
> the singing bird from branch to branch,
> the singing song from mouth to mouth.

> When they grew tired of sitting at home,
> of luring fish and chasing roe,
> they rode down onto the plain
> for new adventures, finer fights.

> On the empty land, on the level heath,
> music sounds in the dark night,
> music, pipe and drum, deep in the wilds,
> as if from heaven, as in a dream.

> Fairy maidens live there;
> they dance and make merry;
> they have a tent woven of mist,
> and in their tent they make merry.

> Not one man is near them;
> but the fairest of the earthly maidens,
> the daughters of Belár and of Dúl,
> are learning fairy-craft there.

> King Dúl's are two, the fairest;
> old Belár's are twelve;
> and in all a hundred and two
> are beginning to become fairies.

> A hard test: to slay a man,
> to bewitch nine young men,
> to lure them on to love,
> and to stay untouched by love.

> So they learn the fairy-craft,
> a heart-breaking art;
> every night they show what they have learned,
> and every night they make merry so.

> The bird flies on, the song flies on
> of the two sons of fair Enéh;
> the singing bird from branch to branch,
> the singing song from mouth to mouth.

> Following the sound, against the wind,
> following the light, hidden by the night,
> they go warily, they go in shadow:
> whoever would catch a moth must creep.

> Said Magyar: "This piping, brother,
> thrills me through and through."
> Said Hunor: "My blood is stirred
> by the shadows of the maidens turning."

> "Ho, warriors! Ho, at them!
> Each of you take one up before him!
> Let us carry home our wives,
> and let the high wind blow away our tracks."

> They set spurs to their horses,
> they let loose the reins;
> the maidens are inside the ring,
> all within the ring, and many in their arms.

> At this a great shrieking rises;
> the host of maidens tries to scatter;
> fire in front of them, water behind,
> every way they fall into men's hands.

> The fairy maidens vanished there;
> they had wings, and flew away;
> but where are the others to go?
> Hide in the earth? Sink out of sight?

> Now nothing will come of that,
> of becoming fairies in maiden pride;
> the horses gallop, and on the plain
> the great empty night is silent then.

> The bird flies on, the song flies on
> of the two sons of fair Enéh;
> the singing bird from branch to branch,
> the singing song from mouth to mouth.

> The daughters of Dúl, the fairest,
> became the wives of Hunor and Magyar;
> and the young warriors, a hundred exactly,
> shared out all the hundred others.

> In time the proud maidens there
> made their peace, as wives;
> they no longer thought of going home,
> and bore sons, and were reconciled.

> The island in the lake became a sweet homeland,
> their tents became fair homes,
> their beds a blessed rest;
> there is nothing now to draw them away.

> They bore sons for a heroic race,
> and lovely daughters, too, for love:
> young branches for a gallant trunk,
> maiden flowers to take their place.

> Of the hero sons, two each,
> of the two leaders' sons, twice two,
> each became the head of a clan:
> into a hundred and eight branches they spread.

> Hunor's branch begot the Huns,
> and Magyar's the Magyar nation;
> they grew beyond all counting,
> and the island could not hold them.

> They flooded out over the Scythian land,
> the rich inheritance of King Dúl;
> and ever since, O pair of heroes,
> your fame flies on from mouth to mouth.
