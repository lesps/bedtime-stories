## Snorri Sturluson, Skáldskaparmál ("The Language of Poetry")

Note: Bragi, the god of poetry, is telling stories to Ægir, the lord of the sea, to explain the names poets give to Thor.

Thor had gone east to fight trolls, and Odin rode Sleipnir into Giantland and came to the giant called Hrungnir. Hrungnir asked who that man in the golden helmet was, who rode through the air and over the sea, and said he had a wonderfully good horse. Odin said he would bet his head that there was no horse as good in all Giantland. Hrungnir said it was a good horse, but that he had one with a much longer stride, called Gullfaxi, Golden Mane. Hrungnir grew angry, leapt onto his horse and galloped after Odin, meaning to pay him back for his boasting. Odin galloped so hard that he kept a whole hill ahead of him, but Hrungnir was in such a giant's rage that before he knew it he had ridden in through the gates of Asgard.

When he came to the door of the hall, the gods invited him in to drink. He went into the hall and asked for a drink. They brought out the bowls that Thor always drank from, and Hrungnir emptied every one. When he grew drunk, there was no shortage of big words. He said he would pick up Valhalla and carry it off to Giantland, and sink Asgard, and kill all the gods, except Freyja and Sif, whom he would take home with him. Freyja alone dared to pour for him, and he said he would drink up all the gods' ale.

When the gods were sick of his boasting, they called on Thor. At once Thor came into the hall with his hammer raised, furious, and asked who had allowed cunning giants to drink there, and who had promised Hrungnir safety in Valhalla, and why Freyja was pouring for him as if at a feast of the gods.

Hrungnir answered, looking at Thor with unfriendly eyes, that Odin had invited him in to drink, and he was under Odin's protection. Thor said that Hrungnir would be sorry for that invitation before he got out.

Hrungnir said there would be little glory for Thor in killing him unarmed. It would be a greater test of courage if Thor dared to fight him at the border, at Grjotunagard. "And it was very foolish of me," he said, "to leave my shield and my whetstone at home. If I had my weapons here, we would fight it out now. But if not, I call you a coward if you kill me unarmed."

Thor would not miss a duel for anything, now that he had been challenged, for no one had ever challenged him before. Then Hrungnir went on his way, galloping hard until he came back to Giantland. His journey became very famous among the giants, and so did the news that a duel had been arranged between him and Thor. The giants felt that a great deal hung on who won, for they could expect nothing good from Thor if Hrungnir fell, since he was the strongest of them.

Then the giants made a man of clay at Grjotunagard. He was nine leagues tall and three leagues broad under the arms. But they could not find a heart big enough for him, until they took one from a mare, and that did not stay steady in him when Thor came.

Hrungnir had a famous heart, made of hard stone and pointed with three corners, like the carved sign that has been called Hrungnir's heart ever since. His head was stone too. His shield was stone as well, broad and thick, and he held the shield in front of him as he stood at Grjotunagard waiting for Thor. For a weapon he had a whetstone, which he held over his shoulder, and he was not a pleasant sight. At his side stood the clay giant, who is called Mokkurkalfi, and he was terrified. It is said that he wet himself when he saw Thor.

Thor went to the duel, and Thialfi with him. Thialfi ran on ahead to where Hrungnir stood and said to him, "You are standing carelessly, giant. You hold your shield in front of you, but Thor has seen you, and he is going down under the earth, and he will come at you from below."

Then Hrungnir pushed the shield under his feet and stood on it, and held the whetstone in both hands. Next he saw lightning and heard great thunder. Then he saw Thor in his godly rage. Thor came on furiously, swung his hammer and threw it from a long way off at Hrungnir. Hrungnir lifted the whetstone in both hands and threw it to meet it. It met the hammer in the air, and the whetstone broke in two. One piece fell to the ground, and from it come all the whetstone rocks there are. The other piece crashed into Thor's head, so that he fell forward to the ground. But the hammer Mjollnir struck Hrungnir in the middle of his head and smashed his skull to little pieces, and he fell forward over Thor, so that his leg lay across Thor's neck. And Thialfi attacked Mokkurkalfi, who fell with little glory.

Then Thialfi went to Thor and tried to lift Hrungnir's leg off him, and could not move it at all. When they heard that Thor had fallen, all the gods came and tried to lift the leg off him, and could not shift it.

Then Magni came, the son of Thor and Jarnsaxa. He was three nights old. He threw Hrungnir's leg off Thor and said, "What a shame, father, that I came so late. I think I would have knocked this giant dead with my fist if I had met him."

Then Thor stood up and gave his son a warm welcome, and said he would grow up to be a great man. "And I will give you the horse Gullfaxi," he said, "that Hrungnir owned."

Then Odin spoke, and said Thor did wrong to give that fine horse to the son of a giantess, and not to his father.

## Skáldskaparmál: Groa the Seeress

Thor went home to Thrudvangar, with the whetstone stuck in his head. Then a seeress came to him called Groa, the wife of Aurvandil the Bold. She sang her spells over Thor until the whetstone began to loosen. When Thor felt that, and thought there was hope that the whetstone would come out, he wanted to reward Groa for her healing and make her happy. He told her the news that he had waded from the north across the icy rivers called Elivagar, carrying Aurvandil out of Giantland in a basket on his back. As proof, he said, one of Aurvandil's toes had stuck out of the basket and frozen, so Thor broke it off and threw it up into the sky and made a star of it, the one called Aurvandil's Toe. Thor said it would not be long before Aurvandil came home. But Groa was so glad that she forgot all her spells, and the whetstone came no looser, and it is still stuck in Thor's head. That is why it is forbidden to throw a whetstone across the floor, for then the whetstone in Thor's head stirs.
