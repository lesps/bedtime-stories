## Homer, Iliad 6.152–155

There is a city, Ephyre, in a corner of Argos where horses graze. There lived Sisyphus, the craftiest of men, Sisyphus son of Aeolus. He had a son, Glaucus, and Glaucus was the father of noble Bellerophon.

## Apollodorus, The Library 1.9.3

Sisyphus, son of Aeolus, founded Ephyra, which is now called Corinth, and married Merope, the daughter of Atlas. Their son was Glaucus, and Glaucus had a son by Eurymede, Bellerophon, who killed the fire-breathing Chimaera.

Sisyphus is punished in Hades by rolling a stone with his hands and his head, trying to push it over the top; but however he pushes it, it is pushed back again. He pays this penalty because of Aegina, the daughter of Asopus. When Zeus carried her off in secret, Sisyphus, it is said, told Asopus, who was searching for her.

## Pausanias, Description of Greece 2.5.1

When you have climbed up to Acrocorinth, the citadel of Corinth, there is a temple of Aphrodite. Its statues are Aphrodite herself, armed, the Sun, and Eros with a bow.

The spring behind the temple, they say, was a gift from Asopus to Sisyphus. Sisyphus knew that it was Zeus who had carried off Aegina, the daughter of Asopus. But when Asopus came searching, Sisyphus would not tell him until he too had water up on Acrocorinth. When Asopus gave it, Sisyphus told him, and for telling, if you believe the story, he pays the penalty in Hades. I have heard people say that this spring is Peirene, and that the water in the city flows down from here underground.

## Pindar, Olympian Odes 1.54–64

Note: The ode is about Pelops, the son of Tantalus. Here the poet tells why Tantalus was punished.

If the watchers on Olympus ever honoured a mortal man, that man was Tantalus. But he could not digest his great good fortune, and in his excess he won himself an overwhelming ruin. The Father hung a mighty stone above him, and always longing to throw it off his head, he wanders, cut off from joy.

He has this helpless life of endless toil, a fourth labour among three others, because he stole from the immortals the nectar and ambrosia with which they had made him deathless, and gave them to the friends he drank with. But if any man hopes to hide what he does from a god, he is wrong.

## Apollodorus, The Library, Epitome 2.1

Tantalus is punished in Hades with a stone hanging over him, ready to fall. He stands forever in a lake, and he sees trees laden with fruit growing beside the lake on either side of his shoulders. The water touches his chin, but whenever he wants to drink, it dries up. Whenever he wants to take some of the fruit, the trees, fruit and all, are lifted by the winds as high as the clouds.

Some say he is punished like this because he blabbed the secrets of the gods to men, and because he shared the gods' ambrosia with his friends.

## Homer, Odyssey 11.582–600

Note: Odysseus, visiting the land of the dead, tells what he saw there.

“And I saw Tantalus suffering cruel torments, standing in a pool, and the water came up to his chin. He stood there thirsting, but he could not get a drink. For every time the old man stooped down, eager to drink, the water was swallowed up and vanished, and the black earth showed around his feet, for some god dried it away. And tall, leafy trees let their fruit hang down over his head, pears and pomegranates and apple trees with shining fruit, sweet figs and flourishing olives. But whenever the old man reached out to take them in his hands, the wind tossed them up toward the shadowy clouds.

“And I saw Sisyphus suffering cruel torments, heaving a monstrous stone with both his arms. Straining with hands and feet, he would push the stone up toward the top of a hill. But just as he was about to heave it over the top, its great weight turned it back, and then down to the plain the shameless stone came rolling again. And again he would strain and push it back up, and the sweat poured from his limbs, and the dust rose from his head.”
